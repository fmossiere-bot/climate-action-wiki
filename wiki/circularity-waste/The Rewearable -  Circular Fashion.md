Go fully circular with your fashion choices (buy only pre-loved) and reduce the harm that comes with expressing yourself through clothes.

### “Fast fashion isn’t free. Someone, somewhere is paying.” – Lucy Siegle.

Clothes matter for comfort and for self-expression. Yet we rarely think about the whole life of a garment, or its environmental and social impact at scale. The fashion industry is one of the biggest polluters on the planet, but there are many ways you can help change it. This guide gives you the knowledge and resources to approach fashion in a way that is sustainable, circular and still stylish.

The Impact of the Fashion Industry Today

Take a T-shirt made from a cotton-polyester blend, the most common material in clothing. The cotton is usually grown with chemical fertilisers and a lot of water. The polyester is made from oil in an energy-hungry process called polymerisation. Both are spun into fibres and woven together into fabric. The fabric is then treated in various ways, including dyeing, which uses highly toxic chemicals. Once sewn into a T-shirt, it is shipped to a shop, bought, worn and washed a limited number of times, and each wash releases microplastic fibres. In the end, it is donated, upcycled, thrown away or burned. At every step, the material is shipped long distances, leftover material is thrown away, and toxic chemicals leak into the air, water and soil.

> [!Here are some statistics about the environmental impacts of fashion:]
> - The fashion industry accounts for around 8-10% of global emissions (UN estimate, 2019) and uses more water than any other industry except one
> - Between 80 and 100 billion new garments are produced around the world every year
> - 87% of the materials and fibres used to make clothing will end up in incinerators or landfill
> - Only 20% of discarded textiles are collected
> - Only 1% of clothes will be recycled into new garments
> - About 35% of the microplastics that enter the ocean come from washing synthetic textiles


The environmental cost is only part of it. The fashion industry also causes a great deal of social harm. Unsafe working conditions are normal for garment workers. Forced labour is not unusual, and nor is child labour. Wages are often unfair and too low to live on. Large fast fashion brands push responsibility for these abuses onto the complicated networks of subcontractors they use. Most brands say they support fair labour practices, but there is little regulation or oversight.

## A Primer on Circular Fashion

Serious efforts are under way to make the fashion industry less harmful, and you can be part of that. Today's economy is mostly **linear**: materials are extracted, used and thrown away. A circular economy copies the cycles of nature, which form a closed loop with no waste. Everything one natural process leaves behind becomes material for another. Fallen leaves, for example, are eaten by worms, which feed the soil so that plants can grow. For fashion, this means producing less waste, using healthy materials, making clothes last longer, and finding new uses for textiles once they can no longer be worn.

Changes in supply chains and in how we shop can close the loop on fashion. Brands are doing this through upcycling, recycling and trials of regenerative and bio-based materials. Governments are changing laws to encourage this. The EU in particular is making real progress on forcing fast fashion brands to take responsibility for the whole life of their products.[4](https://climatemoney.substack.com/p/the-eu-vs-fast-fashion-and-the-end?utm_medium=email) As a shopper, you can also make choices that keep your clothes going for longer, move your money away from harmful supply chains and connect you to the growing circular economy.



## Actions to Make Your Fashion More Circular

For the next four weeks, think about how you buy clothes and look for chances to be more circular. If you do not buy clothes often, give your wardrobe a thorough clear-out and see whether anything can be repaired, upcycled, recycled or donated.

> [!TIPS]
> - Sort through your wardrobe. Knowing what you already have helps you avoid buying too much.
> - Buy vintage clothes whenever you can, and favour local shops to cut down on shipping.
> - Buy less, buy better: choose quality over quantity. You will pay more per garment, but each item will last much longer than a cheap fast fashion one.
> - Care and repair: make your clothes last by treating them well, not over-washing them and repairing them when you can.
> - Upcycling and customising: look for ways to give clothes a new life, even if that means cutting them up for rags.
> - Use alternatives to buying for special occasions: renting, swapping and consignment shops are all good options.
> - Support circular and sustainable brands when you buy new.
> - Choose organic, single-material textiles when you buy new.
