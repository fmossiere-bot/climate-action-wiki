Archaeological evidence suggests people have been composting since Neolithic times, about 12,000 years ago.

Composting is nature's own recycling system, and it does two useful things at once: it cuts emissions and it improves the health of the soil. It turns kitchen scraps, garden waste and other organic material into a rich, soil-like substance called compost. Gardeners sometimes call it "black gold", and it is key to healthy plants.

A compost pile is a tiny ecosystem, and the main workers are microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi. They break down the organic matter, and they need a balance of:

- Browns (carbon source): dry materials like leaves, shredded paper and wood chips.
- Greens (nitrogen source): food scraps, grass clippings, coffee grounds.
- Water: to keep things moist but not soggy.
- Air: turning your pile helps the microbes breathe.

Composting has many advantages:
- It reduces the waste sent to landfill or incinerators, both of which generate greenhouse gas emissions.
- Less landfill waste can mean less air pollution and fewer related breathing illnesses, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma and pneumonia.
- Used in gardens or on farms, compost reduces the need for fertilisers and pesticides, improves soil health and so increases crop yields.
- Compost holds water, prevents erosion, reduces runoff, helps plants establish, and improves water quality downstream by trapping pollutants such as heavy metals, nitrogen, phosphorus, oil, grease, fuels, herbicides and pesticides.

Composting rates are already quite high in many developed regions, such as the EU (57%). 
This is mostly thanks to community composting and better waste separation by industry, companies and individuals. 

### Diverts harmful methane emissions from landfills

When organic waste rots in a landfill, the lack of oxygen means it breaks down through a process called anaerobic decomposition. **This releases methane, a greenhouse gas far more harmful than carbon dioxide over short periods.** Methane is responsible for more than 25% of global warming and is 80 times as harmful as CO2 over a 20 year period.

Composting, by contrast, creates an oxygen-rich environment. The waste breaks down through aerobic decomposition, a different process that mainly releases carbon dioxide instead. CO2 is something we are trying to cut too, but it is far better than releasing methane. By composting, we change how the waste breaks down and cut methane emissions sharply.

### Stores carbon in soil

When finished compost is used as soil to grow plants and vegetables, it **acts as a carbon sink, storing carbon in the soil for the long term**. This keeps that carbon out of the atmosphere and slows the rate of climate change.

### Reduces the Need for Fertilisers

Making chemical fertilisers uses a lot of energy and releases nitrous oxide, a gas over 300x more potent than carbon dioxide. Using compost as a natural alternative reduces the need for these fertilisers, and plants generally do better with natural fertiliser anyway.

### Improves Soil Health and Resilience

Soil enriched with compost holds moisture better, which helps during droughts (and droughts are becoming more frequent with climate change). That means less need for heavy watering, which saves water.

# A real climate solution
Composting ranges from garden bins to industrial-scale sites. Either way, it turns organic waste into carbon in the soil, avoids methane emissions from landfill, and brings several other benefits along the way. 

> Backyard bins and industrial-scale composting turn organic waste into valuable fertilisers, while preventing it from producing methane as it decomposes in a landfill, reducing emissions by 50% 

 **Impact**
> In 2015, an estimated 38 percent of food waste was composted in the US and 57 percent in the EU. If all low-income countries reached the US rate and all middle-income countries reached the EU rate, composting could avoid landfill methane emissions equivalent to 1.13–1.40 gigatons of carbon dioxide by 2050. That total does not include the extra gains from putting compost on soil. Compost facilities cost less to build than to run, which shows in the financial results. For these reasons, composting is a real solution.

**Co-benefits**
> - Composting reduces the waste sent to landfill or incinerators, both of which generate greenhouse gas emissions.
> - Less landfill waste can mean less air pollution and fewer related breathing illnesses, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma and pneumonia.
> - Used in gardens or on farms, compost reduces the need for fertilisers and pesticides, improves soil health and so increases crop yields.
> - Compost holds water, prevents erosion, reduces runoff, helps plants establish, and improves water quality downstream by trapping pollutants such as heavy metals, nitrogen, phosphorus, oil, grease, fuels, herbicides and pesticides.

## Some countries are well ahead

#### South Korea composts close to 100% of its food waste

> In the US, most food waste ends up in landfill, while South Korea recycles close to 100% of it every year. Its model shows some of the core principles at work. 

- Since 2013, Korea has had a mandatory food waste scheme, with households using yellow bags that are then collected. 
- Back in 1996, only 2.6% of food waste was recycled. As the problem kept growing, the government began banning food waste from landfill.

> Ease of use and accessibility have been key to the South Korean model. "**South Korea's waste system, especially in terms of frequency of collection, is incredibly convenient compared to other countries**," says Hong Su-yeol, a waste expert and director of Resource Recycling Consulting.

> **What about Ireland?** 
> We still have room to improve. About 800,000 households in Ireland have a compostable waste bin (usually a brown bin), out of a total of 1.3 million households using kerbside waste collection. 

#### From Kitchen Waste to Farmland: How Lyon Is Closing the Loop on Food Waste

Greater Lyon has built one of France's most ambitious food waste composting programmes. It turns household food waste into compost that local farmers within a 30-kilometre radius use on their land. Since it started in 2021, the scheme has cut incinerated waste by 16% per person and produced over 16,000 tonnes of compost in four years.

**A Political Bet That Paid Off**
Isabelle Petiot, vice-president of the Greater Lyon metropolitan authority in charge of waste reduction, did not wait for the French legal deadline of January 2024 to make food waste sorting compulsory. The programme covers 58 municipalities and 1.4 million people, and it was running years before the law required it.

Not everyone believed in it at first. "People told me I was crazy," Petiot recalls. "They said residents would throw anything in the bins. Mayors were doubtful: they did not want something that would smell."

The pilot began in 2021 in Lyon's 7th arrondissement, a densely populated area of over 80,000 residents. Every household got a free kitchen caddy and paper kraft bags, backed by an active awareness campaign through town halls, markets and local shops. The message was simple and positive: all food waste was welcome, including leftovers, vegetable peelings, coffee grounds, eggshells, chicken bones, bruised fruit, stale bread and out-of-date products. The only firm rule was no plastic.

The response was better than expected. "The first collections were incredible," Petiot says. "People really got behind it."

 **Scale and Results**
The programme spread step by step from the first pilot district to every Lyon arrondissement, then to the inner suburbs, and finally across the whole metropolitan area.

> **Key figures:**
> - Over 1,000 shared composting sites set up across the area
> - 2,700 public compost drop-off points open to residents
> - 33,500 home composters handed out to households
> - **16% less incinerated waste per person between 2021 and 2025**, or 40 kg less waste per resident per year

Between 2021 and 2024, the programme produced **16,700 tonnes of compost**.

**Compost Back to the Land**
Lyon's approach stands out for what happens to the waste after collection, as well as for its scale. In some other French schemes, food waste is partly turned into biogas through anaerobic digestion. In Lyon, all the collected food waste is composted.

Where the compost goes is just as notable. Research by the Rhône Chamber of Agriculture found that it can supply farmland within a 30-kilometre radius of the city. Roughly 80% of it goes straight to farms, and the other 20% is used for urban green spaces and landscaping.

The compost is sold through short supply chains at a token price of 15 euros per tonne and is certified as compatible with organic farming. Local farmers want it. "We have no problem finding buyers," Petiot confirms. "On the contrary, farmers really appreciate it." The metropolitan authority is now looking into official certification for the product.

## Composting in Ireland
Ireland still has some way to go.
It is disappointing that so much food waste is still not separated and recycled: 
 - 64% of food waste still goes in black bins
 - only 66% of households had access to a brown bin for food and organic waste in 2022, according to the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Ireland, in figures published on 16 December 2024. This is a decrease of 3 per cent from 2021.

> Starting with the brown bin, which, if you are disposing of your rubbish correctly, should contain only cooked and raw food, light garden waste and compostable packaging. "The contents of the brown bins go for anerobic digestion or industrial composting and the waste management companies pay for it to be taken," explains Crinion. There are seven private industrial composting facilities in Ireland, licensed by local authorities or the EPA and the Department of Agriculture. The compost they make is sold mainly as a peat replacement for landscaping, farming and horticulture.

- About 800,000 households in Ireland have a compostable waste bin (usually a brown bin), out of a total of 1.3 million households using kerbside waste collection.
- The contents of about 10 per cent of brown bins go to anaerobic digesters in Ireland and the UK, where methane gas is captured and turned into electricity for the grid.
- The leftover material, known as digestate, is spread by farmers on their land as an organic alternative to nitrogen fertilisers, which reduces the greenhouse gases released from that source.

# Getting started with composting at home

Even a tiny patch of outdoor space is enough to start composting. Here is how to get going, and answers to the main questions.

## Finding your Spot and Sizing it Up

You do not need much land to compost. Look for a small corner of your garden, a patio or even a balcony. A 1x1m area is usually enough. To work out the size you need, think about how much food waste your household produces. Do you live alone with few scraps, or are you a family with lots of vegetable peelings? Choose a method that matches the amount.

### Compost Bin Options

- DIY wooden frame: a cheap option that lets you choose the size and materials. It does need some building skills, though, and may not last as long as a bought bin.
- Ready-made compost bin: these come in various sizes, materials (plastic, recycled materials) and designs (ventilation, lids, tumbling mechanisms). They are convenient, often easier to manage, and some look quite nice. Think about durability, capacity and budget when choosing.

### Compost Supplies and Setup 

The cheapest approach is to build a pile directly on the ground or in a simple DIY wooden frame. A pitchfork or aerating tool makes turning the pile easier. You can set it all up in an afternoon.

## Making your compost

### Filling Your Compost

The golden rule is a good mix of "browns" and "greens". Browns include leaves, shredded paper and cardboard. Greens are your vegetable scraps, fruit cores, coffee grounds and so on. In towns and cities it is best to avoid meat, dairy, bones and oily foods: they can slow down decomposition and cause bad smells. Some local authorities also ban them, because they can spread disease or attract pests. 

### Time to Compost

A home compost pile takes several months to a year to turn into usable "black gold". Turning the pile regularly speeds things up.

## Composting tips

- Chop it up: smaller pieces break down faster.
- Moisture matters: compost should feel like a wrung-out sponge.
- Turn it: mixing your pile regularly with a pitchfork keeps it working. Once a week in warm weather, less often in colder months.
- Location matters: put the bin somewhere partly shaded with good air flow. Avoid direct sunlight, which can dry the compost out, and keep it away from the house to avoid unwanted pests.
- Layer it up: alternate browns and greens when adding material.
- Check the temperature: ideally your compost should reach 54-71°C to break down well. If it is too cool, add more browns or turn the pile more often.
- Get the balance right: a good ratio of browns to greens is 3:1. If your compost smells bad, it probably needs more browns to soak up moisture and balance the nitrogen.
- Welcome the worms: they are not essential, but they speed up decomposition, let air into the pile and produce nutrient-rich castings.
- Be resourceful: look for creative ways to collect browns. Shredded paper, cardboard egg boxes and leaves from your garden all work well.

# What to do with your compost

## Enhance your own garden

Once your compost is mature, dark and crumbly, you can dig it straight into your garden beds. Aim for a 5-10 cm layer and mix it gently into the top layer of soil. You can also use it as potting mix for houseplants or outdoor pots by adding 20-30% compost, which provides nutrients and improves drainage.

---
## Share it with others

Many community gardens welcome finished compost to enrich their shared growing space. Contact your local community garden to ask about their policy.

If your council offers kerbside composting or green waste collection, you can probably put your compost out for collection too. Check with your local waste management department to see what is available. If you are not sure who that is, search online for "Food recycling in" followed by the name of your area.

Finally, you can share your "black gold" with gardening friends or neighbours. It builds community spirit and spreads good habits in your local area.
