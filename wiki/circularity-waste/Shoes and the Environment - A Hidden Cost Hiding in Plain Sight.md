We buy shoes without much thought, wear them until they fall apart and throw them away. Yet they are one of the most environmentally complicated things we own. They are made from many materials, put together across several countries, and are almost impossible to recycle at the end of their life.

> The footwear industry produced around **23.9 billion pairs of shoes in 2024.** It accounts for approximately 1.4% of global carbon emissions, about one fifth of the fashion industry's total. Manufacturing alone generates approximately 313 million metric tonnes of CO2 a year, the same as the emissions of 66 million cars. For comparison, global aviation accounts for around 2.5% of emissions. Shoes, a single type of product, are not far behind.

**Around 95% of used footwear ends up in landfill or incineration**. There is no mature, large-scale system for reusing or recycling shoes. Most pairs combine leather, synthetic rubber, polyester, foam, glue and metal eyelets, all bonded together in ways that make them very hard to separate and recycle.

---

### Why Shoes Are So Hard to Make Sustainable

A typical pair of trainers can have 65 or more separate components. They often come from different countries and are assembled in factories where working conditions vary enormously. The environmental problems run through the whole supply chain.

**Leather** is one of the most common materials. It comes from livestock farming, which produces a lot of greenhouse gas emissions and uses large amounts of land and water. Tanning uses chromium and other harsh chemicals that are hard to manage and can badly pollute water.

**Synthetic materials** such as polyester and nylon are made from oil. They take a lot of energy to produce, shed microplastics when worn and washed, and do not biodegrade.

**Rubber** for soles, especially synthetic rubber, also comes from fossil fuels. Natural rubber from tropical forests is a better option, but it is often hard to know exactly where it comes from.

A life cycle assessment by MIT found that a single pair of trainers generated 13.6 kg of greenhouse gas emissions, the same as leaving a 100-watt bulb on for a week. Multiply that by 24 billion pairs and the scale becomes clear.

### Brands Trying to Do It Differently

A small but growing number of brands are rethinking how shoes are designed, sourced and made. Here are some worth knowing about.

**Veja (France)**
URL: https://project.veja-store.com/en
Veja is probably the best-known name in ethical footwear. Two French graduates founded it in 2004 and built it around direct relationships with small family cooperatives in South America. It uses organic cotton from Peru for canvas and laces, natural Amazonian rubber for soles, and leather from Uruguay. Its shoes are made in factories in Brazil and Portugal with high labour standards, and some ranges use recycled plastic bottles and recycled polyester. A social inclusion company handles its distribution in Europe. Veja was one of the first brands to put supply chain transparency at the heart of its identity and back it up with detail, rather than treating it as a marketing line.

**Ubac (France and Portugal)**
URL: https://ubac-store.com/en/
Ubac makes trainers from recycled wool, produced locally in France or Portugal with natural and recycled materials. The sole contains 35% recycled material. The brand runs an end-of-life recycling scheme, so customers can send back old pairs instead of throwing them away. It also supports the LPO, the French league for bird protection, which links the brand to wider biodiversity work.

**Circle Sportswear (France)**
URL: https://en.circlesportswear.com/collections/chaussures-de-running
Circle Sportswear designed its running shoe around circular economy principles from the start. Every component was chosen with the end of the shoe's life in mind. The materials include wood fibre, bio-based compounds and biodegradable parts. The aim is that a worn-out shoe can go back into a material cycle instead of being wasted.

**MOEA (Portugal)**
URL: https://moea.io/en-int
MOEA is a newer brand making vegan shoes entirely in Portugal, which keeps its supply chain short and local. It sells mainly online and relies on social media instead of shops, which also reduces its distribution footprint. Being open about its supply chain is central to how it presents itself.

### The Flip Flop Problem

Flip flops might seem trivial, but the numbers are large. Around 3 billion pairs are produced every year. One in two people on the planet wears them regularly. They are the best-selling footwear in China, India and across Africa. And 10% of all plastic collected on beaches is flip flops.

> **Did you Know?**
> - 1 in 2 people in the world wear flip flops every day
> - 3 billion are produced every year
> - They are the top seller in China, India and Africa

Most are made from cheap EVA foam or PVC, and neither biodegrades. They are usually churned out in a single low-cost factory run, worn for a season and thrown away.

Some alternatives are starting to appear. Smaller brands are using bamboo, natural rubber and recycled materials to make longer-lasting, less damaging versions.

> One example worth highlighting is [**Hodoki**](https://hodokisandals.com/en/hodoki-soul/), a Spanish brand that has rethought the flip flop from scratch. Hodoki sandals are made in one piece. That avoids the usual weak point where the strap pulls away from the sole, and it makes recycling easier. The material includes a compound made from sugar cane, considered more sustainable than conventional fossil-fuel plastics. The brand makes everything in Spain, which avoids shipping between continents and keeps working conditions in line with European rules. The design is inspired by a 13th century Indian sandal, updated with modern materials.

### What to Look For When Buying

The market for sustainable footwear is still small and can be confusing. A few principles help:

- **Fewer materials, better end of life.** Shoes made from fewer bonded parts are generally easier to repair or recycle.
- **Local or regional production** cuts transport emissions and usually means better working conditions.
- **Natural and bio-based materials** tend to do better than synthetics, as long as they are sourced responsibly.
- **Durability matters more than anything.** The most sustainable shoe is the one you wear for ten years instead of two.
- **Brand transparency** is a reasonable sign that a brand is serious. Brands that publish detailed supply chain information are generally more accountable than those that do not.

The industry is changing, slowly. Pressure from shoppers, tighter EU rules on product sustainability, and a new generation of brands that build ethics into the design from the start are all pushing in the right direction.
