## 1. Where the impact comes from

Fashion touches almost every part of the environment, from the field where cotton is grown to the ocean where old clothes end up as microplastics. The main sources of harm are:

- **Growing natural fibres.** Cotton is usually grown with heavy pesticide use, which reduces wildlife in and around farmland.
- **Dyeing.** Toxic dyes are a major source of water pollution in regions that make textiles.
- **Synthetic fibres.** Polyester and other fibres made from fossil fuels are cheap to produce, which feeds fast fashion. Every wash of a synthetic garment releases microplastic fibres into rivers and oceans.
- **Production waste.** Cutting and manufacturing leave large amounts of unused fabric, much of which goes straight to landfill or incineration.

## 2. Key facts and figures

- Fashion accounts for around 8-10% of global emissions (UN estimate, 2019) and uses more water than any other industry except one.
- Between 80 and 100 billion new garments are produced around the world every year.
- 87% of the materials used to make clothing end up incinerated or in landfill.
- Only 20% of discarded textiles are collected for reuse or recycling, and just 1% of clothes are recycled into new garments.
- About 35% of the microplastics entering the ocean come from washing synthetic textiles.
- A single pair of jeans has an average footprint of around 25 kg CO2 equivalent and uses roughly 7,000 litres of water, the same as 285 showers.
- Conventional cow-skin leather has a carbon footprint of about 110 kg per square metre.

## 3. Case study: puffer jackets and overproduction

Puffer jackets show how the industry's problems pile up in a single product. China alone produces an estimated 100 million a year.

- Around 83% of a puffer jacket's carbon and water footprint comes from producing the raw materials, rather than from making or transporting the finished jacket.
- Down filling is mostly a by-product of the meat industry, but 1 to 2% is still believed to come from live plucking. The Responsible Down Standard bans this practice and requires better animal welfare.
- Wool filling can involve mulesing, a practice that harms sheep welfare. It is still legal in Australia but banned in New Zealand. Certifications such as the Responsible Wool Standard or ZQ Merino rule it out.
- Jackets labelled "100% recycled polyester" are usually made from recycled PET bottles or textile waste. This is better than new polyester, but the environmental claim is sometimes overstated.

Research from Adelaide University found that 89% of the clothes people own were bought new. Padded jackets are among the most expensive items people own and among the least worn. In Australia, around 53% of unwanted clothes end up in landfill, 38% are reused or exported, and only 9% are recycled. A repair adds an average of 2.5 years to a garment's useful life, yet only around 1 in 10 items are ever repaired.

## 4. Good players: examples of companies changing the model

Not all of the industry is moving the same way. A few companies show what change can look like in practice.

- **Unspun** has developed 3D weaving technology that makes garments on demand instead of in large batches. Traditional cutting and sewing wastes around 15% of fabric, and Unspun's approach removes most of that step. Its Vega machine can weave the two fabric tubes for a pair of trousers in ten minutes. Unspun has partnered with Walmart on a pilot that aims for 350 Vega machines by 2030.
- **Solena** uses computer-aided protein design to create new biodegradable fibres. The aim is to combine the comfort of natural materials with the toughness of synthetic ones, without using fossil fuels or livestock.
- **Pact** makes a leather alternative called Oval from collagen recovered from fish waste instead of livestock. Its estimated footprint is about 3 kg CO2 equivalent per square metre, against roughly 110 kg for conventional cow-skin leather.
- **1083**, a French jeans maker, makes everything in France to cut transport emissions. It buys cotton from Tanzania, where irrigation relies mostly on natural rainfall, and fades its denim with ozone instead of chemical washes. It has also designed a pair of jeans meant to be recycled indefinitely, backed by a refundable deposit.

None of these examples solves the industry's problems on its own. They do show that alternative materials and ways of making clothes are technically possible today.

## 5. Policy shift: the EU ban on destroying unsold clothes

From 19 July 2026, large fashion companies operating in the EU can no longer destroy unsold clothes, accessories or footwear. The rule is part of the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR).

- It applies to companies with more than 250 employees and over €50 million in annual turnover, and will extend to medium-sized firms from 2030.
- Companies must try to sell surplus stock through discounts, other markets or donations to charity. Destruction is only allowed for items that are unsafe, damaged, counterfeit or turned down by charities.
- Firms must publish annual reports on the goods they discard and keep records for five years.

The European Environment Agency estimated that 4 to 9% of unsold textile products were destroyed each year before the ban, and that one in five items ordered online is returned and never resold. The German Retail Federation welcomed the benefits for shoppers but pointed to practical problems, including damaged packaging and the low resale value of returned items.

Critics point out that the ban deals with what happens to unsold stock, not with how much stock is made in the first place. Companies can still put large quantities of clothing on the EU market. They just cannot burn what is left over. If firms respond by exporting unsold stock outside the EU or storing it indefinitely, the environmental benefit will be smaller than intended.

## 6. Credibility and limitations

Most of the statistics on this page come from industry and NGO sources, not peer-reviewed studies, so figures on emissions, water use and recycling rates vary between reports depending on how they were calculated. The company examples (Unspun, Solena, Pact, 1083) rely on the companies' own claims and on press coverage, and have not been independently checked at scale. The EU ban is new, and its real effect will depend on enforcement and on how companies use the loophole of exporting or storing surplus stock.

## 7. The overall picture

Fashion uses a lot of resources. It is responsible for a significant share of global emissions, water use and microplastic pollution in the ocean, and much of that comes from making too many clothes rather than from how clothes are used. New materials and production methods that could reduce this impact already exist, and rules such as the EU's ban on destroying unsold clothes are starting to tackle the waste. How much is produced in the first place remains the biggest unsolved problem.

There are many ways the fashion industry harms the planet:
- Pesticides used to grow natural fabrics such as cotton, which reduce biodiversity
- Toxic dyes that pollute waterways
- Fossil fuels used to make synthetic fibres, often for cheap clothes that are bought by the armful and worn just a few times. These clothes shed microplastics into rivers and oceans with every wash
- Production processes that create a lot of waste, with unused fabric going straight to scrap

# Go Deeper
## 2026 update: EU bans the destruction of unsold clothes

From 19 July 2026, large fashion companies operating in the EU can **no longer destroy unsold clothes, accessories or footwear**. The ban is part of the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR). It is the first legally binding limit, in a major economic bloc, on one of fast fashion's usual habits: making more clothes than people will buy, then burning what is left.

### What the rule actually says

- Applies to companies with **more than 250 employees and over €50 million in annual net turnover**
- Extended to medium-sized firms in 2030
- Firms must find ways to sell products through **discounts, other markets or donations to charity**
- Destruction is only allowed when items are **unsafe, damaged, counterfeit, or rejected by charities**
- Companies must publish annual reports on discarded goods and **keep records for five years**. Disposal, which used to happen unmeasured in the background, can now be checked

### Why the ban was introduced

- The European Environment Agency estimated that **4% to 9% of unsold textile products are destroyed each year**
- **One in five fashion goods ordered online is returned to the retailer and not resold**
- Only **~20% of clothing sold in the EU is made in the EU**. The ban therefore targets the retail and distribution end, and pushes companies to fix how they buy further up the chain
- The industry has long defended incineration on cost grounds: storing, repairing or discounting often costs more than burning

### Reaction

- The **German Retail Federation (HDE)** welcomed the gains for shoppers (more discounted stock, more second-hand supply) but pointed to practical problems: damaged packaging, high logistics costs and low product values
- The EEA described it as the circular economy "moving from vision into law"
- Critics note that the ban does not stop **overproduction** further up the chain. Companies can still push large quantities of clothes onto the EU market. They just cannot destroy the surplus. If firms respond by dumping stock outside the EU or by storing it indefinitely, the emissions benefit will be limited

## Case study: puffer jackets

The Guardian estimates that China alone makes **~100 million puffer jackets per year**. They bring many of the industry's problems together in one product:

- **~83% of a puffer's carbon and water footprint comes from producing the materials**, according to a 2023 lifecycle study. Shells are usually nylon or polyester. Fillings are down, wool, synthetic fibre or, more and more, **plant-based fluff** (for example Pangaia's wildflower, biopolymer and aerogel filling).
- **Down** is mostly a by-product of the meat industry, but ~1-2% is still thought to come from live plucking. The **Responsible Down Standard** requires better welfare and bans the practice.
- **Wool** carries a particular welfare concern, **mulesing**, which is still legal in Australia but banned in New Zealand and elsewhere. Look for ZQ Merino or the **Responsible Wool Standard**.
- **Polyester puffers labelled "100% recycled"** are usually made from PET bottles or textile waste. That is better than new polyester, but the claim is often overstated (see the [mass-balance greenwashing loophole](plastic-mass-balance-greenwashing.md) for how the accounting works).

### Overproduction is the bigger problem

An **Adelaide University** study of ~600 people found that **89% of garments are bought new**. Padded jackets are among the most expensive items people own and among the least worn, which is why second-hand shops are now full of nearly new puffers in good condition. Across Australia, **~53% of unwanted clothes end up in landfill**, ~38% are reused or exported, and only ~9% are recycled. Textile recycling takes a lot of time and labour, and new polyester is so cheap that, in the words of sustainable fashion consultant Julie Boulton, "it's cheaper just to make a whole new jacket than it is to recover and repurpose one".

### Small changes that make a difference

- **Only ~1 in 10 items are repaired**, but a repair extends a garment's useful life by an average of 2.5 years
- Most clothes do not need washing as often as people think. **Airing them out is often enough**
- **Wash cold**, because heat damages synthetic fabrics. Some water-repellent finishes can be **reapplied** instead of replacing the jacket
- Some outdoor brands offer repair services or take-back schemes (for example Upparel in Australia)

Dr Carolina Quintero Rodriguez of RMIT University sums up the practical advice: *"Extend the life of the item as much as possible. Care for the item as per the care instructions. And make sure that you purchase only what you need."*
