## 1. Where the impact comes from

Fashion touches almost every part of the environment, from the field where cotton is grown to the ocean where old clothes end up as microplastics. The main sources of harm are:

- **Growing natural fibres.** Cotton is usually grown with heavy pesticide use, which reduces biodiversity in and around farmland.
- **Dyeing.** Toxic dyes are a major source of water pollution in textile-producing regions.
- **Synthetic fibres.** Polyester and other fossil-fuel-based fibres are cheap to make, which fuels fast fashion. Every wash of a synthetic garment releases microplastic fibres into rivers and oceans.
- **Production waste.** Cutting and manufacturing processes leave large amounts of unused fabric, much of which goes straight to landfill or incineration.

## 2. Key facts and figures

- Fashion accounts for around 10% of global carbon emissions and is the second-largest consumer of water of any industry.
- Between 80 and 100 billion new garments are produced globally every year.
- 87% of the materials used to make clothing end up incinerated or in landfill.
- Only 20% of discarded textiles are collected for reuse or recycling, and just 1% of clothes are recycled into new garments.
- About 35% of the microplastics entering the ocean come from washing synthetic textiles.
- A single pair of jeans has an average footprint of around 25 kg CO2 equivalent and uses roughly 7,000 litres of water, the equivalent of 285 showers.
- Conventional cow-skin leather has a carbon footprint of about 110 kg per square metre.

## 3. Case study: puffer jackets and overproduction

Puffer jackets are a useful example of how the industry's problems stack up in one product. China alone produces an estimated 100 million units a year.

- Around 83% of a puffer jacket's carbon and water footprint comes from producing the raw materials, not from making or transporting the finished item.
- Down filling is mostly a by-product of the meat industry, but 1 to 2% is still believed to come from live plucking. The Responsible Down Standard bans this practice and requires higher animal welfare.
- Wool filling can involve mulesing, a welfare-damaging practice still legal in Australia but banned in New Zealand. Certifications such as the Responsible Wool Standard or ZQ Merino avoid it.
- Jackets labelled "100% recycled polyester" are usually made from recycled PET bottles or textile waste. This is better than virgin polyester, but the environmental claim is sometimes overstated.

Research from Adelaide University found that 89% of garments people own were bought new, and padded jackets are among the most expensive items people own but wear the least. In Australia, around 53% of unwanted clothes end up in landfill, 38% are reused or exported, and only 9% are recycled. Repair extends a garment's useful life by an average of 2.5 years, yet only around 1 in 10 items are ever repaired.

## 4. Good players: examples of companies changing the model

Not every part of the industry is moving in the same direction. A few companies illustrate what change can look like in practice.

- **Unspun** has developed 3D weaving technology that produces garments on demand rather than in mass batches. Traditional cutting and sewing wastes around 15% of fabric; Unspun's approach removes most of that step. Its Vega machine can weave two tubes of fabric for trousers in ten minutes. Unspun has partnered with Walmart on a pilot aiming for 350 Vega machines by 2030.
- **Solena** uses computational protein design to create new biodegradable fibres, aiming to combine the comfort of natural materials with the durability of synthetic ones, without relying on fossil fuels or livestock.
- **Pact**, through its Oval material, makes a leather alternative from collagen recovered from fish waste rather than from livestock. Its estimated footprint is about 3 kg CO2 equivalent per square metre, compared with roughly 110 kg for conventional cow-skin leather.
- **1083**, a French jeans maker, manufactures entirely in France to cut transport emissions, sources cotton from Tanzania where irrigation relies mostly on natural rainfall, and uses an ozone-based process to fade denim rather than chemical washing. The company also designed a jean intended to be recycled indefinitely, backed by a refundable deposit system.

These examples do not solve the industry's problems on their own, but they show that alternative materials and production models are technically possible today.

## 5. Policy shift: the EU ban on destroying unsold clothes

From 19 July 2026, large fashion companies operating in the EU can no longer destroy unsold clothes, accessories or footwear. The rule is part of the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR).

- It applies to companies with more than 250 employees and over €50 million in annual turnover, extending to medium-sized firms from 2030.
- Companies must try to sell surplus stock through discounts, alternative markets or charity donations. Destruction is only allowed for unsafe, damaged, counterfeit or unwanted-by-charity items.
- Firms must publish annual reports on discarded goods and keep records for five years.

The European Environment Agency estimated that 4 to 9% of unsold textile products were destroyed each year before the ban, and one in five items ordered online is returned and never resold. The German Retail Federation welcomed the consumer-side benefits but flagged practical issues, including damaged packaging and low resale values on returned items.

Critics point out that the ban addresses what happens to unsold stock, not the volume of stock produced in the first place. Companies can still bring large quantities of clothing onto the EU market; they simply cannot burn the surplus. If firms respond by exporting unsold stock outside the EU or by storing it indefinitely, the environmental benefit will be smaller than intended.

## 6. Credibility and limitations

Most of the statistics in this article come from industry and NGO sources rather than peer-reviewed studies, so figures on emissions, water use and recycling rates vary between reports depending on methodology. Company-level examples (Unspun, Solena, Pact, 1083) are based on the companies' own claims and press coverage, and have not been independently verified at scale. The EU ban is recent legislation, and its real-world effect will depend on enforcement and on how companies respond to the loophole around exporting or storing surplus stock.

## 7. Bottom line

Fashion is a resource-intensive industry, responsible for a significant share of global emissions, water use and ocean microplastic pollution, much of it driven by overproduction rather than by how clothes are used. New materials and production methods already exist that could reduce this impact, and regulation such as the EU's ban on destroying unsold clothes is starting to address the waste side of the problem. The production volume itself remains the largest unresolved issue.

There are many ways the Fashion industry is hurting the planet:
- Pesticides used to grow the natural fabrics like Cotton - reducing biodiversity
- Toxic Dyes pollute the waterways
- Fossil Fuel used to make synthetic fibres, often used to make cheap clothes, bought by armful and worn just a few times. These kind of clothes leaches micrplastics into the rivers and oceans with each wash
- Production Processes resiult in a lot of waste, with unused fabrics heading straight to scrap

## 2026 update: EU bans the destruction of unsold clothes

From **19 July 2026**, large fashion companies operating in the EU can **no longer destroy unsold clothes, accessories or footwear**. The ban is a provision of the **Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR)**, and it is the first legally binding cap in a major economic bloc on one of fast fashion's core operating patterns: **producing more clothes than the market can absorb, then incinerating the overhang**.

### What the rule actually says

- Applies to companies with **more than 250 employees and over €50 million in annual net turnover**
- Extended to **medium-sized firms in 2030**
- Firms must find ways to sell products via **discounts, alternative markets or charity donations**
- Destruction is only allowed when items are **unsafe, damaged, counterfeit, or rejected by charities**
- Companies must **publish annual reports on discarded goods** and **keep records for five years** — turning disposal from an unmeasured background practice into an auditable one

### Why the ban was necessary

- The **European Environment Agency** estimated that **4% to 9% of unsold textile products are destroyed each year**
- **One in five fashion goods ordered online is returned to the retailer and not resold**
- Only **~20% of apparel sold in the EU is produced in the EU** — so the ban targets the retail and distribution end, and puts pressure on companies to fix procurement upstream
- The industry has historically defended incineration on cost grounds: storing, repairing or discounting often costs more than burning

### Reaction

- The **German Retail Federation (HDE)** welcomed the consumer-side gains (more discounted stock, more second-hand supply) but flagged practical challenges: damaged packaging, high logistics costs, low product values
- The **EEA** framed it as circular economy "moving from vision into law"
- Critics note the ban does not stop **overproduction** upstream — companies can still push large quantities of clothes into the EU market, they just cannot destroy the surplus. If firms respond by dumping stock outside the EU or by warehousing indefinitely, the emissions benefit will be limited

## Case study: puffer jackets

Puffer jackets — a category the Guardian estimates at **~100 million units per year made in China alone** — condense many of the industry's problems into one product:

- **~83% of a puffer's carbon and water footprint comes from producing the materials**, according to a 2023 lifecycle study. Shells are usually nylon or polyester; fillers are down, wool, synthetic, or increasingly **plant-based fluff** (e.g. Pangaia's wildflower/biopolymer/aerogel).
- **Down** is mostly a byproduct of the meat industry, but ~1-2% is still thought to come from **live plucking**; the **Responsible Down Standard** requires higher welfare and prohibits it.
- **Wool** carries the specific welfare concern of **mulesing** — a practice still legal in Australia but banned in New Zealand and elsewhere; look for **ZQ Merino** or the **Responsible Wool Standard**.
- **Polyester puffers labelled "100% recycled"** are usually made from PET bottles or textile waste — a better story than virgin polyester, but often overstated (see the [mass-balance greenwashing loophole](plastic-mass-balance-greenwashing.md) for how the accounting works).

### Overproduction, not use

An **Adelaide University** study of ~600 people found **89% of garments are bought new**, and padded jackets are one of the **most expensive items people own but rarely wear** — which is why the secondhand market is now full of near-new puffers in good condition. Nationally in Australia, **~53% of unwanted clothes end up in landfill**, ~38% are reused or exported, and only ~9% are recycled. Textile recycling is time- and labour-intensive, and because virgin polyester is so cheap, "**it's cheaper just to make a whole new jacket than it is to recover and repurpose one**" (Julie Boulton, sustainable fashion consultant).

### Small behaviour-change lever with real impact

- **Only ~1 in 10 items are repaired**, but a repair extends the useful life of a garment by an **average of 2.5 years**
- Most garments do not need to be washed as often as people think; **airing them out is often enough**
- **Wash cold** — heat damages synthetic fabrics; some water-repellent finishes can be **reapplied** rather than replacing the jacket
- Some outdoor brands offer **repair services or take-back schemes** (e.g. Upparel in Australia)

The practical takeaway from RMIT's Dr Carolina Quintero Rodriguez: *"Extend the life of the item as much as possible. Care for the item as per the care instructions. And make sure that you purchase only what you need."*
