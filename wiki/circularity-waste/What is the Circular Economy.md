We use more than the planet can regenerate. Each year, Earth Overshoot Day marks the date when humanity has used up more natural resources than the Earth can renew in twelve months. In 2024, that date fell on 1 August. For the rest of the year, we are living on borrowed resources.

> The circular economy is one of the most serious attempts to change the system behind this. It has moved well beyond a niche idea. It is increasingly central to EU climate and industrial policy, and it is starting to reshape regulation, business models and consumer rights across Europe.

---

### What Is the Circular Economy?

Today's economy is mostly linear: take raw materials, make a product, use it, throw it away. The circular economy works differently. In the European Commission's definition, a circular economy keeps the value of products, materials and resources for as long as possible, so that as little as possible is wasted.

In practice, this means designing products to last longer and to be repaired or reused, or so that their parts can be recovered and fed back into production. It means asking, at the design stage, what will happen to a product at the end of its life, instead of leaving that question to someone else.

The three core principles are often summed up as:

- **Eliminate waste and pollution** at the design stage, before it is created
- **Keep products and materials in use** for as long as possible
- **Regenerate natural systems** instead of depleting them

---

### What Does It Look Like in Practice?

The circular economy takes different forms in different sectors.

**Repair and reuse** is probably the most visible starting point for most people. Instead of replacing a broken appliance, a circular model helps you get it fixed. Sharing platforms and tool libraries, sometimes called Libraries of Things, are growing fast across Europe. A 2025 European market study found that the number of sharing stations rose by 70% in a single year, with over 1,300 stations now serving more than 350,000 users.

**Extended producer responsibility** moves the cost of dealing with waste from public budgets to the companies that make the products. Manufacturers pay for what happens to their products at the end of their life, which gives them a direct reason to design them better from the start.

**Industrial symbiosis** is less visible but just as important. In some industrial areas, the waste or by-products of one company become the raw material for another. The Kalundborg Symbiosis in Denmark is one of the oldest and best-documented examples: a network of companies exchange steam, water, ash and other materials that would otherwise be wasted.

**Textile and fashion** is one of the most pressing areas. The EU produces **12.6 million tonnes of textile waste per year**, and clothing and footwear make up 41% of that. Fast fashion has made this worse. Circular approaches here include rental and resale platforms, schemes to take back old clothes, and design standards that make clothes easier to repair or recycle.

### Is It Part of the EU Climate Plan?

Yes, and it plays a big part. The **European Green Deal**, launched in 2019, makes the circular economy a central pillar of the EU's plan to reach climate neutrality by 2050. The reasoning is simple: approximately half of global greenhouse gas emissions come from extracting and processing raw materials. Keeping materials in use for longer means less extraction, and so fewer emissions.

The EU has published two Circular Economy Action Plans. The first, in 2015, set out 54 specific actions. The second, in 2020, set more ambitious targets, above all doubling the EU's circularity rate by 2030. The current target, confirmed and slightly raised under the Clean Industrial Deal, is **to reach 24% by 2030, up from 12.2% today**.

There is a big gap between that goal and reality.

> The EU's circular material use rate has risen since 2010, from 10.7% to 12.2% in 2024. But progress is considered slow, and the EU is not currently on track to meet its 2030 target. To reach it, the circularity rate would have to grow by more than 1.7 percentage points a year. That is more than the total increase over the whole period from 2010 to 2024.

Member states differ a lot. In 2024, the Netherlands led with a circularity rate of 32.7%, followed by Belgium at 22.7% and Italy at 21.6%, all already meeting or close to the 2030 target. At the other end, Romania recorded just 1.3%, while Finland and Ireland each stood at 2%. The gap shows that the target can be reached, but only by countries that have made it a real priority.

Money is a lasting problem. Recent estimates put the yearly investment needed for the EU's move to a circular economy at **55 billion euros**. The European Investment Bank lent 3.8 billion euros to circularity projects over 2019 to 2023. That is moving in the right direction, but it is still far short of what is needed.

Looking ahead, the European Commission opened a public consultation in August 2025 on a new Circular Economy Act, expected to be adopted in 2026. The Act aims to create a single market for secondary raw materials (materials recovered from waste), increase the supply of good-quality recycled materials, and boost demand for them across EU industries.

### New Policies and Laws

Overall progress is slow, but the law is changing. Several important measures have been introduced or are being prepared.

**Right to Repair Directive (2024)** The European Parliament passed new rules requiring sellers to repair products instead of replacing them, unless repair costs more. The law also gives consumers clearer rights to get repairs after a product's guarantee has run out. This matters because electronics are the fastest-growing source of waste in the EU. More than 3.5 million tonnes of electronic waste were collected in 2017, and only 40% was recycled.

**Textile and Food Waste Rules (provisional, 2025)** EU lawmakers have reached a provisional agreement on new rules requiring real cuts in both textile and food waste. Around 59 million tonnes of food waste are produced in the EU each year, a direct economic loss estimated at **132 billion euros**. Under the new framework, food manufacturers will need to cut waste by 10% by 2030, while retailers, restaurants and households will have to cut food waste by 30% per person. Fashion brands will pay fees linked to how circular their products are, so companies that design more durable and recyclable products pay less. The provisional agreement still needs formal approval from the European Council and Parliament before it becomes law.

**Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation** This broader regulation, agreed in 2024, extends ecodesign rules beyond energy-related products to most goods sold in the EU. It sets requirements for durability, repairability, recyclability and recycled content. The aim is to make unsustainable products the exception, not the norm.

### Companies and Initiatives Worth Watching

**Renewlondon / Repair Cafes** Community repair projects have grown a lot across Europe. Repair Cafes, which started in the Netherlands, now run in over 2,500 locations across the continent, where volunteers help people repair household items for free.

**Backmarket** The French platform for refurbished electronics has become one of Europe's best-known circular economy successes. It now operates in over 16 countries and is valued at several billion euros, which shows that circular models can grow into large businesses.

**Patagonia** Patagonia is based in the US, but it runs a well-regarded Worn Wear programme in Europe that buys back, repairs and resells used clothing. It has helped push other outdoor and sportswear brands towards take-back schemes.

**Libraries of Things** Networks of shared tool and equipment libraries are growing across the UK, Ireland, Belgium and the Netherlands. They let people borrow things they only need now and then instead of buying them. In Dublin, the Share Library runs from several locations and is a practical local example of the sharing economy.

**WEEE Ireland** Ireland's largest non-profit for collecting e-waste has kept more than 250 million electrical items out of landfill since 2005, collecting over 609,000 tonnes of electrical waste in twenty years. It has also raised a fair concern: EU recycling targets are based on weight, so they do not properly reward repair and reuse, or reflect the longer lifespans of modern appliances. This is a gap in the current rules that needs fixing.

---

### Where Things Stand

In 2024, 78% of Europeans said environmental problems directly affect their daily lives, and around 60% were worried about growing amounts of waste, a share that has nearly doubled over the past 15 years. Public awareness is there. The gap is in turning that concern into changes to the whole system, at the speed and scale the targets need. The companies and projects that make circularity work usually have one thing in common: they have found a way to make it pay as well as help the environment. Closing that gap across all 27 member states is the main task for the next decade.
