# The 'Recycled' Plastic That Isn't: Mass-Balance Accounting and the Pyrolysis Loophole

## What is it

In almost any European supermarket you will find brands labelling their plastic packaging as "recycled", "circular" or "climate-friendly". In January 2026, a **cross-border investigation led by The Guardian's Stefano Valentino** (with Voxeurop, Mediapart, Altreconomia, Público, Deutsche Welle and others under the IJ4EU banner) looked behind those labels. For many of the biggest brands, including Kraft's Heinz Beanz and Mondelēz's Philadelphia, it found the claims rest on accounting rules rather than on what the plastic is made of. The plastic in your hand is still almost entirely made from oil.

Two techniques make this possible: mass-balance bookkeeping and "avoided emissions". Both are legal today, both have strong backing from the petrochemical industry, and both are set to be written further into law, by the EU's PPWR packaging rules coming into effect in 2026 and by the UK plastic packaging tax rules taking effect in 2027.

## Why it matters

Compulsory targets for recycled content are one of Europe's main tools for tackling plastic pollution and cutting demand for new oil. If a company can meet those targets on paper without putting any recycled material into its products, the policy stops doing what it was designed to do. The IEA's *World Energy Outlook 2025* now expects plastic to become **a critical growth engine for oil majors' future profits** as demand for oil as a fuel falls. So the loophole matters well beyond packaging: it is one of the ways the fossil fuel industry plans to stay large.

## How the pyrolysis pipeline actually works

Most "chemical recycling" running at scale uses **pyrolysis**: heating plastic waste without oxygen to produce a hazardous liquid called pyrolysis oil. There is a hard technical limit. Pyrolysis oil can make up at most 5% of what goes into the next stage, and the other 95% has to be virgin naphtha (a product made from crude oil). Otherwise the steam-cracking plants further down the line get damaged.

Helmut Maurer, a former senior expert in the European Commission's environment department, puts it plainly: *"The whole process is labelled as plastic recycling, while fossil fuel use expands because virgin feedstock must be added."*

## Trick one: mass-balance bookkeeping

Mass balance is an accounting rule that lets a company assign its recycled input to particular batches of what it produces. Picture 100 tonnes of plastic made from 5 tonnes of pyrolysis oil mixed into 95 tonnes of naphtha. Under mass-balance rules, the producer can pick any 5 tonnes of the finished plastic and certify it as **"100% recycled"**, even though those 5 tonnes may physically contain only fossil material.

Lauriane Veillard, policy officer at the NGO Zero Waste Europe, says: *"This is unfair to consumers: recycled content should be physically part of the final product."*

The certificates that make this legal come from the industry-led International Sustainability and Carbon Certification (ISCC) scheme, and they are passed down the supply chain to consumer brands.

## Trick two: avoided emissions

<!-- tag-start: company-evaluations -->
The carbon side of the story relies on **life-cycle assessments (LCAs)**, studies that add up a product's emissions from start to finish. Sabic's own LCA admits that the whole pyrolysis-to-cracking process emits 6 to 8% more than making plastic from fossil fuel in the first place. The numbers only turn positive when the accounting gives credit for the emissions that would have been released if the same amount of waste had been burned instead. These are the "avoided emissions".

That one accounting step turns a slightly worse process into a claim of "about 2 kg less CO₂ per kg of recycled plastic". As Maurer says: *"What matters is not hypothetical emissions from incineration that are 'avoided' on paper, but what is actually emitted in reality."*

Sabic's LCA says it had a "rigorous critical review" by experts, including the co-founder of Plastic Energy. Plastic Energy is Sabic's main supplier of pyrolysis oil, a business relationship that raises obvious questions about impartiality. Sabic and Plastic Energy declined to share their full LCAs with the investigation. The named brands did not respond.
<!-- tag-end -->

Peter Quicker, professor of emission control at Aachen University, goes further: *"LCA documents serve no purpose other than advertising, because companies control the parameters to achieve desired results."*

## Who benefits

<!-- tag-start: company-evaluations -->
Ownership tells part of the story. The plastics maker behind many of these products is **Sabic, a subsidiary of Saudi Aramco**. Aramco is the world's largest corporate greenhouse gas emitter, at over 70 billion tonnes CO₂e from 1854 up to 2023 (Carbon Majors database), and one of the loudest opponents of production cuts under the UN plastic treaty. Public records suggest Sabic used 2,600 tonnes of pyrolysis oil in 2022 against roughly 4 million tonnes of virgin naphtha at its cracking plants in the Netherlands. That puts the recycled share at well under 1%, too small to matter.
<!-- tag-end -->

Behind the scenes, petrochemical companies have stepped up their lobbying of EU institutions to make sure the new rules allow mass-balance accounting, and they have rushed to sign supply deals with pyrolysis oil producers. The timing fits. As the new rules arrive, the industry already has ways to comply that need **no change in what actually goes into the plastic**.

## Key facts

- Pyrolysis oil = **at most 5% of feedstock**; 95% must be virgin naphtha
- Sabic's own LCA: the pyrolysis-to-cracking process emits 6–8% more than fossil plastic; only the "avoided incineration" credit turns the number positive
- Sabic 2022: **2,600 tonnes of pyrolysis oil** vs ~4 million tonnes of naphtha at its Dutch plants
- EU PPWR rules that allow mass balance take effect in **2026**; UK plastic packaging tax mass-balance rules in **2027**
- Certificates are issued by the industry-led ISCC
- **Saudi Aramco** (Sabic's parent) is the world's largest corporate greenhouse gas emitter and opposes production cuts under the UN plastic treaty
- IEA *World Energy Outlook 2025*: plastic set to become a critical growth engine for oil majors' future profits
- Named consumer brands: **Kraft Heinz Beanz, Mondelēz Philadelphia**, which did not respond to the investigation

## October 2026 update: Coca-Cola, Nestlé and Danone agree to change the labels

> Following the January 2026 Guardian/IJ4EU investigation and a November 2023 BEUC complaint, the European Commission announced on **25 October 2026 (press release IP/26/2238)** that Coca-Cola, Nestlé Waters and Danone have committed to change the recycling claims on their plastic bottle labels:

> - Claims like *"I am a bottle made from 100% recycled plastic"* will be replaced with *"This bottle is 100% recycled. Excluding cap and label"*
> - Claims like *"Recycle me again"* become *"Recycle me"*, removing the false impression that the bottle was recycled before and will become another bottle
> - The overall impression of the label and marketing must not mislead about environmental benefits
> - Digital marketing on websites and social media must follow the same commitments

The talks were led by the **Swedish Consumer Agency** and the Hungarian Competition Authority under the Consumer Protection Cooperation (CPC) network. Coca-Cola and Nestlé have already put some of the commitments in place, particularly in their digital campaigns.

> **What this changes.** The new "excluding cap and label" wording makes clear what the "100%" claim covers (the cap and label are usually not recycled). "Recycle me" instead of "Recycle me again" is more accurate about the one-way nature of most plastic bottle recycling. But these are fixes to labels. The mass-balance accounting that lets 95%-virgin-naphtha pyrolysis plastic count as "recycled" is untouched, so the problem raised by the Guardian/IJ4EU investigation and by Helmut Maurer, Lauriane Veillard and Peter Quicker remains. See [EU recycling claims commitments (source)](../sources/eu-recycling-claims-commitments-ec-2026.md).

## What good looks like

Two benchmarks for comparison:

- **Physical recycled content**: the recycled material is actually and traceably in the final product. One example is **[Seaqual ocean plastic yarn](seaqual-ocean-plastic-fashion.md)**, made from 10% upcycled marine plastic + 90% land-based rPET (recycled PET), with an LCA showing a 37% reduction in GWP (global warming potential, its climate impact) and third-party certification
- **Real chemical recyclability**: bio-based materials such as the new [wood-based epoxy/polyester resins](../sectors/industry/bio-based-resins-composites.md) from the University of Oulu, which are designed to be chemically broken back down into their original raw materials
