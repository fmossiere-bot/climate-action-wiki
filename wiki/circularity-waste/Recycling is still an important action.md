> Recycled products take fewer resources and less energy to make, and they avoid the emissions from landfill. Ways to boost recycling include making companies responsible for their waste and building markets for recycled materials.

> [!IMPACT]
> Recycled products produce 40% less emissions on average.
>
> Household and commercial recycling covers metals, plastic, glass and other materials such as rubber, textiles and e-waste (old electronics). Project Drawdown treats paper products and organic waste as separate solutions.
>
> The emissions savings come from two places: avoiding the emissions from landfill, and using recycled materials instead of new raw materials. About 50 percent of recycled materials come from households and the rest from industry. If the average worldwide recycling rate for these materials rose to 62–82 percent of all recyclable household and municipal waste, recycling could avoid **10.36–11.29 gigatons of carbon dioxide equivalent** emissions by 2050.

Recovering waste materials and recycling them into new products means less has to be made from scratch. That produces fewer greenhouse gas emissions and puts less pressure on landfill.

Household waste is usually managed by city governments, or in lower-income cities by informal waste collectors. Ways to increase recycling from homes and industry include:

- charging fees for landfill waste but not for recycling
- deposits paid at purchase and refunded on return
- extended producer responsibility (making producers pay for dealing with their products as waste)
- stronger markets for recycled materials
- new technologies for turning waste into usable materials
- circular business models.

For now, collecting, transporting and processing recycling still runs mostly on fossil fuels. Even so, recycling remains an effective way to manage waste and cut emissions. It also reduces the need to extract new resources, limits other pollution and creates jobs.
