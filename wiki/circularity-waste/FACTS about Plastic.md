Plastic production has grown from 2 million tonnes in 1950 to almost 350 million tonnes in 2017, and it is expected to double again by 2040. Alongside this growth, plastic waste has become one of the most visible and measurable environmental problems of our time, affecting oceans, soil, air quality and human health. This article sets out the scale of the problem, where the waste actually goes, the health and climate implications, the state of international negotiations to address it, and a number of companies working on practical solutions.

## 1. The scale of the problem

Plastic production has become a global industry worth over US$500 billion. A few figures give a sense of how large the problem has become:

- About 400 million tonnes of plastic waste are generated every year.
- Half of all plastic produced is designed for single use.
- One million plastic bottles are purchased every minute worldwide.
- Up to five trillion plastic bags are used worldwide every year.
- Only around 9% of plastic ever produced has been recycled.
- Roughly half of the plastic waste entering the oceans comes from just five countries: China, Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and Vietnam, all of which are experiencing rapid economic growth and rising plastic use.

Since the 1950s, around 10 billion tonnes of plastic have been produced in total, and more than 8 billion tonnes of that has become waste.

## 2. Where does the plastic actually go?

A widely cited 2017 study from the University of California traced the fate of every tonne of plastic ever made. It remains the most complete picture available:

- About 55% has gone to landfill or been discarded directly into the environment.
- Around 8% has been incinerated.
- Only about 6% has been recycled (more recent estimates put current annual recycling closer to 14%, with wide variation between countries).

An estimated 10 to 12 million tonnes of plastic enter the ocean every year, equivalent to around 700 billion plastic bottles. A 2019 survey of the Pacific Ocean found that microplastic particles are more common below the surface than at it, with the highest concentrations found at depths of around 300 metres.

## 3. Health and climate impacts

Plastic pollution is no longer only an environmental issue confined to oceans and landfill. It has become part of the human food chain, and its production carries a significant climate cost.

**Human health.** A 2024 review across 38 countries linked common plastic-related chemicals, including BPA and DEHP, to hundreds of thousands of deaths and millions of cases of heart disease worldwide. These chemicals are found in food packaging, household goods and electronics. Some countries, including the United States, Canada and South Korea, monitor chemical levels in blood and urine samples, which has strengthened the evidence base. Regulation introduced around 2000 has reduced exposure in the US and Europe, but related deaths were still being recorded as late as 2015, an indication of how persistent these substances are in the body.

**Climate.** According to a 2021 report by Bennington College and Beyond Plastics, the plastic industry releases at least 232 million tonnes of greenhouse gases each year across its full lifecycle, from drilling for the oil and gas used in production through to incineration of waste. This is roughly equivalent to the emissions of 116 coal-fired power stations. The UN Environment Programme (UNEP) has estimated that, on current trends, emissions linked to plastic could account for 15% of the world's allowable carbon budget by 2050 under a 1.5°C pathway. The same 2021 report found that petrochemical facilities are disproportionately located near low-income and minority communities, where the large majority of associated pollution occurs.

**Marine life.** More than 800 marine and coastal species are affected by plastic pollution through ingestion, entanglement or habitat disruption.

## 4. Is recycling actually working?

The honest answer is: only partially, and unevenly.

Globally, only around 14% of plastic waste is currently recycled. Collection remains a major bottleneck, with only about 27% of plastic waste even reaching a sorting facility. Consumer packaging is the single largest source of plastic waste, making up 44% of global use. Some countries have turned to incineration rather than recycling, with around 34% of plastic waste burned in 2022.

Recycling rates vary considerably by country and by material. Polyethylene terephthalate (PET, used in most drinks bottles) makes up about 7% of plastic waste, and up to 90% of it can be recycled mechanically. However, recycled PET is rarely turned back into bottles. It is usually downcycled into products such as carpets, upholstery or insulation, meaning the material eventually still ends up as waste. France recycles around 72% of packaging, avoiding an estimated 2.2 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent, which shows what is achievable with the right infrastructure. Ireland, by contrast, has recently introduced additional waste levies to try to reach a 55% separation rate, suggesting there is still considerable room for improvement.

A newer approach known as advanced recycling, most commonly through pyrolysis (heating plastic to around 500°C without oxygen) or gasification, can in principle convert mixed and contaminated plastic waste into new raw material rather than downcycling it. Europe currently leads this field, with over 100 advanced recycling facilities in operation or development and processing capacity expected to double by 2026. These processes are energy-intensive, though broadly comparable to the energy needed to produce virgin plastic.

## 5. The UNEP roadmap: an 80% reduction by 2040

A 2023 UNEP report concluded that plastic pollution could be reduced by 80% by 2040 if governments and companies pursue "deep policy and market shifts using existing technologies". The proposed roadmap has five elements:

1. **Reduce** unnecessary plastic use at source.
2. **Reuse** plastic through refill and deposit return systems, potentially reducing pollution by around 30%.
3. **Recycle** more effectively, potentially reducing pollution by around 20%.
4. **Reform** the sector by removing fossil fuel subsidies and enforcing design standards that improve recyclability, potentially reducing pollution by 21 to 50%.
5. **Reorient** production towards alternative materials where genuinely justified, potentially reducing pollution by around 17%.

UNEP executive director Inger Andersen has been clear that recycling alone will not solve the problem: "We will not recycle our way out of the plastic pollution crisis: we need a systemic transformation to achieve the transition to a circular economy."

## 6. The global plastics treaty: progress under pressure

Since 2022, UN member states have been negotiating a legally binding international treaty covering the full lifecycle of plastic, from design and production through to disposal. The treaty was originally due to be finalised in 2024, but talks in Busan in December 2024 failed to reach agreement, and a further round took place in Geneva in August 2025.

Reporting by the Guardian and other outlets has documented a significant increase in industry lobbying at these talks. More than 220 corporate lobbyists attended the Busan round, more than the host nation's own delegation and around three times the number of independent scientists present. Over 30 plastic and chemical industry associations have joined the process since 2023. There is currently no official scientific advisory panel for the treaty; an independent group, the Scientists' Coalition for an Effective Plastics Treaty, has attempted to fill this gap but has reported being given limited access to negotiating sessions.

A separate investigation published by the Guardian in January 2026 examined how some companies use a method called mass-balance accounting to market plastic packaging as recycled, even where it contains only a small proportion of recovered material. The investigation raised concerns that upcoming EU regulation could risk formalising this practice rather than closing the loophole, a point worth watching for anyone assessing corporate sustainability claims in this sector.

## 7. Companies and projects working on solutions

Alongside policy efforts, a number of companies are developing practical approaches to reducing plastic waste or replacing it altogether.

**Notpla** (UK) produces biodegradable packaging made from seaweed and other marine plants, intended as a direct alternative to conventional single-use plastic. The company won an Earthshot Prize for this work.

**Novoloop** (US) has developed a process that converts hard-to-recycle plastic waste, including material collected from the ocean, into performance materials that are molecularly equivalent to conventional plastic. The company reports emissions savings of 70 to 90% compared with conventional plastic production, and its material has been used in 3D printing and, more recently, in running shoe components.

**The Ocean Cleanup**, founded by Boyan Slat, focuses on removing plastic already in the ocean and intercepting it before it arrives via rivers. An estimated 80% of ocean plastic originates from around 1,000 rivers, which is why the organisation has developed river interceptor technology alongside its ocean cleanup systems. To date it has removed more than 600 tonnes of plastic, with a stated goal of removing 90% of floating ocean plastic by 2040.

**Trifol Resources** (Ireland) has patented a process that converts waste plastic into sustainable fuels, including sustainable aviation fuel, using reactors installed at a former Bord na Móna site in Littleton, Co. Tipperary. The company states it can independently certify the destruction of the plastic input and its conversion into fuel without subsidy.

**Monomeris** (France) has developed a green chemistry process, without the use of pyrolysis, that breaks plastic down into monomers intended to be fully reusable as raw material.

**Enval** (UK) is one of the established players in advanced recycling in Europe, using pyrolysis-based technology to process mixed plastic waste.

Researchers in China have also developed a hard bioplastic made from bamboo cellulose that matches the strength of conventional plastic used in car interiors and appliances, while remaining recyclable and biodegradable in soil. Bioplastics of this kind currently make up only around 0.5% of global plastic production, so the potential for growth is considerable.

## 8. Credibility and limitations

The figures in this article draw on a mix of peer-reviewed research, UN and NGO reporting, and investigative journalism, and some caution is warranted. Estimates of ocean plastic volumes and the proportion of waste from any given country vary between sources depending on methodology. Company claims, particularly around emissions savings and recycling rates, are often self-reported and have not always been independently verified. The treaty negotiations remain unresolved at the time of writing, and the influence of industry lobbying on the eventual outcome is difficult to quantify precisely, though the scale of lobbyist attendance is well documented. Readers assessing individual corporate sustainability claims in this sector should treat unverified figures with some scepticism, particularly given the documented use of mass-balance accounting to overstate recycled content.

## Bottom line

Plastic pollution is a large, well-documented and still-growing problem, with meaningful impacts on oceans, human health and the climate. Recycling alone has clearly not solved it: current global recycling rates remain low, and much of what is recycled is downcycled rather than genuinely reused. The UNEP roadmap suggests an 80% reduction is achievable by 2040, but only through a combination of reduction, reuse, recycling reform and material substitution together, not through any single measure. Progress on the international treaty has been slower than hoped, partly due to the scale of industry involvement in the negotiations. At the same time, a growing number of companies, from ocean cleanup technology to genuinely circular recycling processes to plant-based alternatives, are demonstrating that practical solutions exist and are being deployed at increasing scale.
