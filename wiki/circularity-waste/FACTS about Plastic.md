## Key facts 

> - **1 million plastic bottles** are bought every minute
> - up to **five trillion plastic bags** are used worldwide every year. 
> - In total, **half of all plastic produced is designed for single use**, according to UNEP.
> - About 400 million tonnes of plastic waste are produced every year.
> - Only **9%** of plastic produced is recycled
> - About half of all the plastic waste that ends up in the oceans comes from just five countries: China, Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and Vietnam. These economies are growing fast, and as they grow, consumption booms, and so does their use of plastic goods.
> - Older estimates put single-use plastic bag use at 500 billion a year; UNEP's more recent figure is up to five trillion


Plastic production has grown from 2 million tonnes in 1950 to almost 350 million tonnes in 2017 and around 460 million tonnes in 2019 (OECD), and it is expected to double again by 2040. As production has grown, plastic waste has become one of the most visible and measurable environmental problems of our time, affecting oceans, soil, air quality and human health. This page covers the scale of the problem, where the waste actually goes, the effects on health and climate, where international negotiations stand, and some of the companies working on practical solutions.

## 1. The scale of the problem

Plastic is now a global industry worth over US$500 billion. A few figures show how big the problem has become:

- About 400 million tonnes of plastic waste are produced every year.
- Half of all plastic produced is designed to be used once.
- One million plastic bottles are bought every minute worldwide.
- Up to five trillion plastic bags are used worldwide every year.
- Only around 9% of all plastic ever produced has been recycled.
- Roughly half of the plastic waste entering the oceans comes from just five countries: China, Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and Vietnam. All five have fast-growing economies and rising plastic use.

Since the 1950s, around 10 billion tonnes of plastic have been produced in total, and more than 8 billion tonnes of it has become waste.

## 2. Where does the plastic actually go?

A widely cited 2017 study from the University of California traced what happened to every tonne of plastic ever made. It is still the most complete picture available. Of all the plastic waste generated up to 2015:

- About 79% has gone to landfill or been dumped straight into the environment.
- Around 12% has been burned.
- Only about 9% has been recycled (estimates of today's yearly recycling range from around 9% to 14%, with wide differences between countries).

An estimated 10 to 12 million tonnes of plastic enter the ocean every year, about the same as 700 billion plastic bottles. A 2019 survey of the Pacific Ocean found that microplastic particles are more common below the surface than at it, with the highest concentrations at depths of around 300 metres.

## 3. Health and climate impacts

Plastic pollution is no longer only a problem for oceans and landfill. It has entered the human food chain, and making plastic carries a large climate cost.

**Human health.** A 2024 review covering 38 countries linked common plastic-related chemicals, including BPA and DEHP, to hundreds of thousands of deaths and millions of cases of heart disease worldwide. These chemicals are found in food packaging, household goods and electronics. Some countries, including the United States, Canada and South Korea, measure chemical levels in blood and urine samples, which has strengthened the evidence. Rules brought in around 2000 have reduced exposure in the US and Europe, but related deaths were still being recorded as late as 2015, which shows how long these substances stay in the body.

**Climate.** According to a 2021 report by Bennington College and Beyond Plastics, the plastic industry releases at least 232 million tonnes of greenhouse gases a year across its whole lifecycle, from drilling for the oil and gas used to make it to burning the waste. That is roughly the same as the emissions of 116 coal-fired power stations. The UN Environment Programme (UNEP) has estimated that, on current trends, emissions linked to plastic could use up 15% of the world's remaining carbon budget by 2050 on a 1.5°C pathway. The same 2021 report found that petrochemical plants are disproportionately sited near low-income and minority communities, which bear most of the resulting pollution.

**Marine life.** More than 800 marine and coastal species are affected by plastic pollution, through eating it, getting tangled in it, or losing habitat.

## 4. Is recycling actually working?

Partly, and unevenly.

Worldwide, only around 9% to 14% of plastic waste is currently recycled, depending on the estimate. Collection is a major bottleneck: only about 27% of plastic waste even reaches a sorting facility. Consumer packaging is the largest single source of plastic waste, making up 44% of global use. Some countries have turned to burning rather than recycling, and around 34% of plastic waste was burned in 2022.

Recycling rates differ a lot by country and by material. Polyethylene terephthalate (PET, used in most drinks bottles) makes up about 7% of plastic waste, and up to 90% of it can be recycled mechanically. But recycled PET is rarely turned back into bottles. It is usually downcycled into lower-grade products such as carpets, upholstery or insulation, so the material still ends up as waste in the end. France recycles around 72% of its packaging, avoiding an estimated 2.2 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent, which shows what the right infrastructure can achieve. Ireland, by contrast, has recently brought in extra waste levies to try to reach a 55% separation rate, so there is still plenty of room to improve.

A newer approach, known as advanced recycling, can in principle turn mixed and dirty plastic waste into new raw material instead of downcycling it. It mostly works through pyrolysis (heating plastic to around 500°C without oxygen) or gasification. Europe leads the field, with over 100 advanced recycling plants running or being developed, and processing capacity expected to double by 2026. These processes use a lot of energy, though roughly as much as making new plastic from scratch.

## 5. The UNEP roadmap: an 80% reduction by 2040

A 2023 UNEP report concluded that plastic pollution could be cut by 80% by 2040 if governments and companies make "deep policy and market shifts using existing technologies". The roadmap has five parts:

1. **Reduce** unnecessary plastic use at source.
2. **Reuse** plastic through refill and deposit return schemes, which could cut pollution by around 30%.
3. **Recycle** more effectively, which could cut pollution by around 20%.
4. **Reform** the sector by removing fossil fuel subsidies and enforcing design standards that make plastic easier to recycle, which could cut pollution by 21 to 50%.
5. **Reorient** production towards alternative materials where that makes sense, which could cut pollution by around 17%.

UNEP executive director Inger Andersen has been clear that recycling alone will not solve the problem: "We will not recycle our way out of the plastic pollution crisis: we need a systemic transformation to achieve the transition to a circular economy."

## 6. The global plastics treaty: progress under pressure

Since 2022, UN member states have been negotiating a legally binding international treaty covering the whole life of plastic, from design and production to disposal. It was meant to be finalised in 2024, but talks in Busan in December 2024 ended without agreement, and a further round took place in Geneva in August 2025.

The Guardian and other outlets have reported a large increase in industry lobbying at these talks. More than 220 corporate lobbyists attended the Busan round. That was more than the host country's own delegation and around three times the number of independent scientists there. Over 30 plastic and chemical industry associations have joined the process since 2023. The treaty has no official scientific advisory panel. An independent group, the Scientists' Coalition for an Effective Plastics Treaty, has tried to fill the gap but says it has been given limited access to negotiating sessions.

A separate Guardian investigation, published in January 2026, looked at how some companies use a method called mass-balance accounting to sell plastic packaging as recycled, even when it contains only a small share of recovered material. It raised concerns that upcoming EU rules could make this practice official rather than closing the loophole. That is worth keeping in mind when judging companies' sustainability claims in this sector.

## 7. Companies and projects working on solutions

Alongside policy, a number of companies are working on practical ways to cut plastic waste or replace plastic altogether.

**Notpla** (UK) makes biodegradable packaging from seaweed and other marine plants, as a direct replacement for ordinary single-use plastic. The company won an Earthshot Prize for this work.

**Novoloop** (US) has developed a process that turns hard-to-recycle plastic waste, including plastic collected from the ocean, into high-performance materials that are molecularly the same as conventional plastic. The company reports emissions savings of 70 to 90% compared with making conventional plastic. Its material has been used in 3D printing and, more recently, in parts for running shoes.

**The Ocean Cleanup**, founded by Boyan Slat, removes plastic that is already in the ocean and catches it in rivers before it gets there. An estimated 80% of ocean plastic comes from around 1,000 rivers, which is why the organisation has built river interceptors as well as its ocean cleanup systems. So far it has removed more than 600 tonnes of plastic, and its stated goal is to remove 90% of floating ocean plastic by 2040.

**Trifol Resources** (Ireland) has patented a process that turns waste plastic into sustainable fuels, including sustainable aviation fuel, using reactors at a former Bord na Móna site in Littleton, Co. Tipperary. The company says it can have the destruction of the plastic and its conversion into fuel independently certified, without subsidy.

**Monomeris** (France) has developed a green chemistry process, without pyrolysis, that breaks plastic down into monomers (its basic building blocks) intended to be fully reusable as raw material.

**Enval** (UK) is one of the established names in advanced recycling in Europe, using pyrolysis-based technology to process mixed plastic waste.

Researchers in China have also made a hard bioplastic from bamboo cellulose that is as strong as the conventional plastic used in car interiors and appliances, while staying recyclable and biodegradable in soil. Bioplastics like this make up only around 0.5% of global plastic production today, so there is a lot of room for growth.

## 8. Credibility and limitations

The figures on this page come from a mix of peer-reviewed research, UN and NGO reports, and investigative journalism, so some caution is needed. Estimates of how much plastic is in the ocean, and how much comes from any one country, vary between sources depending on the method used. Company claims, especially about emissions savings and recycling rates, are often self-reported and have not always been checked independently. The treaty negotiations were still unresolved at the time of writing, and it is hard to measure exactly how much industry lobbying will affect the outcome, though the number of lobbyists attending is well documented. When judging individual companies' sustainability claims in this sector, treat unverified figures with some scepticism, especially given the documented use of mass-balance accounting to overstate recycled content.

## Summary

Plastic pollution is a large, well-documented and still-growing problem, with real effects on oceans, human health and the climate. Recycling alone has clearly not solved it. Global recycling rates are still low, and much of what is recycled is downcycled rather than truly reused. The UNEP roadmap suggests an 80% reduction is possible by 2040, but only by combining less use, reuse, better recycling and substitute materials, with no single measure enough on its own. The international treaty has moved more slowly than hoped, partly because of how heavily industry is involved in the talks. Meanwhile, a growing number of companies, working on ocean cleanup technology, circular recycling processes and plant-based alternatives, show that practical solutions exist and are being used on a growing scale.
