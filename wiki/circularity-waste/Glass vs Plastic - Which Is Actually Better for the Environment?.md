Most people assume glass is the better choice. The data is more mixed than that, but it still points the same way, as long as a few conditions are met.

### The Short Answer

Glass does better on health and recyclability. Plastic does better on the energy needed to make it and on transport. Plastic's recycling record is so poor in practice, though, that **glass remains the better overall choice, provided you reuse it**.

---

### Glass: The Strengths and the Catch

Glass is made from sand, soda ash and limestone, all common natural materials. It is chemically inert, which means it does not leach anything into food or drink, even when heated or frozen. It can also be recycled again and again without losing quality, which few other packaging materials can match.

> The catch is energy. Glass has to be melted at very high temperatures, so making it takes much more energy than making plastic. It is also much heavier, which raises transport emissions. Because of that energy and fuel, a single-use glass bottle can sometimes have a bigger overall environmental impact than a plastic one.

The important word there is single-use. When a glass container is reused many times, the cost of making it is spread across all those uses, and the balance tips clearly in its favour. Some European countries recycle over 90% of their glass, which shows what is possible when the collection and processing systems are in place.

---

### Plastic: Convenient, but the Numbers Are Poor

Plastic is light, cheap to produce and takes less energy to make than glass. If you only compare production, it looks competitive. The full picture is much harder to defend.

> **Only 9% of all plastic ever produced has been recycled**. The rest has gone to landfill, to incineration or into the environment. The Great Pacific Garbage Patch is one visible result. Microplastic contamination is another, harder to see and possibly more worrying. A recent study found an average of 240,000 plastic particles in a single litre of bottled water. Microplastics have now been found in human breast milk, blood and organs. Scientists do not yet fully understand the long-term health effects, but the research so far is not reassuring.

Plastic also depends on fossil fuels, both as the raw material and as the energy used to make it. Many plastics that carry a recycling symbol are not recyclable in practice, because of what they are made of, because they are contaminated, or because there is no local facility to process them.

On health, the worry is about chemical additives such as BPA and phthalates, which can leach into food, especially when plastic is heated or scratched. BPA-free alternatives exist, but they often swap one questionable chemical for another.

---

### What the Numbers Tell Us

A single pair of trainers generates 13.6 kg of CO2. A single litre of bottled water contains up to 240,000 plastic particles. And 91% of all plastic ever made has never been recycled: it has been landfilled, burned or left in the environment. These are not unusual cases. They show how the system works today, at scale.

A comparison on production energy helps. A standard pair of shoes made from conventional materials has a carbon footprint of 10 to 14 kg CO2 equivalent. Alternatives made with recycled materials can bring that down to around 2.2 kg. Packaging works the same way: the material matters, and so does what happens to it after use.

---

### What to Do in Practice

Neither material is perfect. A simple way to think about it:

- **Choose glass** when you will reuse it, when you are heating or freezing food, and when there is glass recycling where you live. Glass is inert, recyclable and better for health.
- **Avoid single-use glass**, which can be worse than plastic on emissions alone because of its weight.
- **Avoid plastic where you can**, especially for hot food and drink, scratched or old containers, and anything for young children. If you cannot avoid plastic, choose reusable containers and never heat food in them.
- **Use less in the first place.** The most sustainable container is the one you already own and keep using.

The bigger question sits behind the glass-or-plastic choice: whether everything needs to come in a single-use container at all.
