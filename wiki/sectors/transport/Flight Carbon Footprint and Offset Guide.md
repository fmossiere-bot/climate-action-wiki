# Flight Carbon Footprint and Offset Guide

## What is it

In principle, working out the CO2 from a flight is fairly simple, because it depends directly on how much jet fuel is burnt. Working out how much of that belongs to one passenger is harder, and depends on several things. This guide explains how footprint calculators work, why different tools can give very different answers for the same flight, and what buying a carbon offset really achieves.

Other related reading: [Carbon Offsets: How They Work and Why They Often Fall Short](../../concepts/key-ideas/Carbon%20Offsets.md)

## Why it matters

Two people can fly exactly the same route and be given very different carbon footprints, depending on which calculator they use and what it counts. Knowing why helps travellers look at offset claims with a critical eye, rather than trusting a green tick at face value.

## How a flight's carbon footprint is calculated

Five factors make the biggest difference.

1. Flight distance. The longer the flight, the more fuel is burnt overall. But take-off and landing use a large share of the fuel on short routes, so short flights are less efficient per kilometre. Calculators usually take the great circle distance (the shortest path between two airports) and add an allowance for real-world routing, weather diversions and air traffic delays.
2. Aircraft and engine type. Fuel use varies a lot between aircraft models and engines.
3. Cargo versus passengers. Most of a flight's weight is the aircraft and its fuel. The rest, the "payload", is people and cargo. Passenger aircraft, especially wide-bodies on long-haul routes, often carry a lot of freight and mail as well as passengers. Emissions are usually split by each group's share of the payload weight: if passengers are 80% of the payload weight and cargo 20%, then 20% of the flight's emissions go to cargo and are taken out before the rest is divided among passengers. Industry practice assumes 100 kg per passenger including luggage, plus another 50 kg per passenger for shared items such as toilets, galleys and crew. The passenger share of payload varies a lot by route: over 96% on flights within Europe, but only 64 to 80% on flights between Europe and Asia ([ICAO, 2017](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CarbonOffset/Documents/Methodology%20ICAO%20Carbon%20Calculator_v10-2017.pdf)).
4. Seat occupancy rate, also called the passenger load factor. Longer flights tend to use larger, more efficient aircraft, but the emissions per passenger depend on how full the plane is. The same total emissions shared among fewer people means a bigger footprint each. Planes are fullest on routes from Central America to Europe (83%) and emptiest between Africa/Middle East and South America (60%) ([ICAO, 2017](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CarbonOffset/Documents/Methodology%20ICAO%20Carbon%20Calculator_v10-2017.pdf)).
5. Seat class. Business and first-class seats take up more cabin space, so fewer passengers fit on each plane. These seats are given a larger share of the aircraft's weight and emissions. A multiplier of around 1.5 is usually used for business class, and 2 to 3 for first class.

## Typical fuel consumption by distance

Average jet fuel use per passenger, according to [BDL (2015)](https://www.bdl.aero/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/energieeffizienz_klimaschutz_2015_de.pdf):

- 2 to 6.8 litres per 100 passenger-km on short flights (under 800 km)
- 4.2 to 6 litres per 100 passenger-km on medium flights (800 to 3,000 km)
- 3.5 to 9 litres per 100 passenger-km on long flights (over 3,000 km)

## Why calculators disagree: a real comparison

Several popular calculators were tested on the same direct flight from Dublin to Geneva. Their results varied by up to 2x:

- Google Flights: 230 kg CO2e
- ICAO Environment calculator: around 230 kg
- MyClimate: around 500 kg, roughly double the figures above
- CarbonFootprint.com: around 320 kg, with the option to include or leave out radiative forcing from contrails

Google and ICAO are the most open about their methods. Google is also clear that estimating the real climate impact of contrails is still an unsolved problem.

## Contrails: the piece most calculators leave out

Most carbon calculators do not count contrails. These are the feathery ice clouds that form when hot jet exhaust meets cold, humid air high up. Most contrails disappear quickly, but a small share last and spread, trapping heat that would otherwise escape to space.

According to [Google's Travel Impact Model documentation](https://support.google.com/travel/answer/11116147), once contrails are included, the warming impact of flying can be up to 60% higher than estimates based on fuel burn alone ([Lee et al., 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1352231020305689)). Roughly 10% of flights cause most of the lasting, warming contrails. But scientists cannot yet predict exactly which flights will form them, or measure the impact of each one, which is why most calculators leave contrails out altogether. Separate research in the [2022 IPCC report](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGIII_Chapter10.pdf) estimated that cloud cover caused by contrails makes up roughly 35% of aviation's total warming impact, more than half as much as the warming from burning the jet fuel itself. Another study, from 2019, on the radiative forcing of contrail cirrus (the heat-trapping effect of these thin clouds) projected that this effect will grow considerably as air traffic increases.

The science on contrails has also moved on recently. A newer analysis, using AI to study 700,000 contrails over Europe and North Africa during a week in late January 2023 and the same week in 2024, was reported by [New Scientist](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2449212-jet-contrails-may-cool-the-planet-by-day-and-warm-it-by-night/). It found the picture is more complicated than "contrails always warm". Contrails reflect incoming sunlight, which cools, and trap outgoing heat, which warms. Some 62% of the contrails observed formed at night, when only the warming effect applies. Most daytime contrails, though, had a net cooling effect, because the two effects partly cancel out. The average contrail lasts 2.5 to 3.5 hours, but some last 14 hours or more, so a contrail formed late in the day can carry on into the night and add warming. This suggests day flights may have a smaller climate impact than night flights on the same route, although that does nothing to cancel the flight's CO2 emissions. The more promising fix being researched is to route aircraft around the very humid air where lasting contrails form, though this can add around 30 minutes to a long-haul flight. For the AI-driven trial now testing this at scale, see [Operation Blue Skies](FACTS%20about%20Air%20Travel.md#contrails).

## Carbon offsetting for the aviation industry

People thinking of buying an offset sometimes assume airlines already offset their emissions because the law requires it. That is only partly true, and coverage is patchy. Within the EU, the Emissions Trading System (ETS) covers flights, but international flights are excluded. The Kyoto Protocol asked wealthier countries to limit international aviation emissions through the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO). In 2008, with little progress at ICAO, the EU added both domestic and international flights to and from the EU to its ETS. The international aviation industry objected, and in 2012 the EU exempted international flights to give ICAO time to build its own scheme. That scheme, the [Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation (CORSIA)](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/pages/climate-change.aspx), was adopted by ICAO in 2016. Both the EU ETS and CORSIA are steps in the right direction, but together they still do very little, especially outside the EU.

In response, many airlines now let passengers buy a carbon offset when they book. Almost all companies and organisations with net-zero pledges buy offsets or take part in offset projects in some way, yet the whole sector remains highly controversial. In September 2022, the British budget airline EasyJet said it would stop using most carbon credits to reach its mid-century net-zero goal, [citing its own net zero roadmap](https://corporate.easyjet.com/files/doc_downloads/easyjet-nz-roadmap.pdf).

Offset prices vary enormously, from a few euros on some budget airlines to much more on airlines that price offsets more realistically. One widely cited example: a passenger flying roughly 34,000 km from London to Tahiti paid an extra £27 to offset 1.7 tonnes of CO2, when their actual footprint for the journey was closer to 6 tonnes. At a more realistic carbon price of around $80 a tonne, which many economists think is still too low, 1.7 tonnes should cost far more than £27. Airline offset schemes are voluntary, and they are often unclear about how much of the money goes to permanent, additional emissions cuts, rather than to projects that would have happened anyway. As [New Scientist has argued](https://www.newscientist.com), the offset market is a drop in the ocean compared with what is needed, and it risks drawing attention away from the real solution: cutting emissions from aviation itself.

## Key facts

- The same Dublin to Geneva flight got footprint estimates from 230 kg to 500 kg CO2e, depending on the calculator used.
- Contrails can push the real warming impact of flying up to 60% above estimates based only on fuel burn (Lee et al., 2021, via Google).
- Cloud cover caused by contrails is estimated to account for around 35% of aviation's total warming, more than half as much as the CO2 from the fuel itself (IPCC AR6).
- Around 10% of flights cause most of the lasting, warming contrails.
- A study published in 2024, of 700,000 contrails observed in late January 2023 and late January 2024, found most daytime contrails have a net cooling effect, while contrails that form at night (62% of those studied) warm.
- Rerouting to avoid air where contrails form can add roughly 30 minutes to a long-haul flight.
- One documented case: an offset of 1.7 tonnes of CO2 cost £27, against an actual footprint of around 6 tonnes for the trip.
