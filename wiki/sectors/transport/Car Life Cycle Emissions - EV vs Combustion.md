# Car Life Cycle Emissions: EV vs Combustion

## What is it

Transport relies on fossil fuels more than any other part of the economy. In 2021 it accounted for 37% of all CO2 emissions from end-use sectors, according to the [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/topics/transport). **Electric vehicles (EVs) cut greenhouse gas emissions over their whole life in almost all circumstances**, and the [IPCC](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGIII_FullReport.pdf) sees them as the key technology for cutting emissions from road transport.

Not owning a car at all is even better for the climate, but many people cannot go car-free because of where they live and what other options they have. That is why the IPCC describes EVs as "likely crucial" for tackling transport emissions. Sales are growing fast: one in every seven cars sold worldwide in 2022 was electric, up from one in 70 just five years earlier. Even so, parts of the mainstream media in several large economies, including the UK, still report on EVs in a hostile way.

To compare vehicles fairly, researchers use a standard unit, tonnes of CO2 equivalent (tCO2e). It accounts for the different warming effects of the various greenhouse gases across a vehicle's whole life: manufacturing, use and end of life.

## Why it matters

Most people assume a car's environmental impact comes mainly from the exhaust pipe. The full picture has more to it. EVs cause more emissions when they are made but far fewer when they are driven. Getting the comparison right matters for anyone choosing what to drive, and for governments deciding what to subsidise.

## Manufacturing vs use: where EV emissions actually come from

Take a medium-sized battery electric vehicle (BEV), a hybrid and a combustion (ICE) car, each used for a typical 16 years and 240,000 km. The BEV has the lowest lifetime emissions of the three. The details are worth knowing:

- **Making a BEV produces around 40% more emissions** than making a hybrid or ICE vehicle, according to a [McKinsey & Company study](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/automotive-and-assembly/our-insights/the-race-to-decarbonize-electric-vehicle-batteries). This comes from mining and refining battery raw materials such as lithium, cobalt and nickel, and from the energy-hungry manufacturing process itself.
- **Producing the electricity is by far the most emissions-heavy stage of a BEV's life cycle.** Cleaning up the grid with renewables and nuclear power would sharply cut these emissions from driving.
- Recycling materials and parts at the end of a car's life can make up for some of the emissions caused earlier on.

## Bigger is not better: SUVs, batteries and pickup trucks

Size matters with EVs too. An electric SUV can end up causing more carbon over its life than a small petrol car, because bigger batteries need more raw materials to be mined. As a rough guide, above 60 kWh of battery capacity the resource cost starts to outweigh the climate benefit. So a small EV beats a large one on lifetime emissions, and in some cases a small petrol car can beat a large EV.

The same goes for electric pickup trucks. As the [New York Times reported](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2023/02/17/climate/electric-vehicle-emissions-truck-suv.html), heavier electric pickups often need bigger batteries and more electricity to charge. Per mile, once all lifetime emissions are counted, they can end up as polluting as some smaller petrol cars.

## Should I keep my old car or buy an EV?

The maths still favours switching to an EV, but smaller is still better. Despite claims to the contrary, EVs do not put more carbon into the air than combustion vehicles over their lifetime. Even counting everything that goes into making them, EVs start to do net good for the climate after about a single year of use. They stay cleaner than the petrol or diesel equivalent even when charged from a grid that is still mostly fossil-powered, according to [Yale Climate Connections](https://yaleclimateconnections.org/2022/08/electrifying-transportation-reduces-emissions-and-saves-massive-amounts-of-energy/). The downside is that EVs tend to be heavier, which raises separate concerns about wear on roads and about road safety.

## The charging infrastructure gap

Most countries still lack enough EV charging points, and this is a serious constraint. Building them quickly enough is a real challenge, and it is also creating openings for sustainable transport start-ups in markets from India to elsewhere around the world.

EV sales alone do not quickly bring down a country's emissions. In Norway, 65% of all new vehicles sold in 2021 were electric, yet oil demand had fallen by less than 10% since 2013. New cars are only a small share of all the cars already on the road, so replacing the fleet takes years, even where people are buying EVs quickly.

## Key facts

- Transport accounted for 37% of CO2 emissions from end-use sectors globally in 2021 (IEA).
- Making a BEV produces about 40% more emissions than making a hybrid or ICE vehicle, mainly from mining battery materials (McKinsey).
- EVs usually make up for their bigger manufacturing footprint within about a year of driving, even on a grid that is not green.
- Electric SUVs and EVs with large batteries (above roughly 60 kWh) can lose much of their lifetime advantage over small petrol cars.
- One in seven cars sold globally in 2022 was electric, up from one in 70 five years earlier.
- In Norway, 65% of 2021 car sales were electric, yet oil demand fell by less than 10% since 2013, which shows how slowly replacing the fleet turns into lower emissions.
