# Car Life Cycle Emissions: EV vs Combustion

## What is it

Transport is the sector most reliant on fossil fuels in the entire economy: in 2021 it accounted for 37% of all CO2 emissions from end-use sectors, according to the [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/topics/transport). **Electric vehicles (EVs) significantly cut lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions in almost all circumstances** and are considered the key technology for decarbonising road transport by the [IPCC](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGIII_FullReport.pdf).

Not owning a car at all has even bigger climate benefits, but many people's ability to go car-free is limited by where they live and what alternatives are available. That is why the IPCC describes EVs as "likely crucial" for tackling transport emissions. Sales are growing fast: EVs accounted for one in every seven cars sold globally in 2022, up from one in 70 just five years earlier. Even so, EVs remain the subject of hostile reporting in parts of the mainstream media in several major economies, including the UK.

To compare the climate impact of different vehicles fairly, researchers use a standardised unit, tonnes of CO2 equivalent (tCO2e), which accounts for the different warming effects of various greenhouse gases over a full lifecycle: manufacturing, use and end of life.

## Why it matters

Most people assume a car's environmental impact comes mainly from the tailpipe. In reality, the picture is more nuanced: EVs have higher manufacturing emissions but far lower running emissions, so getting the comparison right matters for anyone deciding what to drive, and for policymakers deciding what to subsidise.

## Manufacturing vs use: where EV emissions actually come from

Comparing a medium-sized battery electric vehicle (BEV), hybrid and combustion (ICE) car over a typical use phase of 16 years and 240,000 km, BEVs come out with the lowest lifecycle emissions of the three. But the detail is instructive:

- **Production emissions for BEVs are around 40% higher** than for hybrid or ICE vehicles, according to a [McKinsey & Company study](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/automotive-and-assembly/our-insights/the-race-to-decarbonize-electric-vehicle-batteries). This comes from extracting and refining battery raw materials such as lithium, cobalt and nickel, plus the energy-intensive manufacturing process itself.
- **Electricity production is by far the most emissions-intensive stage of a BEV's life cycle.** Decarbonising the grid through renewables and nuclear power would sharply cut these use-phase emissions.
- Recycling materials and components at end of life can offset some of the emissions generated earlier in a vehicle's life.

## Bigger is not better: SUVs, batteries and pickup trucks

Even with EVs, size matters. An electric SUV can end up more carbon-intensive over its lifetime than a small petrol car, because larger batteries require proportionally more raw material extraction. As a rough guide, above 60 kWh of battery capacity the resource cost starts to outweigh the climate benefit. The takeaway: a small EV beats a large one on lifecycle emissions, and in some cases a small EV beats a large petrol car too.

The same logic applies to electric pickup trucks. As the [New York Times reported](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2023/02/17/climate/electric-vehicle-emissions-truck-suv.html), heavier electric pickups often need bigger batteries and more electricity to charge, so per mile, taking full lifecycle emissions into account, they can end up just as polluting as some smaller petrol cars.

## Should I keep my old car or buy an EV?

The maths still favours switching to an EV, but the smaller-is-better rule applies here too. Despite claims to the contrary, EVs do not put more carbon into the air than combustion vehicles over their lifetime. Even accounting for the full manufacturing footprint, EVs start delivering a net climate benefit after about a single year of use, and they remain cleaner than the fossil-fuelled equivalent even when charged from a still mostly non-green grid, according to [Yale Climate Connections](https://yaleclimateconnections.org/2022/08/electrifying-transportation-reduces-emissions-and-saves-massive-amounts-of-energy/). The trade-off is that EVs tend to be heavier, which raises separate concerns about infrastructure wear and road safety.

## The charging infrastructure gap

A lack of EV charging infrastructure remains a critical constraint in most countries, and building it out fast enough is a genuine challenge, one that is also creating opportunities for sustainable transport start-ups in markets from India to elsewhere globally.

Crucially, EV sales alone do not translate quickly into lower national emissions. In Norway, 65% of all new vehicles sold in 2021 were electric, yet oil demand had fallen less than 10% since 2013. New vehicle sales are only a small fraction of the total cars already on the road, so fleet turnover takes years even where EV uptake is fast.

## Key facts

- Transport accounted for 37% of CO2 emissions from end-use sectors globally in 2021 (IEA).
- BEV production emissions run about 40% higher than hybrid or ICE vehicles, mainly from battery material extraction (McKinsey).
- EVs typically pay back their higher manufacturing footprint within about a year of driving, even on a non-green grid.
- Electric SUVs and large-battery EVs (above roughly 60 kWh) can lose much of their lifecycle advantage over small petrol cars.
- One in seven cars sold globally in 2022 was electric, up from one in 70 five years earlier.
- In Norway, 65% of 2021 car sales were electric, yet oil demand fell less than 10% since 2013, showing how slowly fleet turnover translates into emissions cuts.
