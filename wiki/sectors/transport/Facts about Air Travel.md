## 2026 Update: The Industry Admits the 2050 Target Is Slipping

In June 2026, IATA director general Willie Walsh publicly admitted at the annual IATA summit in Rio de Janeiro that aviation's landmark net zero 2050 pledge will "probably not now be achieved." He called for a "realistic timeline" and blamed three failures: fuel suppliers not delivering on SAF commitments, governments undermining CORSIA through inaction, and aircraft manufacturers delaying next-generation efficient planes.

The numbers tell the story. **SAF production in 2026 will reach only 2.4 million tonnes, just 0.8% of airline fuel needs.** The 2050 target requires 500 million tonnes (65% of fuel supply). Walsh was blunt: "There is no path to meet that outcome" for the 5% reduction by 2030. IATA's own sustainability vice-president called EU and UK e-SAF targets for 2030 "utterly detached from reality."

Environmental campaigners, who have long argued that aviation's net zero pledges were greenwashing and a cover for continued expansion, are unsurprised. The admission puts pressure on the UK government, which tied further Heathrow expansion to climate tests being met.

Source: [The Guardian, Gwyn Topham, 8 June 2026](../../sources/iata-netzero-unlikely-2026.md)

# Facts about Air Travel

## What is it

In October 2022, nations agreed to aim for net-zero emissions from flying by 2050. Air travel currently accounts for only 2 to 3% of global emissions, but it is a potent source of warming and is projected to keep growing. Longer-term solutions include battery-electric and hydrogen-powered planes, but in the meantime most airlines are turning to sustainable aviation fuel (SAF), a "drop-in" replacement that can be made from corn stalks or municipal solid waste, though it most often comes from used cooking oil. Climate Action 100+ has said a massive, rapid scale-up of SAF will be necessary to keep the 1.5C target within reach.

There is also a case for retooling commercial aviation to minimise contrails, the feathery ice clouds that some flights leave across the sky. Evidence suggests they have an outsized climate impact, and that adjusting a handful of flight plans each day could meaningfully help (see the Contrails section below).

Aviation is a heavy polluter by any measure: Ryanair alone sits among the top ten emitters in Europe, alongside nine fossil fuel companies.

## Why it matters

Flying is a small but fast-growing and highly unequal source of emissions, concentrated in a wealthy, frequent-flying minority, and it is one of the hardest sectors to decarbonise. Understanding where the emissions actually come from, and how far current policy really goes, matters for judging both personal choices and industry claims.

## Progress against a 1.5C pathway: "Critically insufficient"

The Climate Action Tracker rates aviation's progress as **Critically Insufficient**. Despite the sector agreeing to "carbon neutral growth" from 2020 and a "long-term aspirational goal" of net zero by 2050, the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) and national governments are not taking the action needed to keep aviation on a 1.5C-aligned path.

The sector is also far off its own 10% sustainable fuel target for 2030. As one commentator put it, aviation is less "hard-to-abate" and more "not-really-trying-to-abate": many airlines have promised to source around 10% of their fuel from cleaner sources by 2030, yet SAF use stood at just 0.17% in 2023, according to the International Air Transport Association (IATA).

## EU policy and Airbus's transition

The EU and its 27 member states include international aviation in their 2030 climate pledge (NDC), but the bloc's overall climate neutrality target does not explicitly cover international aviation emissions. Despite efforts by the European Commission and Parliament to reform the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) and introduce a kerosene tax and SAF quotas, measures to cut international aviation emissions remain limited:

- The EU ETS currently only covers flights within the European Economic Area (EEA) and some flights to and from Switzerland and the UK.
- A 2013 "stop the clock" decision limited the ETS to intra-EEA flights, to support a global approach through ICAO instead.
- Recent revisions expanded coverage to flights between the EEA and outermost EU regions, with some exceptions.
- Including all international flights departing the EEA in the ETS is suspended until at least 2026, pending an assessment of CORSIA, which critics see as a missed opportunity compared with what full ETS coverage could achieve.
- The ETS is gradually phasing out free allowances for airlines, but will keep allocating free SAF allowances until 2030 to encourage uptake.
- A revised ETS Directive requires airlines to report non-CO2 aviation impacts from 2025, initially for EEA/UK/Switzerland flights, with the Commission able to propose extending the ETS to cover non-CO2 effects after reviewing the data by the end of 2027.
- A proposed kerosene tax on intra-EU flights faces potential exemptions from member states.
- The ReFuelEU Directive mandates minimum SAF shares at EU airports, starting at 2% in 2025 and rising to 70% by 2050, including specific targets for synthetic fuels. Even so, this may fall short of a genuine 1.5C-aligned trajectory, and there are concerns about the sustainability of some permitted biofuels.

Manufacturers are also starting to respond. Airbus anticipates demand for 43,000 new aircraft over the next 20 years, an opportunity to replace older planes with far more efficient ones (up to 25% better fuel consumption). The best-known levers for cutting aviation emissions are well understood: more direct flights, efficiency gains from new aircraft and fuel substitutes, avoiding unnecessary circling, and investment in SAF. Airbus has signed a strategic partnership with TotalEnergies on the fuel side and remains committed to a 63% emissions reduction by 2030.

## What governments could do

Recommended measures for governments include scaling up SAF to 10 to 15% of fuel by 2030 and 100% by 2050; investing in R&D for alternatives such as electric batteries for small aircraft; optimising routing and air traffic flow management; improving aircraft energy efficiency; and reducing demand for international aviation. Governments, ICAO and industry all focus, to varying degrees, on the first four measures. Reducing demand gets far less attention, even though it is unlikely the sector can reach a 1.5C-compatible path without it.

## CORSIA: the global offsetting scheme explained

**CORSIA**, the Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation, is a global scheme created by ICAO, a UN agency, to address the growth in CO2 emissions from international flights. It aims to ensure that any growth in international aviation emissions above a set baseline is offset, mainly through improved efficiency, SAF use, and buying carbon offsets for remaining emissions.

**Why it exists:** international aviation emissions are not covered by national climate targets under the Paris Agreement and are growing rapidly relative to other sectors. CORSIA was designed as a single, harmonised global approach so airlines are not regulated differently country by country.

**How it works:**

1. **Baseline emissions** were originally meant to be based on 2019 to 2020 levels, but were adjusted because of COVID-19 to use 2019 alone.
2. **Monitoring and reporting**: airlines must monitor, report and verify (MRV) the CO2 emissions from their international flights.
3. **Offsetting**: if an airline's emissions exceed the baseline, it must purchase and cancel eligible carbon credits to compensate for the excess.

**Phases:** a voluntary pilot phase ran from 2021 to 2023, a voluntary first phase runs from 2024 to 2026, and a mandatory second phase (for most countries) runs from 2027 to 2035. Some countries, including least developed countries and small island states, are exempt.

**Coverage:** CORSIA applies only to international flights, not domestic aviation, and currently only to CO2, not other warming effects. It targets emissions growth above the baseline, not total emissions.

**Eligible offsets** must meet strict ICAO criteria: additionality, permanence, no double counting, and robust verification. ICAO approves specific programmes and credit vintages; not all carbon credits qualify.

**Common criticisms:** CORSIA relies heavily on offsets rather than direct emissions cuts, does not address non-CO2 impacts such as contrails and NOx, and its baseline approach allows absolute emissions to stay high. There are also ongoing quality concerns about some offset credits.

**Why it matters for Europe and Ireland:** CORSIA operates alongside the EU ETS. For many flights, airlines must comply with both schemes, though rules are being aligned to avoid double regulation. This is directly relevant to airlines, corporate travel emissions, and sustainability and climate reporting more broadly.

**Case study, clean cooking offsets:** as CORSIA moves from policy into real-world delivery, Gold Standard has become an operational leader for CORSIA-eligible credits. Its position is that clean cooking, often seen as a complex, hard-to-measure area, is an under-recognised way for airlines to fund verified emissions reductions alongside real social benefits, provided the underlying methodologies stay robust, conservative and evidence-based. ICAO has confirmed Gold Standard as an approved supplier of eligible units for CORSIA's second phase, from 2027 to 2029.

## AI for airport and approach efficiency

AI could reduce the minimum landing distance from 2.5 miles to 2 miles. That sounds simple, but is not: it would need to cut the amount of time planes spend waiting and circling while burning fuel, and could also help air traffic control manage flight levels more precisely during the step-down descent approach.

There is also potential on the ground. A parked plane burns 60 to 80 gallons of fuel per hour. Using sensors to track emissions and instruct airports to shut down idling aircraft would cut fuel waste and improve air quality for airport staff.

#### Contrails

Contrails form when hot jet exhaust meets cold high-altitude air and triggers ice crystals. Most vanish quickly, but in cold, humid conditions they persist and spread into cloud that traps outgoing heat. They are estimated to cause **around a third of aviation's total climate warming impact** — roughly as much as the CO2 from burning the fuel itself.

A £5m UK trial called **Operation Blue Skies** (starting November 2026) is the first attempt to tackle this across an entire airspace rather than one airline at a time. Google, the UK government, the Met Office, NATS and researchers at Cambridge and Imperial College London are using AI-generated forecasts of atmospheric conditions to predict where persistent, warming contrails will form over the **Shanwick Oceanic airspace** (eastern North Atlantic, ~5% of global contrail warming, concentrated November-February). Air traffic controllers will then instruct pilots to shift altitude by up to 2,000 feet — the kind of routine adjustment already used to dodge turbulence.

The trade-off is small: re-routing costs about 100kg of extra fuel per aircraft (a CO2 penalty of under 1%), against a contrail warming saving measured in tonnes or tens of tonnes. Earlier single-airline trials of the same AI forecasts cut contrail formation by 50-70%.

Source: [Operation Blue Skies (BBC, source summary)](../../sources/contrails-operation-blue-skies-bbc-2026.md); [Operation Blue Skies (New Scientist, source summary)](../../sources/contrails-operation-blue-skies-newscientist-2026.md)

## Air traffic is growing, and will keep growing

Air travel is only 2 to 3% of global emissions today, but the number of flights may double between 2020 and 2040. As Delta's sustainability director Andie Wood put it: "We just know as an industry, yes, we're 2% to 3% of global greenhouse gas emissions today, but as other industries start to decarbonize, there's modeling that suggests we would start to have a really big impact on society in terms of as a percentage of greenhouse gas emissions. And so we can't wait for those other technologies to come online."

Some key numbers:

- Flying remains the most carbon-intensive way to travel: an average of 285g of CO2 per km, against 158g for cars and 14g for trains.
- There were 4.5 billion passengers per year as of the most recent data, with 10 billion forecast for 2050.
- Airbus and Boeing took around 2,500 new orders in 2023, with more than 48,000 planes forecast by 2042, up from around 25,000 today.
- New planes are considerably more efficient, using up to 25% less fuel, which is genuine good news within an otherwise growing fleet.
- Cheap flights have made flying a commodity; some now argue it should be rationed or taxed more heavily.
- In Ireland, 40% of the population takes at least three flights a year.

## Flying is not an equal footprint

When discussions of climate action turn to inequality, it is worth remembering that those of us who can fly at all are a global outlier. Flying's total share of emissions looks small worldwide (1.7 to 2.4%), but that share is concentrated in a very small slice of the population:

- Only 11% of the world's population flew in 2018.
- More than two-thirds of people in Germany never fly.
- More than half of adults in the US and UK never fly.
- The most frequent 1% of flyers cause half of all aviation emissions.
- 68% of all flights are taken by just 12% of adults, who take six or more flights a year.

Private jets sharpen this picture further. They pollute up to 14 times more per passenger than commercial flights, yet are taxed less. Emissions from private aviation jumped 46% between 2019 and 2023, based on an analysis of 18.7 million flights by almost 26,000 aircraft, according to [New Scientist](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2455196-carbon-emissions-from-private-jets-have-exploded-in-recent-years/) and [the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2023/aug/10/private-jets-are-awful-for-the-climate-its-time-to-tax-the-rich-who-fly-in-them).

This concentration is at the heart of the fairness problem: a small, wealthy group of frequent flyers accounts for a hugely disproportionate share of aviation's climate impact. Some campaigners argue that access to flying should be rationed, with individuals given an annual allowance of kilometres they can travel, though it is far from clear whether frequent flyers are willing to accept limits on their own travel.

## We consistently misjudge our own climate impact

People tend to overestimate the impact of actions like recycling and underestimate the impact of flying and having children. A widely cited [2017 study](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/aa7541) recommended four high-impact, low-emissions personal actions: having one fewer child (58.6 tonnes CO2e saved per year on average in developed countries), living car-free (2.4 tCO2e saved per year), avoiding a transatlantic round-trip flight (1.6 tCO2e saved), and eating a plant-based diet (0.8 tCO2e saved per year). The study found these actions are far more effective than commonly promoted alternatives: comprehensive recycling is roughly four times less effective than a plant-based diet, and changing household lightbulbs roughly eight times less effective.

## Are people willing to fly less?

Only 54% of 18 to 34 year-olds say they are ready to consider reducing flights for holidays, and younger people are notably divided on the issue.

One proposed solution is reducing the number of take-off slots at busy airports. Dublin Airport has been pushing to expand, a move that would add an estimated 750,000 tonnes of additional emissions, while other airports have gone the other way: Amsterdam Schiphol, Europe's third-largest airport, has cut take-off slots by 12%.

Some individuals and organisations are already acting. British junior athlete Innes Fitzgerald turned down a competition place because it was in Australia, citing the flight's climate impact. Coldplay has committed to cutting its live tour emissions by 50%. And according to Delta's chief sustainability officer, sustainability is now in the top ten concerns raised by the airline's customers, not the single biggest concern, but a rising one.

## Corporate travel's outsized role

Corporate air travel deserves particular attention: although aviation is a small share of global emissions overall, business flying drives a disproportionately large share of that impact, and business travel spending is projected to keep rebounding. Most corporations, however, are failing to measure, report or set credible targets for their flight-related emissions, including important non-CO2 effects like contrails.

This matters because corporate action, concentrated among a relatively small number of frequent flyers and large companies, has real potential for fast, significant cuts:

- Business travel accounts for roughly 15 to 20% of air travel emissions globally, and 25 to 30% at the European level.
- Business travellers make up only around 12% of passengers in Europe, yet account for around 30% of all aviation demand.
- A 2023 study found 85% of global corporations have no credible plan to reduce emissions from corporate flying.
- In an updated 2025 travel-smart ranking, 44% of the 326 companies assessed still had no specific target for business travel.
- Companies that set specific air travel targets achieved an average emissions reduction of 48%, compared with just 28% for those without targets.

## Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF)

SAF is a biofuel used to power aircraft, with similar properties to conventional jet fuel but a smaller carbon footprint. Depending on the feedstock and production method, SAF can cut lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions dramatically compared with conventional jet fuel, and some emerging SAF pathways are even net-negative.

The industry's stated path to net zero by 2050 includes a target of SAF supplying 10% of all flight fuel by 2030. Progress against that target varies sharply by region: around 0.5% in the US, and in Europe a target of 2% by 2025, rising to 6% by 2030 and 70% by 2050. For more detail on how SAF is made and its trade-offs, see [Sustainable Aviation Fuel: A Necessary but Complicated Solution](../../solutions/electrification/Sustainable%20Aviation%20Fuel%20-%20A%20Necessary%20but%20Complicated%20Solution.md).

## Carbon offsets and flying

Carbon offsetting is part of the net-zero picture, since aviation is unlikely ever to be fully neutral without it: Europe has the ETS, and ICAO has CORSIA. Personal offsetting, though, is a much murkier landscape. For the detail on how offset prices vary and what they actually buy, see [Flight Carbon Footprint and Offset Guide](Flight%20Carbon%20Footprint%20and%20Offset%20Guide.md#carbon-offsetting-for-the-aviation-industry).

## Key facts

- SAF production in 2026 will reach only 2.4 million tonnes, 0.8% of airline fuel needs, against a 2050 target of 500 million tonnes (IATA, June 2026).
- SAF use stood at just 0.17% of aviation fuel in 2023 against a ~10% by 2030 industry target (IATA).
- Contrails are estimated to cause around a third of aviation's total warming impact, roughly as much as CO2 from the fuel burnt.
- The most frequent 1% of flyers cause half of all aviation emissions; 68% of flights are taken by 12% of adults.
- Private jet emissions rose 46% between 2019 and 2023.
- Avoiding one transatlantic round-trip flight saves an estimated 1.6 tonnes CO2e (Wynes & Nicholas, 2017).
- Business travel drives 15-20% of global aviation emissions and 25-30% in Europe, despite being a minority of passengers.
