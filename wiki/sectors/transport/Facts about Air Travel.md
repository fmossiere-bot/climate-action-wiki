## 2026 Update: The Industry Admits the 2050 Target Is Slipping

In June 2026, at the annual IATA summit in Rio de Janeiro, IATA director general Willie Walsh admitted publicly that aviation's net zero 2050 pledge will "probably not now be achieved." He called for a "realistic timeline" and blamed three groups: fuel suppliers for not delivering on their SAF commitments, governments for weakening CORSIA through inaction, and aircraft manufacturers for delaying the next generation of efficient planes.

The figures show why. **SAF production in 2026 will reach only 2.4 million tonnes, just 0.8% of what airlines need.** The 2050 target needs 500 million tonnes (65% of fuel supply). Walsh was blunt about the goal governments agreed through ICAO in 2023, to make aviation fuel 5% less carbon-intensive by 2030: "There is no path to meet that outcome". IATA's own sustainability vice-president called the EU and UK targets for e-SAF by 2030 "utterly detached from reality."

Environmental campaigners have long argued that aviation's net zero pledges were greenwashing and a cover for further expansion, so they are not surprised. The admission puts pressure on the UK government, which made further Heathrow expansion conditional on meeting climate tests.

Source: [The Guardian, Gwyn Topham, 8 June 2026](../../sources/iata-netzero-unlikely-2026.md)

# Facts about Air Travel

## What is it

In October 2022, countries agreed to aim for net-zero emissions from flying by 2050. Air travel currently accounts for only around 2-3% of global emissions, but it causes a lot of warming and is expected to keep growing. In the longer term, battery-electric and hydrogen-powered planes may help. In the meantime, most airlines are turning to sustainable aviation fuel (SAF). It is a "drop-in" replacement, meaning it works in today's engines, and can be made from corn stalks or household rubbish, though it most often comes from used cooking oil. Climate Action 100+ has said SAF will need to be scaled up massively and quickly to keep the 1.5C target within reach.

There is also a case for changing how commercial flights are run to avoid contrails, the feathery ice clouds some planes leave across the sky. Evidence suggests they have a large climate impact, and that changing a handful of flight plans each day could make a real difference (see the Contrails section below).

Aviation is a heavy polluter by any measure. Ryanair alone is among the top ten emitters in Europe, alongside nine fossil fuel companies.

## Why it matters

Flying is a small source of emissions, but it is growing fast and is very unequal: most of it comes from a wealthy minority who fly often. It is also one of the hardest sectors to decarbonise. Knowing where the emissions really come from, and how far current policy really goes, helps when judging both personal choices and industry claims.

## Progress against a 1.5C pathway: "Critically insufficient"

The Climate Action Tracker rates aviation's progress as **Critically Insufficient**. The sector agreed to "carbon neutral growth" from 2020 and a "long-term aspirational goal" of net zero by 2050. Even so, the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) and national governments are not doing what is needed to keep aviation on a path consistent with 1.5C.

The sector is also far from the 10% sustainable fuel by 2030 that many airlines have pledged. As one commentator put it, aviation is less "hard-to-abate" and more "not-really-trying-to-abate". Many airlines have promised to get around 10% of their fuel from cleaner sources by 2030, yet SAF made up just 0.17% of fuel in 2023, according to the International Air Transport Association (IATA).

## EU policy and Airbus's transition

The EU and its 27 member states include international aviation in their 2030 climate pledge (NDC), but the EU's overall climate neutrality target does not explicitly cover emissions from international flights. The European Commission and Parliament have tried to reform the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) and to bring in a kerosene tax and SAF quotas. Even so, measures to cut emissions from international aviation are still limited:

- The EU ETS currently only covers flights within the European Economic Area (EEA) and some flights to and from Switzerland and the UK.
- A 2013 "stop the clock" decision limited the ETS to flights within the EEA, to support a global approach through ICAO instead.
- Recent revisions extended coverage to flights between the EEA and the EU's outermost regions, with some exceptions.
- Bringing all international flights leaving the EEA into the ETS is on hold until at least 2026, pending a review of CORSIA. Critics see this as a missed opportunity compared with what full ETS coverage could achieve.
- The ETS is gradually phasing out free allowances for airlines, but will keep giving free allowances for SAF until 2030 to encourage its use.
- A revised ETS Directive requires airlines to report the non-CO2 effects of flying from 2025, at first for flights within the EEA, UK and Switzerland. After reviewing the data by the end of 2027, the Commission can propose extending the ETS to cover non-CO2 effects.
- A proposed kerosene tax on flights within the EU may be weakened by exemptions for some member states.
- The ReFuelEU Directive sets minimum shares of SAF at EU airports, starting at 2% in 2025 and rising to 70% by 2050, with specific targets for synthetic fuels. Even this may fall short of a path that really matches 1.5C, and there are concerns about how sustainable some of the permitted biofuels are.

Manufacturers are starting to respond too. Airbus expects demand for 43,000 new aircraft over the next 20 years, a chance to replace older planes with much more efficient ones (using up to 25% less fuel). The main ways to cut aviation emissions are well known: more direct flights, more efficient new aircraft, replacement fuels, less circling while waiting to land, and investment in SAF. Airbus has signed a strategic partnership with TotalEnergies on fuel and remains committed to cutting the emissions from its own sites and energy use (scope 1 and 2) by 63% by 2030, compared with 2015. That target does not cover the far larger emissions from the planes it sells.

## What governments could do

Recommended measures for governments include scaling up SAF to 10 to 15% of fuel by 2030 and 100% by 2050; investing in research into alternatives such as batteries for small aircraft; better routing and air traffic management; more energy-efficient aircraft; and reducing demand for international flights. Governments, ICAO and industry all work on the first four to some degree. Reducing demand gets much less attention, even though the sector is unlikely to reach a 1.5C-compatible path without it.

## CORSIA: the global offsetting scheme explained

**CORSIA**, the Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation, is a global scheme set up by ICAO, a UN agency, to deal with rising CO2 emissions from international flights. It aims to make sure any growth in international aviation emissions above a set baseline is offset, mainly through better efficiency, using SAF, and buying carbon offsets for the emissions that remain.

**Why it exists:** emissions from international flights are not covered by national climate targets under the Paris Agreement, and they are growing fast compared with other sectors. CORSIA was designed as one shared global approach, so airlines are not regulated differently in each country.

**How it works:**

1. Baseline emissions were first meant to be based on 2019 to 2020 levels, but because of COVID-19 they were changed to use 2019 alone.
2. Monitoring and reporting: airlines must monitor, report and verify (MRV) the CO2 emissions from their international flights.
3. Offsetting: if an airline's emissions go above the baseline, it must buy and cancel eligible carbon credits to make up for the excess.

**Phases:** a voluntary pilot phase ran from 2021 to 2023, a voluntary first phase runs from 2024 to 2026, and a mandatory second phase (for most countries) runs from 2027 to 2035. Some countries, including the least developed countries and small island states, are exempt.

**Coverage:** CORSIA only applies to international flights, not domestic ones, and for now only to CO2, not to other warming effects. It targets the growth in emissions above the baseline, not total emissions.

**Eligible offsets** must meet strict ICAO criteria: additionality (the cut would not have happened anyway), permanence, no double counting, and robust verification. ICAO approves specific programmes and credit vintages (the year a credit was issued), and not all carbon credits qualify.

**Common criticisms:** CORSIA relies heavily on offsets rather than direct cuts in emissions. It ignores non-CO2 effects such as contrails and NOx, and its baseline approach lets total emissions stay high. There are also continuing concerns about the quality of some offset credits.

**Why it matters for Europe and Ireland:** CORSIA runs alongside the EU ETS. On many flights, airlines must comply with both, though the rules are being aligned so they are not regulated twice. This matters directly for airlines, for company travel emissions, and for sustainability and climate reporting more widely.

**Case study, clean cooking offsets:** as CORSIA moves from policy into practice, Gold Standard has become a leading supplier of CORSIA-eligible credits. Its view is that clean cooking, often seen as complex and hard to measure, is an under-recognised way for airlines to pay for verified emissions cuts that also bring real social benefits, as long as the methods behind them stay robust, cautious and evidence-based. ICAO has confirmed Gold Standard as an approved supplier of eligible units for CORSIA's second phase, from 2027 to 2029.

## AI for airport and approach efficiency

AI could reduce the minimum landing distance between planes from 2.5 miles to 2 miles. That sounds simple, but it is not. It would need to cut the time planes spend waiting and circling while burning fuel, and it could also help air traffic control manage flight levels more precisely as planes step down on their descent.

There is also potential on the ground. A parked plane burns 60 to 80 gallons of fuel an hour. Using sensors to track emissions and tell airports to shut down idling aircraft would cut wasted fuel and improve air quality for airport staff.

#### Contrails

Contrails form when hot jet exhaust meets cold air high up and creates ice crystals. Most disappear quickly, but in cold, humid conditions they last and spread into cloud that traps heat escaping from the Earth. They are estimated to cause **around a third of aviation's total climate warming impact**, roughly as much as the CO2 from burning the fuel itself.

A £5m UK trial called **Operation Blue Skies** (starting November 2026) is the first attempt to tackle this across a whole airspace rather than one airline at a time. Google, the UK government, the Met Office, NATS and researchers at Cambridge and Imperial College London are using AI forecasts of atmospheric conditions to predict where long-lasting, warming contrails will form over the Shanwick Oceanic airspace. This area of the eastern North Atlantic accounts for ~5% of global contrail warming, mostly between November and February. Air traffic controllers will then tell pilots to change altitude by up to 2,000 feet, the kind of routine adjustment already used to avoid turbulence.

The trade-off is small. Re-routing costs about 100kg of extra fuel per aircraft (adding under 1% to its CO2), while the saving in contrail warming is measured in tonnes or tens of tonnes. Earlier trials with single airlines, using the same AI forecasts, cut contrail formation by 50-70%.

Source: [Operation Blue Skies (BBC, source summary)](../../sources/contrails-operation-blue-skies-bbc-2026.md); [Operation Blue Skies (New Scientist, source summary)](../../sources/contrails-operation-blue-skies-newscientist-2026.md)

## Air traffic is growing, and will keep growing

Air travel is only 2 to 3% of global emissions today, but the number of flights may double between 2020 and 2040. Delta's sustainability director Andie Wood put it this way: "We just know as an industry, yes, we're 2% to 3% of global greenhouse gas emissions today, but as other industries start to decarbonize, there's modeling that suggests we would start to have a really big impact on society in terms of as a percentage of greenhouse gas emissions. And so we can't wait for those other technologies to come online."

Some key numbers:

- Flying is still the most carbon-intensive way to travel: an average of 285g of CO2 per km, against 158g for cars and 14g for trains.
- There were 4.5 billion passengers a year in the most recent data, with 10 billion forecast for 2050.
- Airbus and Boeing took around 2,500 new orders in 2023, and more than 48,000 planes are forecast by 2042, up from around 25,000 today.
- New planes are much more efficient, using up to 25% less fuel, which is good news even as the fleet grows.
- Cheap flights have turned flying into an everyday product, and some now argue it should be rationed or taxed more heavily.
- In Ireland, 40% of the population takes at least three flights a year.

## Flying is not an equal footprint

When talk of climate action turns to inequality, it helps to remember that anyone who can fly at all is unusual by global standards. Flying's share of total emissions looks small worldwide (around 2-3%), but it comes from a very small part of the population:

- Only 11% of the world's population flew in 2018.
- More than two-thirds of people in Germany never fly.
- More than half of adults in the US and UK never fly.
- The most frequent 1% of flyers cause half of all aviation emissions.
- 68% of all flights are taken by just 12% of adults, who take six or more flights a year.

Private jets make the gap even wider. They pollute up to 14 times more per passenger than commercial flights, yet are taxed less. Emissions from private flying jumped 46% between 2019 and 2023, according to an analysis of 18.7 million flights by almost 26,000 aircraft reported by [New Scientist](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2455196-carbon-emissions-from-private-jets-have-exploded-in-recent-years/) and [the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2023/aug/10/private-jets-are-awful-for-the-climate-its-time-to-tax-the-rich-who-fly-in-them).

This concentration is the heart of the fairness problem: a small, wealthy group of frequent flyers causes a hugely outsized share of aviation's climate impact. Some campaigners argue that flying should be rationed, with each person given a yearly allowance of kilometres, though it is far from clear that frequent flyers would accept limits on their own travel.

## We consistently misjudge our own climate impact

People tend to overestimate the impact of things like recycling and underestimate the impact of flying and having children. A widely cited [2017 study](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/aa7541) recommended four personal actions that cut emissions a lot: having one fewer child (58.6 tonnes CO2e saved per year on average in developed countries), living without a car (2.4 tCO2e saved per year), avoiding one transatlantic return flight (1.6 tCO2e saved), and eating a plant-based diet (0.8 tCO2e saved per year). The study found these are far more effective than the actions usually promoted. Thorough recycling is roughly four times less effective than a plant-based diet, and changing household lightbulbs roughly eight times less effective.

## Are people willing to fly less?

Only 54% of 18 to 34 year-olds say they are ready to think about taking fewer flights for holidays, and younger people are clearly divided on the issue.

One proposed answer is to cut the number of take-off slots at busy airports. Dublin Airport has been pushing to expand, which would add an estimated 750,000 tonnes of extra emissions. Other airports have gone the other way: Amsterdam Schiphol, Europe's third-largest airport, has cut take-off slots by 12%.

Some people and organisations are already acting. British junior athlete Innes Fitzgerald turned down a place in a competition because it was in Australia, citing the climate impact of the flight. Coldplay has committed to cutting the emissions of its live tours by 50%. And according to Delta's chief sustainability officer, sustainability is now among the top ten concerns raised by the airline's customers. It is not the biggest one, but it is rising.

## Corporate travel's outsized role

Business flying deserves particular attention. Aviation is a small share of global emissions overall, but business travel causes an outsized share of that impact, and spending on business travel is expected to keep recovering. Yet most companies do not measure, report or set credible targets for the emissions from their flights, including important non-CO2 effects such as contrails.

This matters because company action, focused on a fairly small number of frequent flyers and large firms, could deliver fast, significant cuts:

- Business travel accounts for roughly 15 to 20% of air travel emissions globally, and 25 to 30% in Europe.
- Business travellers are only around 12% of passengers in Europe, yet account for around 30% of all demand for flights.
- A 2023 study found 85% of global corporations have no credible plan to cut emissions from business flying.
- In an updated 2025 travel-smart ranking, 44% of the 326 companies assessed still had no specific target for business travel.
- Companies that set specific air travel targets cut emissions by 48% on average, compared with just 28% for those without targets.

## Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF)

SAF is a biofuel for aircraft. It behaves much like conventional jet fuel but has a smaller carbon footprint. Depending on what it is made from and how, SAF can cut greenhouse gas emissions over its whole life cycle dramatically compared with conventional jet fuel, and some new ways of making it even remove more carbon than they emit.

Many airlines have pledged for SAF to supply 10% of their fuel by 2030, as a step towards net zero by 2050. Governments, through ICAO, have set a separate goal of making aviation fuel 5% less carbon-intensive by 2030. These targets measure different things, which is why the numbers differ. Progress varies widely by region: around 0.5% in the US, while Europe has a target of 2% by 2025, rising to 6% by 2030 and 70% by 2050. For more on how SAF is made and its trade-offs, see [Sustainable Aviation Fuel: A Necessary but Complicated Solution](../../solutions/electrification/Sustainable%20Aviation%20Fuel%20-%20A%20Necessary%20but%20Complicated%20Solution.md).

## Solution: efficiency and fewer contrails thanks to AI

This covers flight carbon calculators and Google's work with AI and satellite images on one of aviation's biggest impacts: contrails. These ice clouds created by planes trap heat (a warming effect known as radiative forcing). They are often overlooked, but are estimated to cause around a third of aviation's warming, come from a small share of flights, and can largely be avoided by re-routing. In 2026 this moved from an idea to a funded real-world trial with a start date: **Operation Blue Skies**, a £5m UK programme testing AI-forecast contrail avoidance across the whole Shanwick North Atlantic airspace from November 2026. ## Carbon offsets and flying

Carbon offsetting is part of the net-zero picture, because aviation is unlikely ever to be fully carbon neutral without it. Europe has the ETS, and ICAO has CORSIA. Personal offsetting is much murkier. For detail on how offset prices vary and what they actually buy, see [Flight Carbon Footprint and Offset Guide](Flight%20Carbon%20Footprint%20and%20Offset%20Guide.md#carbon-offsetting-for-the-aviation-industry).

## Key facts

- SAF production in 2026 will reach only 2.4 million tonnes, 0.8% of airline fuel needs, against a 2050 target of 500 million tonnes (IATA, June 2026).
- SAF made up just 0.17% of aviation fuel in 2023, against the ~10% by 2030 that many airlines have pledged (IATA).
- Contrails are estimated to cause around a third of aviation's total warming impact, roughly as much as the CO2 from the fuel burnt.
- The most frequent 1% of flyers cause half of all aviation emissions; 68% of flights are taken by 12% of adults.
- Private jet emissions rose 46% between 2019 and 2023.
- Avoiding one transatlantic return flight saves an estimated 1.6 tonnes CO2e (Wynes & Nicholas, 2017).
- Business travel causes 15-20% of global aviation emissions and 25-30% in Europe, though business travellers are a minority of passengers.
