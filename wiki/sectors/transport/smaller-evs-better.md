# Smaller EVs Are Better: Why Bigger Batteries Undermine the Transition

## What is it

Switching to electric vehicles is essential to cut emissions from transport. But within that progress there is a quieter, awkward pattern: the EVs on sale are getting bigger, fast. In 2023, **two-thirds of battery-electric models** in the major EV markets were SUVs, pickups or large cars, up from one-third in 2017. Only about a quarter of the new EV models expected through 2028 will be small or medium sized. Bigger electric vehicles need bigger batteries and more critical minerals, use more energy per kilometre and take up more room in the same cities. They are also more dangerous to people walking and cycling near them.

This page draws on a WRI analysis by Yiqian Zhang-Billert, Sarah Cassius and Cristina Albuquerque. Their argument is that the transition needs smaller EVs in particular, rather than any EV at all.

## Why it matters

Every large EV that replaces a small petrol or diesel car still cuts tailpipe emissions, and that is real progress. But every large EV bought instead of what could have been a small EV **doubles or triples the battery**. It puts more pressure on supplies of lithium, cobalt and nickel, and uses up to 20% more electricity per mile. Across a whole market, that adds up to extra demand for minerals and grid capacity that the transition could do without. In cities, it also means a 45% higher risk of a fatal collision with a pedestrian, in communities already dealing with traffic and heat.

## The size trend, in numbers

- **Two-thirds of BEV models in 2023** were SUVs, pickups or large cars, up from one-third in 2017
- Europe: fewer than 40% of BEVs are small or medium. US: only 25%. China: 50%
- Petrol and diesel cars in Europe show the same trend: SUV sales have tripled since 2011, while small car sales have halved
- Only about one-quarter of new EV models expected through 2028 will be small or medium

## What bigger EVs actually cost

- Energy: up to **20% more electricity** per vehicle, which puts more strain on the grid and raises emissions over the vehicle's life
- Critical minerals: putting smaller EVs first in Europe could **cut demand for battery minerals by 25%** ([Transport & Environment](https://www.transportenvironment.org)). The IEA finds that measures aimed at curbing demand for large vehicles could cut global mineral demand for EV batteries by 18–20% by 2050
- Safety: larger vehicles raise the risk of a fatal collision with a pedestrian by **45%**. Every extra 300 kg of vehicle weight raises the risk of fatal injury for cyclists, motorcyclists and pedestrians by 30% (European study)
- City space: SUVs and pickups need more parking, add more to traffic jams and fit less well into dense city centres

## Why the trend keeps growing

- Buyer preferences: people link larger vehicles with safety, space and flexibility, even when a smaller model suits their daily needs better
- What is on sale: carmakers cater to that preference, so smaller models are thin on the ground in showrooms
- **US tax credits** of up to $7,500 often work out better for electric SUVs
- **EU fleet-wide CO₂ targets** let carmakers balance heavier vehicles against cleaner smaller ones. This was meant to lower the cost of meeting the rules, but in practice it subsidises the production of larger vehicles

## The mismatch with actual travel

- The average person in Europe travels **12.4 km a day**
- The smallest EV models usually have a range of at least 135 km
- European cities are compact and densely populated. Historic centres, narrow streets and a mix of homes, shops and offices naturally discourage larger vehicles
- Range anxiety is a weak reason to buy a large SUV when the actual journey is the school run

## Policy tools that are working

- France: a **weight-based tax** on petrol, diesel and plug-in hybrid vehicles over 1,600 kg
- City of Paris: **tripled parking fees for SUVs**
- Norway: EV purchase taxes based on weight and emissions, so larger, higher-emission models cost considerably more
- More than **320 European cities** have brought in **low-emission zones** that keep the largest, most polluting vehicles out of city centres
- China: BYD, Wuling and others make cheap small cars (the **BYD Seagull and Wuling Bingo**, both priced under €10,000) that are now arriving in Europe
- VW, Renault and Stellantis are now bringing out smaller EVs under €25,000 in response

## Smaller cars are not the whole answer

Even a perfect switch to smaller EVs would not solve the wider problem of depending on cars. Smaller EVs **use less energy, need fewer minerals and make streets safer**, but they still cause congestion, urban sprawl and induced demand (new roads and easier driving encourage more driving). In cities, sustainable transport will need a broader shift towards public transport, cycling and walking. Smaller EVs have an important part to play where people do use cars, but they should work alongside efforts to reduce how much we rely on cars, not take their place.

## Key facts

- SUVs, pickups and large cars went from **1/3 of BEV models in 2017 → 2/3 in 2023**
- Larger EVs use up to **20% more electricity** than smaller EVs
- Putting smaller EVs first in Europe could **cut critical-mineral demand by 25%**
- Larger vehicles raise the risk of a fatal collision with a pedestrian by **45%**
- The average person in Europe travels **12.4 km a day**, well within the range of a compact EV
- More than **320 European cities** now have low-emission zones
