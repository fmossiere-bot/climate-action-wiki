# Long-Haul Shipping

## What is it

Shipping carries the vast majority of world trade. Along with aviation, it is widely seen as one of the hardest parts of transport to decarbonise. Batteries have become so cheap so quickly that **electric ships** are already the cheapest option on shorter routes, under about 1,500 km. Longer routes across oceans still run almost entirely on fossil fuel, and the technologies that could replace it, from hydrogen to modern sails, are only starting to reach commercial scale.

## Why it matters

Around 40% of all tonnage shipped around the world is fossil fuels and petrochemicals. In other words, the sector is closely tied to the very fuels the world is trying to move away from. Putting shipping on a believable path to cleaner fuels matters for its own emissions, and because almost everything traded between countries depends on it.

## Electrification is already working on shorter routes

A Norwegian company launched the world's first self-steering, all-electric cargo ship in 2021. By 2023, China had launched a battery-electric container ship on the Yangtze River with room for 700 containers. Its batteries can be swapped out mid-journey, so the ship does not need to stop and recharge. These examples show electric ships make commercial sense today, but only on shorter coastal and river routes where charging or battery-swapping stations are practical.

## Alternative fuels for longer routes

For long-haul routes, where batteries are not yet practical, several options are being explored:

- **Hydrogen**, though the infrastructure, and the means of storing it at sea, are still at an early stage.
- **Wind power**, shipping's oldest technology, now coming back through modern rigid sails and kite systems on cargo ships.
- **Green ammonia**, sometimes put forward for the largest and dirtiest cargo ships. However, [research published in Nature Energy](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41560-022-01124-4) warns that using ammonia at scale could do serious harm by disturbing the natural nitrogen cycle, so it is not automatically a clean option.

## Industry targets and the emissions gap

The industry itself calls shipping a "hard-to-decarbonise" sector. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) aims to cut the carbon intensity of international shipping (the emissions for each unit of cargo moved over each unit of distance) by 40% by 2030, compared with 2008. Governments have also agreed to cut shipping's total emissions by at least 20%, and ideally 30%, by 2030, according to [the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2023/jul/07/shipping-emissions-levy-delayed-but-goals-for-greenhouse-gas-cuts-agreed).

## Carbon capture on ships

A start-up called [Seabound](https://www.seabound.co/) is developing carbon capture equipment that sits on board cargo ships, and says it can capture up to 95% of the CO2 a ship emits. The system uses containers filled with a solid material that traps carbon. The captured CO2 is unloaded and treated on shore. This is still an early-stage technology, not a proven solution for the whole industry.

## The return of sail: the world's largest sailing cargo ship

In 2024, the largest sailing cargo ship in the world completed its first voyage across the Atlantic. Its carbon footprint was around ten times smaller than that of a similar container ship, according to [New Scientist](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2445620-worlds-largest-sailing-cargo-ship-makes-first-transatlantic-voyage/). It is an early sign that wind-assisted shipping can work at commercial scale, though it is still a long way from replacing most of the world's container ships.

## Key facts

- Electric ships are already the cheapest option for routes under roughly 1,500 km.
- China's 2023 battery-electric container ship on the Yangtze River carries up to 700 containers and uses swappable batteries.
- Around 40% of all tonnage shipped worldwide is fossil fuels and petrochemicals.
- The IMO's 2030 target is a 40% cut in carbon intensity compared with 2008, plus a 20 to 30% cut in total shipping emissions.
- Green ammonia is promoted as a shipping fuel, but at scale it risks disturbing the nitrogen cycle (Nature Energy).
- Seabound says its on-board carbon capture can catch up to 95% of a ship's CO2.
- The world's largest sailing cargo ship has a carbon footprint around 10 times smaller than a similar container ship.
