# Long-Haul Shipping

## What is it

Shipping moves the vast majority of world trade, and decarbonising it is widely considered one of the hardest transport challenges to solve, alongside aviation. **Rapidly falling battery costs have already made electrification the most economical option for shorter shipping routes, under about 1,500 km.** Longer, transoceanic routes still rely almost entirely on fossil fuel, and the technologies that could replace it, from hydrogen to modern sail power, are only beginning to reach commercial scale.

## Why it matters

Around 40% of all tonnage shipped globally is fossil fuels and petrochemicals, meaning the sector is deeply entangled with the fuels it is trying to help the world move away from. Getting shipping onto a credible decarbonisation path matters both for its own direct emissions and because it underpins almost everything else that gets traded internationally.

## Electrification is already working on shorter routes

A Norwegian company launched the world's first autonomous, all-electric cargo ship in 2021. By 2023, China had launched a battery-electric container ship with room for 700 containers on the Yangtze River, using batteries that can be swapped out mid-journey rather than needing to stop and recharge. These examples show electrification is commercially viable today, but only for shorter coastal and river routes where charging or battery-swapping infrastructure is feasible.

## Alternative fuels for longer routes

For the long-haul routes where batteries are not yet practical, several alternatives are being explored:

- **Hydrogen**, though infrastructure and storage at sea remain immature.
- **Wind power**, a return to shipping's oldest technology, now being reintroduced through modern rigid sails and kite systems on cargo vessels.
- **Green ammonia**, sometimes promoted as an option for the dirtiest, largest cargo ships. However, [research published in Nature Energy](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41560-022-01124-4) warns that ammonia use at scale could be highly damaging by disturbing the natural nitrogen cycle, so it is not a clean solution by default.

## Industry targets and the emissions gap

Shipping remains what the industry itself calls a "hard-to-decarbonise" sector. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has set a goal of cutting the carbon intensity of international shipping (emissions per unit of cargo moved per distance travelled) by 40% by 2030, against a 2008 baseline. Governments have also agreed to cut shipping's total emissions by at least 20%, with an ambition of 30%, by 2030, according to [the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2023/jul/07/shipping-emissions-levy-delayed-but-goals-for-greenhouse-gas-cuts-agreed).

## Carbon capture on ships

A start-up called [Seabound](https://www.seabound.co/) is developing onboard carbon capture for cargo ships, claiming it can capture up to 95% of a ship's emitted CO2. The system uses containers fitted with solid carbon capture material on board, with the captured CO2 unloaded and treated onshore. This remains an early-stage technology rather than a proven industry-wide solution.

## The return of sail: the world's largest sailing cargo ship

In 2024, the largest sailing cargo ship in existence completed its maiden transatlantic voyage, demonstrating a carbon footprint around ten times smaller than that of a comparable container ship, according to [New Scientist](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2445620-worlds-largest-sailing-cargo-ship-makes-first-transatlantic-voyage/). It is an early proof point for wind-assisted shipping at commercial scale, though still far from replacing the bulk of global container capacity.

## Key facts

- Electrification is already the most economical option for shipping routes under roughly 1,500 km.
- China's 2023 battery-electric container ship on the Yangtze River carries up to 700 containers and uses swappable batteries.
- Around 40% of all tonnage shipped globally is fossil fuels and petrochemicals.
- The IMO's 2030 target is a 40% cut in carbon intensity against a 2008 baseline, plus a 20 to 30% cut in total shipping emissions.
- Green ammonia, while promoted as a shipping fuel, risks disturbing the nitrogen cycle at scale (Nature Energy).
- Seabound's onboard carbon capture claims up to 95% CO2 capture per ship.
- The world's largest sailing cargo ship has a carbon footprint around 10 times smaller than an equivalent container ship.
