#### Key Numbers

- **1.2 billion** tyres are made worldwide every year, more than 38 per second
- **92.6%** of a tyre's environmental impact happens while it is being used, not while it is being made
- Making the tyre, including extracting its raw materials and distributing it, accounts for **6 to 10%** of its lifetime impact (Michelin)
- **8 million tonnes** of tyre waste (rubber, carbon, silica, steel, textile, chemicals) are not collected each year
- More than **17 million tonnes** of worn-out tyres are collected each year worldwide
- Tyres are responsible for **10 to 15%** of a car's total carbon footprint, and for around **20%** of its fuel use

---

#### What a Tyre Is Made Of

A tyre is a complicated product. It combines more than 200 different materials, and changing one can affect safety, performance or how long the tyre lasts. According to Michelin, the average car tyre is made of:

- 25% natural rubber (from hevea trees)
- 25% synthetic rubber (polymer chains, carbon-based molecules)
- 20% reinforcing materials such as carbon black and silica
- 15% textile or metal reinforcements
- 15% chemical additives and processing oils

Because of this, swapping in more sustainable materials is very difficult. Each material does a specific job, and a tyre cannot be allowed to become less reliable.

---

#### The Environmental Challenge

Tyres have a large environmental footprint over their whole life, from digging up and growing raw materials to getting rid of them at the end.

**During production**, the process uses a lot of energy and depends heavily on fossil fuels. Growing natural rubber can contribute to deforestation, and factories release volatile organic compounds and other pollutants.

**During use**, the impact is much bigger than most people realise. As tyres wear down, they shed tiny plastic particles that end up in soils and waterways. Rain washing off roads carries tyre chemicals into rivers and lakes. And because tyres directly affect fuel efficiency, heavier or less efficient tyres raise a vehicle's emissions over its whole life.

**At end of life**, tyres are very hard to deal with. They do not break down naturally, and they cannot simply be melted down and reused. Sending them to landfill is more and more restricted. In France, burning tyres is banned, but it still happens in other parts of the world, releasing toxic substances into the air.

---

#### What Is Happening on Recycling

Recycling is still limited, but it is slowly getting better. In France, around 530,000 tonnes of tyres are collected each year, a collection rate of 93.5%. Of those:

- 38% are recovered as materials (steel, rubber crumb, moulded objects)
- 48.6% are burnt for energy (in cement kilns and for urban heating)
- 13.4% are reused or retreaded

Worldwide, 8 million tonnes still go uncollected each year. Newer techniques such as pyrolysis and vapothermolysis (breaking tyres down with heat, or with heat and steam) could one day lead to "zero waste" tyres, but they do not yet work at industrial scale.

---

#### Michelin and the Road to Sustainability

Michelin sells 180 million tyres a year and is the world's market leader. It has set itself two public targets: **40% sustainable materials by 2030**, and 100% by 2050.

Today, the company says 31% of the materials it uses are renewable or recycled. Natural rubber makes up most of that (around 25%), while recycled materials are still only 2 to 3%. Hitting the 2030 target will take a lot of progress in sourcing and materials science.

The challenge is not only technical. There is no agreed European definition yet of what counts as a "renewable" tyre material. That makes it hard to compare claims between manufacturers, or to write useful rules.

There is also a deeper problem with the use phase. Electric vehicles are heavy and cars are getting bigger, so tyres must carry more weight while still lasting at least around 50,000 kilometres. Meeting those performance and safety standards while also switching to plant-based or recycled materials is a real engineering limit, as well as a commercial one.

Michelin is working on future technologies, including airless tyres, 3D-printed tread parts that can be changed with the seasons, and tyres that could last much longer than today's. These are all still in development.

**A European regulation on tyre sustainability is expected in 2026**, though what it will require of manufacturers and buyers has not yet been set out.

---

#### What This Means for Drivers

Most people choose tyres on price, brand and performance ratings. Very few think about environmental impact. That is partly because there is little information on it in the shop, and partly because tyre labels currently show fuel efficiency and grip on wet roads rather than impact over the tyre's whole life.

The coming EU rules may start to change this, but change in this sector is slow, and the technical barriers are real. In the meantime, the most useful thing drivers can do is keep their tyres at the right pressure (which affects both fuel use and tyre wear) and make their tyres last longer with regular rotation and alignment checks.
