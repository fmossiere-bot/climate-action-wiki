# FACTS about Rice Production

## What is it

Rice farming is a major source of agricultural methane, responsible for 18 to 20% of global agricultural methane emissions. Flooded rice fields deplete oxygen in the soil, creating the anaerobic conditions that fuel methane production; the gas escapes mainly through the rice plants themselves or as bubbles rising from the soil, with the exact amount varying depending on irrigation method, soil type and fertiliser use. Rice deserves a place in the climate conversation not just as a food, but as a genuinely significant piece of the emissions puzzle: a kilo of rice can generate more emissions than burning a litre of diesel.

## Greenhouse gas footprint

Rice paddies produce two main greenhouse gases. **Methane (CH4)** comes from the anaerobic conditions created by flooding, which favour methane-producing microbes; rice cultivation contributes roughly 10% of global agricultural methane emissions. **Nitrous oxide (N2O)** is a secondary concern: intermittent flooding, a method used specifically to cut methane emissions, can inadvertently increase nitrous oxide emissions instead, and nitrous oxide has a global warming potential nearly 300 times that of CO2.

Rice's global footprint is significant but geographically concentrated. Europeans and Americans get just 1 to 2% of their food energy from rice, but the figure is far higher across Asia, which consumes 89% of the world's harvested rice. Around 600 million tonnes of CO2-equivalent methane is thought to be emitted from rice paddies each year, accounting for about 1.2% of the global carbon footprint. An even larger factor is the roughly 167 million tonnes of fertiliser, mostly nitrogen-based, applied to rice fields worldwide. Because most rice is eaten close to where it is grown, transport contributes relatively little to its footprint, and what transport does happen would mostly be by cargo ship, a comparatively low-emissions mode, anyway. Put in relative terms, rice provides around 20% of the world's food energy for just 3.5% of its carbon footprint.

## Water, soil and air impacts

Rice farming is also highly water-intensive, accounting for nearly 30% of the world's freshwater usage, which worsens water scarcity in regions already under water stress. Continuous flooding can lead to soil nutrient depletion and reduced fertility over time, and the post-harvest burning of rice straw, still common practice in many regions, releases air pollutants that damage air quality and public health.

## Reducing methane from rice farming

See also [6 Ways to Reduce Methane from Agriculture](6%20Ways%20to%20Reduce%20Methane%20from%20Agriculture.md#6-curbing-methane-from-rice) for more on breeding low-methane rice varieties.

The two biggest levers for reducing rice's environmental footprint are the excess water and excess fertiliser used in conventional flooded cultivation. It is possible to grow rice without permanently flooding the field, cutting methane emissions substantially, though this approach is harder work and can require more fertiliser. Several specific practices are being adopted to address these trade-offs:

- **Alternate Wetting and Drying (AWD):** an irrigation technique that reduces methane emissions by up to 90% and conserves water, without compromising crop yields.
- **Integrated rice-fish farming:** introducing fish into rice paddies naturally controls pests and reduces methane emissions by altering the paddy's microbial communities.
- **Improved fertiliser management:** precision application of fertiliser minimises nitrous oxide emissions and prevents nutrient runoff.
- **Straw management:** rather than burning leftover rice straw, it can be repurposed as livestock feed or for mushroom cultivation, cutting air pollution while adding economic value for farmers.
