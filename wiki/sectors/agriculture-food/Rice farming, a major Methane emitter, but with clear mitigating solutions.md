# FACTS about Rice Production

## What is it

Rice farming is a major source of methane from agriculture, responsible for 18 to 20% of global agricultural methane emissions. Flooding a rice field cuts off the oxygen in the soil. Without oxygen, microbes that produce methane thrive. The gas escapes mainly through the rice plants themselves or as bubbles rising from the soil, and the amount depends on how the field is watered, the type of soil and the fertiliser used. So rice belongs in the climate conversation as a source of emissions as well as a food: a kilo of rice can generate more emissions than burning a litre of diesel.

## Greenhouse gas footprint

Rice paddies produce two main greenhouse gases. The first is **methane (CH4)**, which comes from microbes that thrive in flooded, oxygen-free soil. Rice growing contributes around 8% of all human-caused methane emissions, which is roughly a fifth of the methane from agriculture. The second is nitrous oxide (N2O), a smaller concern but a tricky one. Flooding fields only some of the time, a method used to cut methane, can end up raising nitrous oxide emissions instead, and nitrous oxide warms the planet nearly 300 times as much as CO2.

Rice's global footprint is large but concentrated in one part of the world. Europeans and Americans get just 1 to 2% of their food energy from rice, but the figure is far higher across Asia, which eats 89% of the world's harvested rice. Around 600 million tonnes of CO2-equivalent methane is thought to be released from rice paddies each year, about 1.2% of the global carbon footprint. An even bigger factor is the roughly 167 million tonnes of fertiliser, mostly nitrogen-based, spread on rice fields worldwide. Because most rice is eaten close to where it is grown, transport adds relatively little, and most of what is shipped goes by cargo ship, which has fairly low emissions. Counting methane, fertiliser and everything else together, rice provides around 20% of the world's food energy for about 3.5% of the global carbon footprint.

## Water, soil and air impacts

Rice also needs a lot of water. Irrigated rice takes 34 to 43% of the world's irrigation water, or about 24 to 30% of all the freshwater people draw on, which makes water shortages worse in regions that are already dry. Keeping fields flooded all the time can drain nutrients from the soil and make it less fertile over the years. Burning rice straw after the harvest, still common in many regions, releases pollutants that harm air quality and people's health.

## Reducing methane from rice farming

See also [6 Ways to Reduce Methane from Agriculture](6%20Ways%20to%20Reduce%20Methane%20from%20Agriculture.md#6-curbing-methane-from-rice) for more on breeding rice varieties that give off less methane.

The two biggest levers are the extra water and extra fertiliser used in conventional flooded farming. Rice can be grown without keeping the field permanently flooded, which cuts methane a lot, but it is harder work and can need more fertiliser. Farmers are adopting several practices to handle these trade-offs:

- **Alternate Wetting and Drying (AWD):** a way of watering that lets the field dry out between floods. It cuts methane emissions by up to 90% and saves water, without reducing harvests.
- **Integrated rice-fish farming:** fish in the paddies eat pests and change the mix of microbes in the water, which reduces methane.
- **Better fertiliser management:** applying fertiliser precisely, in the right amount at the right time, keeps nitrous oxide emissions down and stops nutrients washing away.
- **Straw management:** instead of burning leftover rice straw, farmers can use it as animal feed or to grow mushrooms, which cuts air pollution and earns them extra income.
