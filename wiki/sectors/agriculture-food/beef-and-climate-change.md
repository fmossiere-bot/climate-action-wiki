# Beef and Climate Change: Six Questions, Answered Plainly

## What is it

Beef sits where three big climate problems meet: methane from cattle burps (known as enteric fermentation), nitrous oxide from manure and fertiliser, and carbon dioxide from cutting down forests to make new pasture. Added up across a huge global supply chain, these make beef far more **emissions-intensive per gram of protein** than almost any other food. This page follows the World Resources Institute's plain-language approach to answer the six questions that come up most often, without turning it into a "should everyone go vegan?" argument.

## Why it matters

Plenty of people who accept that fossil fuels have to go still find the food side of the change hard. Beef is one of the clearer cases. A moderate cut in beef eating in the countries that eat the most would basically remove the need to keep clearing forests for pasture between now and 2050, and would improve people's health at the same time. No other single change in diet does as much.

## 1. How does beef cause emissions?

There are two main routes. First, cows and other **ruminants** (goats, sheep) give off methane as they digest grass. Manure also gives off methane, and both dung left on pasture and fertiliser on cattle-feed crops release nitrous oxide. Kilo for kilo, both gases trap far more heat than CO2. Second, rising demand pushes farmers to clear forests for new pasture, which releases the carbon stored in the trees and soil. The FAO estimated that in 2010 total beef emissions (production plus land-use change) were around 3 billion tonnes CO2-eq, roughly 7% of the global total.

## 2. Is beef more resource-intensive than other foods?

Yes. Ruminants grow more slowly than pigs and poultry, so they need more feed for each kilo of meat. Feed needs land, and so does grazing. Even allowing for the fact that much grassland could not be used for crops anyway, beef needs **around 20 times more land and emits around 20 times more greenhouse gases per gram of edible protein than beans**. That gap holds for almost every plant protein.

## 3. Why do some people say beef is only a small contributor?

Because they leave out the land. Most national emissions inventories, and many widely cited papers, count only the emissions from cattle and their feed inside the country. They leave out the emissions from cutting down a forest somewhere else to grow that feed or make new pasture. Once land-use change is included, and imports and exports are traced back to where the deforestation actually happens, the picture changes. WRI notes that when land use is properly counted, the average American diet produces about as much emissions as the average American's energy use.

## 4. Can beef be produced more sustainably?

Yes, up to a point. Farming more efficiently can cut emissions per kilo a lot, though beef will always use more resources than plants.

Examples WRI gives:

- **Silvopasture in Colombia:** mixing trees and grasses with cattle can quadruple the number of cows per acre and cut methane per kilo of meat
- **Kenya dairy trials:** feeding cattle napier grass and high-protein Calliandra shrubs cut methane per litre of milk by **8–60%**
- **Feed additives** such as 3-nitrooxypropanol (3-NOP) and seaweed reduce the methane cattle burp out
- Better **manure management**, and technologies that stop nitrogen turning into nitrous oxide

Efficiency helps, but on its own it does not solve the land problem if demand keeps growing.

## 5. Do we all need to stop eating beef?

No. WRI's Sustainable Food Future scenario has **ruminant meat eating in high-consuming countries falling to about 50 calories per day, or roughly 1.5 burgers per person per week**. That is about half current US levels and 25% below current European levels, but still well above the world average. At that level, even with 10 billion people on Earth by 2050, the world would need no more farmland overall, and the temperature targets would stay within reach as far as diet is concerned. Beef eating per person in the US has already fallen by about a third since the 1970s. The fall just needs to be about 1.5 times faster across the regions that eat the most.

Health is an extra benefit rather than the main argument. Diets high in red meat are linked to higher risks of heart disease, type-2 diabetes, stroke and bowel cancer, and people in rich countries usually already eat more protein than they need.

## 6. Would eating less beef harm agricultural jobs?

Not necessarily. Even if rich countries eat less, global demand for ruminant meat is expected to grow a lot by 2050, because populations and incomes are rising elsewhere. US beef production has stayed roughly steady since the 1970s, even as each American eats less, because of exports. Meanwhile, most of the big meat companies are already repositioning themselves as wider "protein companies" and investing in plant-based and blended products.

## Key facts

- Global beef emissions were about **3 billion tonnes CO2-eq (~7% of global GHG) in 2010**, comparable to India's total emissions
- Beef needs about **20× more land and emits 20× more GHG per gram of protein than beans**
- Without changes in diet, pasture could expand by **~400 million hectares (larger than India) by 2050**
- A target of **~50 calories of ruminant meat per person per day** in high-consuming countries would take most of the pressure off forests
- Silvopasture and better feed have cut methane per kilo of meat or milk by **8–60%** in field trials
- At COP26 (2021), countries committed to a **30% methane cut and an end to deforestation by 2030**, and both depend heavily on beef
