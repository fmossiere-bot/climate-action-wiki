# Beef and Climate Change: Six Questions, Answered Plainly

## What is it

Beef sits at the intersection of three big climate problems: **methane from cattle burps** (enteric fermentation), **nitrous oxide from manure and fertiliser**, and **carbon dioxide from cutting down forests to make new pasture**. Because these three add up across an enormous global supply chain, beef ends up much more emissions-intensive per gram of protein than almost any other food. This page follows the World Resources Institute's plain-language framing to answer the six questions that come up most often, without turning it into a "should everyone go vegan?" debate.

## Why it matters

Even people who agree that fossil fuels have to be phased out often struggle with the food side of the transition. The **beef story is unusually clear**: a moderate cut in high-consuming countries would basically eliminate the need to keep clearing forests for pasture between now and 2050, and improve health outcomes at the same time. There is no equivalent single lever in the diet space with that kind of leverage.

## 1. How does beef cause emissions?

Two main pathways. First, cows and other **ruminants** (goats, sheep) emit **methane** as they digest grasses. **Manure** also emits methane, and both wastes on pasture and fertiliser on cattle-feed crops emit **nitrous oxide**. Both are much more potent greenhouse gases than CO2 per kilogram. Second, rising demand pushes farmers to **clear forests to open new pasture**, releasing stored carbon. The FAO estimated that in 2010 total beef emissions (production plus land-use change) were around **3 billion tonnes CO2-eq**, roughly 7% of the global total.

## 2. Is beef more resource-intensive than other foods?

Yes. Ruminants grow more slowly than pigs and poultry, so they need more feed per kilo of meat. Feed needs land. Grazing needs land. Even after allowing for the fact that much grassland cannot be used to grow crops, beef requires **around 20 times more land and emits around 20 times more greenhouse gases per gram of edible protein than beans**. That gap holds across almost every plant protein.

## 3. Why do some people say beef is only a small contributor?

Because they leave out the land. Most national inventories, and many well-cited papers, count only the emissions from cattle and their feed inside the country, not the emissions from cutting down a forest somewhere else to grow that feed or open new pasture. Once land-use change is included, and once imports and exports are traced back to where the deforestation actually happens, the picture changes. WRI notes that when land use is properly counted, the average American diet has emissions roughly on par with per-capita US energy-related emissions.

## 4. Can beef be produced more sustainably?

Yes, up to a point. Efficiency improvements can cut emissions per kilo substantially, though beef will always be resource-intensive compared to plants.

Concrete examples cited by WRI:

- **Silvopasture in Colombia:** integrating trees and grasses with cattle can quadruple the number of cows per acre and cut methane per kilo of meat
- **Kenya dairy trials:** feeding cattle napier grass and high-protein Calliandra shrubs cut methane per litre of milk by **8–60%**
- **Feed additives** such as **3-nitrooxypropanol (3-NOP)** and seaweed reduce enteric methane
- Better **manure management** and technologies that prevent nitrogen turning into nitrous oxide

Efficiency helps, but it does not solve the land question on its own if demand keeps rising.

## 5. Do we all need to stop eating beef?

No. WRI's Sustainable Food Future scenario is that **ruminant meat consumption in high-consuming countries falls to about 50 calories per day, or roughly 1.5 burgers per person per week**. That is about half current US levels and 25% below current European levels, but still comfortably above the world average. At that level, and even in a world of 10 billion people by 2050, no further net agricultural land expansion is needed and the temperature targets remain reachable through diet alone. US per capita beef consumption has already fallen by about a third since the 1970s. The decline just needs to be about **1.5 times faster** across high-consuming regions.

Health is a co-benefit rather than an argument on its own: high red-meat diets are associated with higher risks of heart disease, type-2 diabetes, stroke and colorectal cancer, and people in high-income regions typically already eat more protein than they need.

## 6. Would eating less beef harm agricultural jobs?

Not necessarily. Even with high-income countries eating less, global ruminant meat demand is projected to grow substantially by 2050 because of population and income growth elsewhere. US beef production has held roughly steady since the 1970s despite falling per capita consumption, because of exports. In parallel, most of the large meat companies are already positioning themselves as broader "protein companies" and investing in plant-based and blended products.

## Key facts

- Global beef emissions were about **3 billion tonnes CO2-eq (~7% of global GHG) in 2010** — comparable to India's total emissions
- Beef needs about **20× more land and emits 20× more GHG per gram of protein than beans**
- Without dietary change, pastureland could expand by **~400 million hectares (larger than India) by 2050**
- A target of **~50 calories of ruminant meat per person per day** in high-consuming countries would remove most of the pressure on forests
- Silvopasture and improved feeds have cut methane per kilo of meat or milk by **8–60%** in field trials
- COP26 (2021) committed to a **30% methane cut and an end to deforestation by 2030** — both of which turn on beef
