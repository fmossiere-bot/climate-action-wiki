# Agriculture's Planetary Footprint

## What it is

Farming has changed the planet more than anything else humans do, and on some measures its scale and environmental impact are **bigger than those of fossil fuels**. It began roughly 12,000 years ago and spread slowly across the Earth. In the 20th century it sped up sharply. First farmland spread into new areas as forests and grasslands were cleared. Then came the Green Revolution of the 1960s, which added industrial fertilisers, irrigation and pesticides to get more out of each hectare.

Today, cropland and pasture together cover **37% of Earth's ice-free land**. That is more than Asia and Europe combined, and far more than all towns and cities (1 to 3%). Most of this land does not grow food for people: 75% of all farmland is used to raise animals or grow their feed, including most of the world's maize and soya.

## Why it matters

Because farming is so big, it is the main cause of almost every major environmental crisis on the planet: loss of wildlife, shrinking fresh water, nitrogen pollution and climate change. Most climate conversations focus on energy and transport, and farming's large role tends to be overlooked. Any realistic path to a sustainable planet has to include how we farm and what we eat.

## Land Use and Biodiversity Loss

Farming has taken **more land and driven more species to extinction** than any other human activity in history, more than logging, mining, urban sprawl or climate change. Whole landscapes have all but disappeared. The prairies of North America and Brazil's Atlantic rainforest are now almost entirely farmland. The cerrado (a vast tropical savanna) in South America, forests in West Africa and peatlands in Indonesia are still being cleared at alarming rates, mainly for pasture and for soya to feed animals.

## Freshwater Depletion

Farming accounts for **70% of global freshwater use**, roughly 2,800 km³ a year. That is enough to fill the Grand Canyon two-thirds full. In many regions this level of extraction cannot last. The Colorado River now rarely reaches the sea. The Aral Sea, once one of the world's largest inland bodies of water, has almost completely vanished after the rivers feeding it were diverted to grow cotton. Water tables are falling across major food-growing regions, from India to the US High Plains.

## Nitrogen and Chemical Pollution

Industrial use of synthetic fertilisers has **more than doubled** the natural flows of nitrogen and phosphorus across the Earth's surface. The extra nitrogen and phosphorus wash off fields into rivers, where they set off explosive algae growth that uses up the oxygen in the water and kills marine life. The "Dead Zone" in the Gulf of Mexico, fed by fertiliser running down the Mississippi River, is one of the largest in the world. Similar dead zones exist off almost every coastline downstream of intensive farming.

## Agriculture as a Climate Driver

Direct emissions from farming (crops, livestock and the land they use) make up **~22% of global greenhouse gas emissions**. Include the rest of the food system, such as processing, transport, packaging, refrigeration and food waste, and the figure rises to approximately one-third of all human emissions.

The biggest single driver is **deforestation**, mainly to create pasture and grow soya for animal feed in the Amazon, Central Africa and Indonesia. This alone accounts for ~10–11% of global emissions, about the same as the entire US economy. Cattle also produce large amounts of methane, a greenhouse gas that traps far more heat than CO₂ over the short term. Using too much fertiliser releases nitrous oxide, another potent greenhouse gas.

## Summary findings

- Cropland and pasture cover **37% of Earth's ice-free land**, by far the planet's main type of land cover.
- **75% of farmland** is there to support livestock, directly or by growing their feed.
- Farming uses **70% of global freshwater**, the biggest single human use of fresh water.
- Fertiliser runoff has **more than doubled** natural nitrogen flows, creating dead zones in coastal waters worldwide.
- Direct farming and land use make up **~22% of global GHG emissions**; the full food system makes up **~33%**.
- Deforestation for farming makes up **~10–11% of global emissions**, as much as the entire US economy.
- Protecting tropical forests is among the most effective climate solutions available.

## Update: global meat supply trends (June 2026)

A new FAO report (The Guardian, June 2026) confirms that meat eating is still rising. The average person now eats **six times more chicken** than in 1961 (3 kg → 17 kg) and twice as much pork (15 kg). Overall meat supply rose from 25 kg per person in 1961 to 47 kg in 2022. Farming emissions are forecast to rise 7.6% over the next decade, with livestock responsible for 80% of that increase. Scientists criticised the report for not recommending that wealthy countries eat less meat. Source: [fao-global-meat-supply-2026](../../sources/fao-global-meat-supply-2026.md)
