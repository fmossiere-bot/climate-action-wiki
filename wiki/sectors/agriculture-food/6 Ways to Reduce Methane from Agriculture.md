# 6 Ways to Reduce Methane from Agriculture

Nearly half of global methane emissions come from farming: livestock, rice growing and the burning of crop waste, according to the [Climate and Clean Air Coalition's 2021 Global Methane Assessment](https://www.ccacoalition.org/sites/default/files/resources/2021_Global-Methane_Assessment_full_0.pdf). Methane traps far more heat than CO2 but breaks down much faster, so cutting it is one of the quickest ways to slow warming in the near term. A growing range of farming solutions can help.

## Where agricultural methane comes from

Within farming, methane emissions break down roughly like this:

- **Ruminants** (cattle, sheep and goats) produce 55 to 60% of agricultural methane. Microbes in their stomachs break down food in a process called **enteric fermentation**, which makes methane that the animals release mostly by burping.
- **Manure** produces a further 6 to 8%, though some research suggests the real share could be much higher. On large dairy and pig farms, manure is often washed out with water and stored as liquid slurry in tanks or lagoons. These are anaerobic (oxygen-free) conditions, which suit the microbes that make methane.
- **Rice farming** contributes 18 to 20%. Flooding rice fields uses up the oxygen in the soil, which drives methane production. The gas escapes mainly through the rice plants themselves or as bubbles rising from the soil, and the amount depends on how the field is watered, the type of soil and the fertiliser used.
- **Burning crop residues**, the stalks and other material left after harvests such as rice and wheat, is common in some countries. It contributes 3 to 4% of agricultural methane and also releases air pollution that harms human health.

Six practical approaches are helping to bring these emissions down.

## 1. Making livestock more efficient

In many developing countries, each animal produces far less milk and meat than on farms in places like the United States. That leaves a lot of room to improve through better diets, breeding and animal health. More digestible feed means animals burp less and absorb more nutrients for growth and milk. In the US, [milk production grew by 53%](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2022/march/u-s-dairy-productivity-increased-faster-in-large-farms-and-across-southwestern-states) between 1990 and 2021, while enteric methane per unit of milk fell by around **25%** over the same period. Many farms in rich countries already use highly digestible feed. In Africa and South Asia, though, farmers often feed their animals poor-quality crop leftovers such as rice and wheat straw, which can make up as much as 70% of a cow's diet.

## 2. Stopping methane before it escapes: feed additives

Feed additives that block methane reduce the amount cows produce while digesting. They work best for commercial and large farms, because the cost puts off smaller farmers and the additives do not always raise productivity. A synthetic molecule called **3-nitroxypropanol (3-NOP)** has drawn a lot of interest. It has been through extensive trials and is approved for beef or dairy cows in more than 60 countries. Given in small doses through feed, it can [reduce methane production by around 30%](https://doi.org/10.3168/jds.2022-22211). Red seaweed (*Asparagopsis*), native to Australia and already approved for Australian beef feedlots, has also attracted strong commercial interest. Lab research suggests it could cut methane by up to 90%, but long-term trials have shown [closer to 30%](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022030224014309) on real farms.

## 3. Breeding low-methane cows

How much methane a cow makes is partly down to its genes, and some cows simply produce more than others. Farmers can now breed and select low-methane cows, alongside the long-standing practice of choosing healthier breeds that convert feed more efficiently. On average, low-methane cows [produce 22% less methane](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022030223004022) than high-emitting ones. Unlike feed additives, the benefit lasts the animal's whole life and can even be passed on to its calves.

## 4. Anti-methane vaccines

An early-stage but promising line of research is vaccines that stop methane forming in cows' stomachs. Calves would be given them like any other vaccine, with the effect lasting for life. Some research projects, including one backed by the [Bezos Earth Fund](https://www.bezosearthfund.org/news-and-insights/pioneering-cattle-methane-vaccine-project-launched-by-bezos-earth-fund), are aiming for a 30% cut in methane this way.

## 5. Manure digesters, and their limits

Biogas digesters use manure to make biogas, a mix of methane and carbon dioxide. The methane is captured to generate electricity or compressed into "renewable natural gas." Digesters can reduce methane emissions, but their benefits have often been overstated. Reports frequently leave out methane leaking from the digester itself, and emissions from **digestate**, the wet material left over once digestion is finished.

## 6. Curbing methane from rice

Several approaches can lower methane from rice farming. High-yielding rice varieties can greatly reduce the methane released per kilogram of rice. Among the thousands of varieties grown worldwide, some naturally emit less methane than others, and finding and growing these [could reduce emissions by 22 to 51%](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33387767/). One promising variety, **SUSIBA 2**, is already grown in some parts of the world. A [2025 study](https://www.cell.com/molecular-plant/abstract/S1674-2052(25)00029-2) found out why it emits less: its roots produce chemicals that suppress methane. That knowledge could help breed new varieties that emit up to 70% less methane.

A simpler and cheaper option is **Alternate Wetting and Drying (AWD)**. Instead of keeping fields flooded all the time, farmers let the water dry out completely before flooding the field again. There are several versions of this practice, and they can [cut methane emissions by 40 to 45%](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168192324001904) while also saving water, as long as the farm has reliable drainage and water supply.

## Ireland case study: additives and digesters for slurry
<!-- tag: ireland -->

**Glasport Bio**, a company based in Galway, has developed GasAbate, an additive that can cut greenhouse gas emissions from manure by more than 80% without harming the animals, as [reported by RTE in March 2025](https://www.rte.ie/news/environment/2025/0311/1501355-farms-environment/). The liquid is added to slurry tanks, where it raises the oxygen level and stops the anaerobic microbes that produce methane, nitrous oxide, hydrogen sulphide and ammonia. According to McCarthy of Glasport Bio, the costs compare well with other ways of cutting emissions. The Climate Change Advisory Council estimated that spending €3 billion to get 700,000 people into electric cars would cut around 4 million tonnes of emissions, roughly **€750 per tonne** of carbon. Rolling GasAbate out across all Irish farms would cost an estimated €75 per tonne. About 11% of Irish agricultural emissions come directly from manure. Treating all cattle and pig slurry with GasAbate could cut Ireland's agricultural emissions by 1.8 million tonnes a year, roughly 30% of the total cut Ireland needs from farming.

Anaerobic digesters offer a second route. In the US, digesters that turn manure into biogas do reduce methane from livestock, but as [New Scientist has reported](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2522081-are-manure-digesters-a-real-solution-to-dairy-farm-emissions/), the financial incentives around them have also encouraged factory farms to grow bigger. Manure that has been through a digester [emits 91% less methane](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40572-025-00512-8) while it is stored. The process can also replace fossil gas or diesel, produce digestate to use as fertiliser, and give farms an extra source of income.

In Ireland, the Environmental Protection Agency's 2023 breakdown shows that enteric fermentation (methane from cattle and sheep digestion) makes up **63.1%** of agricultural emissions, while manure management makes up 11.9%. So methane from the animals themselves is roughly five times larger than emissions linked to manure, reflecting the country's large dairy and beef herd. Teagasc, Ireland's farm advisory body, notes that 90 to 95% of methane from ruminant livestock is enteric, with the rest coming from manure. The difficulty for Ireland is that cattle slurry here is wet and fairly low in energy, so digesting slurry on its own often does not pay. Teagasc recommends co-digestion, mixing slurry with grass silage, food waste or other residues, to get more gas and make digesters viable.
