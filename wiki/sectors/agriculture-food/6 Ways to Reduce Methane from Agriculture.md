# 6 Ways to Reduce Methane from Agriculture

Nearly half of global methane emissions come from agricultural activities such as livestock production, rice cultivation and the burning of crop waste, according to the [Climate and Clean Air Coalition's 2021 Global Methane Assessment](https://www.ccacoalition.org/sites/default/files/resources/2021_Global-Methane_Assessment_full_0.pdf). Because methane is a much more potent, but much shorter-lived, greenhouse gas than CO2, cutting it is one of the fastest ways to slow near-term warming, and a growing set of farming solutions are emerging to do just that.

## Where agricultural methane comes from

Within agriculture, methane emissions break down roughly as follows:

- **Ruminants** (cattle, sheep and goats) account for 55 to 60% of agricultural methane. Microorganisms in their stomachs break down food through a process called **enteric fermentation**, generating methane that is released mainly through burping.
- **Manure** produces a further 6 to 8% of agricultural methane, though some research suggests this could be significantly higher. In large dairy and pig facilities, manure is often flushed out with water and stored as liquid slurry in tanks or lagoons, an anaerobic (oxygen-free) environment that favours methane-producing microbes.
- **Rice farming** contributes 18 to 20% of agricultural methane. Flooded rice fields deplete oxygen in the soil, which fuels methane production; the gas escapes mainly through the rice plants themselves or as bubbles rising from the soil, with emissions varying depending on irrigation method, soil type and fertiliser use.
- **Burning crop residues**, leftover stalks and biomass from harvests such as rice and wheat, is common practice in some countries and contributes 3 to 4% of agricultural methane, while also releasing air pollution that endangers human health.

Six practical approaches are helping to bring these emissions down.

## 1. Making livestock more efficient

In many developing countries, livestock produce far less milk and meat per animal than farms in places like the United States, which creates a large opportunity to improve efficiency through better diets, breeding and animal health. Feeding animals more digestible feed helps them burp less while absorbing more nutrients for growth and milk production: in the US, [milk production grew by 53%](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2022/march/u-s-dairy-productivity-increased-faster-in-large-farms-and-across-southwestern-states) between 1990 and 2021, while enteric methane emissions per unit of milk fell by around 25% over the same period. Many farms in developed countries already use highly digestible feeds, but farmers in Africa and South Asia often feed their animals low-quality crop residues, such as rice and wheat straw, which can make up as much as 70% of a cow's diet.

## 2. Stopping methane before it escapes: feed additives

Methane-inhibiting feed additives reduce the amount of methane cows produce during digestion. They suit commercial and large-scale farms best, since cost is a barrier for smaller operations and the additives do not always deliver productivity gains. A synthetic molecule called **3-nitroxypropanol (3-NOP)** has drawn particular interest, having gone through extensive research and development trials and secured regulatory approval for use in beef or dairy cows in more than 60 countries. Given in small doses through feed, it can [reduce methane production by around 30%](https://doi.org/10.3168/jds.2022-22211). Red seaweed (*Asparagopsis*), native to Australia and already approved for use in Australian beef feedlots, is another additive that has attracted considerable market interest. Laboratory research suggests its methane mitigation potential can reach up to 90%, but long-term trials have shown [closer to 30%](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022030224014309) effectiveness in practice.

## 3. Breeding low-methane cows

Methane production is partly linked to genetics, with some cows inherently producing more than others. Breeding and selecting low-methane cows, animals that naturally emit less, is now possible alongside the long-standing practice of selecting for healthier, more feed-efficient breeds. On average, low-methane cows [produce 22% less methane](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022030223004022) than high-emitting ones. Unlike feed additives, this mitigation lasts the entire lifespan of the animal and can even be passed down to its offspring.

## 4. Anti-methane vaccines

An early-stage but promising line of research involves vaccines that prevent methane formation in cows' stomachs. These would be administered to calves like any other vaccine, with effects lasting through the animal's life. Some R&D efforts, including a project backed by the [Bezos Earth Fund](https://www.bezosearthfund.org/news-and-insights/pioneering-cattle-methane-vaccine-project-launched-by-bezos-earth-fund), are currently targeting a 30% reduction in methane through this route.

## 5. Manure digesters, and their limits

Biogas digesters use manure to produce biogas, a mixture of methane and carbon dioxide, capturing the methane to generate electricity or to be compressed into "renewable natural gas." While digesters can reduce methane emissions, their benefits have often been overestimated: reporting frequently fails to account for methane leaking from the digester itself, or for emissions from **digestate**, the wet material left over once digestion is complete.

## 6. Curbing methane from rice

Several approaches can lower methane from rice farming. Adopting high-yielding rice varieties can significantly reduce methane emitted per kilogram of rice produced, and among the thousands of rice varieties grown worldwide, some naturally emit less methane than others: identifying and cultivating these [could reduce emissions by 22 to 51%](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33387767/). One promising variety, **SUSIBA 2**, is already grown in some parts of the world. A [2025 study](https://www.cell.com/molecular-plant/abstract/S1674-2052(25)00029-2) uncovered the biochemical mechanism behind its lower emissions, methane-suppressing chemicals produced in its root system, an insight that could help breed further varieties emitting up to 70% less methane. A simpler and cheaper option is **Alternate Wetting and Drying (AWD)**: rather than keeping fields permanently flooded, the water is allowed to dry out completely before the field is reflooded. Several variations of this practice exist and can [cut methane emissions by 40 to 45%](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168192324001904) while also saving water, though it depends on having reliable drainage and water supply.

## Ireland case study: additives and digesters for slurry

A Galway-based company, **Glasport Bio**, has developed **GasAbate**, a non-invasive additive capable of cutting greenhouse gas emissions from manure by more than 80%, as [reported by RTE in March 2025](https://www.rte.ie/news/environment/2025/0311/1501355-farms-environment/). The liquid is added to slurry tanks, raising the oxygen level in the slurry pits and preventing the anaerobic microbes that produce methane, nitrous oxide, hydrogen sulphide and ammonia from working. According to Glasport Bio's McCarthy, the economics compare favourably with other abatement routes: the Climate Change Advisory Council estimated that paying €3 billion to get 700,000 people into electric cars would cut around 4 million tonnes of emissions, working out at roughly €750 per tonne of carbon abated. Rolling GasAbate out across all Irish farms, by contrast, would cost an estimated €75 per tonne. About 11% of Irish agricultural emissions come directly from manure, and treating all livestock slurry from cattle and pigs with GasAbate could cut Ireland's agricultural emissions by 1.8 million tonnes a year, roughly 30% of the overall reduction Ireland needs from agriculture.

Anaerobic digesters offer a complementary route. In the US, digesters that convert manure into biogas do reduce methane emissions from livestock, but as [New Scientist has reported](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2522081-are-manure-digesters-a-real-solution-to-dairy-farm-emissions/), the financial incentives built around them have also encouraged factory farms to grow larger. Manure that has gone through a digester [emits 91% less methane](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40572-025-00512-8) during storage, and the process can also replace fossil gas or diesel, produce digestate for use as fertiliser, and create an extra income stream for farms. In Ireland, the Environmental Protection Agency's 2023 breakdown of agricultural emissions shows that enteric fermentation (methane from cattle and sheep digestion) accounts for 63.1% of agricultural emissions, while manure management accounts for 11.9%, meaning methane from the animals themselves is roughly five times larger than manure-related emissions, reflecting the country's large dairy and beef herd. Teagasc notes that 90 to 95% of livestock methane linked to ruminants is enteric methane, with the rest coming from manure systems. The catch for Ireland is that cattle slurry here is wet and relatively low-energy, so digesting slurry alone often gives poor economics; Teagasc recommends **co-digestion**, mixing slurry with grass silage, food waste or other residues, to improve gas yield and viability.
