# FACTS about Farming and Food Production

## What is it

Farming and the wider food system, once every stage is counted, are responsible for around a third of total global greenhouse gas emissions. Meat production accounts for a large share of that, driven by the land required to raise livestock, the resources needed to grow their feed, the gases produced by the animals themselves, and the wider toll all of this takes on soil health. Climate change is already affecting yields, particularly for wheat, and is also reshaping where crops can be grown at all: a [Guardian investigation into Europe's climate-driven drought crisis](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2022/aug/08/the-new-normal-how-europe-is-being-hit-by-a-climate-driven-drought-crisis) documented how coffee farmers are being pushed to ever-higher altitudes to keep their crops viable.

## The GHG footprint of the food supply chain

Agriculture has a major environmental impact along three main dimensions, according to [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/environmental-impacts-of-food). It requires large amounts of fresh water, creating significant pressure in water-stressed regions, both as an input and through the nutrient pollution it releases into rivers, lakes and oceans. It is a major driver of climate change, responsible for around a quarter of global greenhouse gas emissions. And it has an enormous footprint simply through land use: half of the world's habitable land (land that is not ice or desert) is used for agriculture.

Several further data points illustrate the scale of this impact:

- Food production accounts for [26% of global greenhouse gas emissions](https://ourworldindata.org/food-ghg-emissions).
- [70% of global freshwater withdrawals](https://ourworldindata.org/environmental-impacts-of-food) are used for agriculture.
- [78% of global ocean and freshwater eutrophication](https://ourworldindata.org/environmental-impacts-of-food), the pollution of waterways with excess nutrients, is caused by agriculture.
- [94% of non-human mammal biomass](https://ourworldindata.org/environmental-impacts-of-food) on Earth is livestock, meaning livestock outweigh wild mammals by a factor of 15 to 1; that share rises to 97% when only land-based mammals are counted.
- [71% of bird biomass](https://ourworldindata.org/environmental-impacts-of-food) is poultry livestock, meaning farmed poultry outweigh wild birds by more than 3 to 1.

## Most of what we grow feeds animals, not people

According to [National Geographic's analysis of feeding nine billion people](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/foodfeatures/feeding-9-billion/), only 55% of the world's crop calories feed people directly today. The rest are fed to livestock (about 36%) or turned into biofuels and industrial products (roughly 9%). The conversion from feed to food is strikingly inefficient: for every 100 calories of grain fed to animals, we get back only around 40 calories of milk, 22 of eggs, 12 of chicken, 10 of pork, or just 3 of beef, by far the lowest return of any major animal product. Feeding nine billion people would be considerably easier if more of the crop calories grown went directly into human diets. Because people in developing countries are unlikely to eat less meat in the near term, given rising prosperity, the more immediate opportunity lies in countries that already have meat-rich diets: shifting from grain-fed beef toward chicken, pork or pasture-raised beef, growing meat more efficiently, and curbing the use of food crops for biofuels could all free up substantial food supply. ## We need to cut food waste

An estimated 25% of the world's food calories, and up to 50% of total food weight, are lost or wasted before they can ever be eaten. In rich countries, most of that waste happens in homes, restaurants and supermarkets; in poorer countries, food is more often lost between the farm and the market, due to unreliable storage and transport infrastructure. In wealthier countries, relatively simple steps, serving smaller portions, eating leftovers, and encouraging restaurants and supermarkets to adopt waste-reducing practices, could make a meaningful difference. Of all the options for boosting global food availability, tackling waste is one of the most effective. ## What about fish farming?

Fish farming is often assumed to be the more sustainable alternative to wild catch, but the evidence is mixed. Research led by scientist Jennifer Hayek and colleagues, [reported by New Scientist](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2452261-why-farming-fish-is-more-unsustainable-than-catching-them-in-the-wild/), found that the amount of wild fish killed to feed farmed fish is between 27% and 307% higher than previous estimates suggested. It takes roughly 4 to 5kg of wild-caught fish to produce just 1kg of farmed salmon, because farmed carnivorous fish eat several times more fish, by weight, than they ultimately provide as food. As the aquaculture industry expands, a growing share of the global wild fish catch is being diverted to feed farmed fish rather than people, a serious problem given that wild fish populations are already declining, and one that also raises the price of fish for people in south-east Asia and other poorer regions who depend on it directly for food.

Switching to plant-based alternatives is not a straightforward fix either: it would require more land to grow the substitute crops, risking further deforestation. As Hayek put it, "you can't get away from impacts somewhere as long as you're eating animals. Farming animals requires more resources to raise and grow their bodies than you can get from eating them. That's a basic fact of biology."
