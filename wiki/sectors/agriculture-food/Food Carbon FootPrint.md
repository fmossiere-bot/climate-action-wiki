# Food Carbon Footprint: Does Food Mileage Really Matter?

## What is it

Food transport is a complicated topic. Different studies have given quite different answers to a simple-sounding question: how much does "food mileage", the distance food travels before it reaches your plate, add to the carbon footprint of what we eat?

## Is it 4%, or 20%?

One thorough piece of research found that food miles make up only around 4% of the carbon footprint of food. Food itself is estimated at 20 to 30% of a person's overall footprint, so food miles work out at roughly 0.8% of the average footprint of someone in the US. The same research warns that food mileage figures can mislead, because so much depends on how the food travels and what it is. Air freight produces around 75 times more emissions than sea freight. And one UK study found that tomatoes grown abroad and brought to the UK by lorry produced only a third of the emissions of tomatoes grown locally in a heated glasshouse.

A separate and much larger study came up with a very different number. According to research covered by the [European Commission](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/news/field-fork-global-food-miles-generate-nearly-20-all-co2-emissions-food-2023-01-25_en), "field to fork" transport could account for as much as 20% of all CO2 emissions from food. Today, international trade provides 19% of all the calories eaten worldwide. Food miles are measured in tonne-kilometres (tkm), meaning one tonne of food moved one kilometre. Measuring this properly across the whole world is hard, because it needs huge amounts of data covering every step of the supply chain. That is why this study used a global model covering many regions rather than simpler estimates.

The researchers worked out food-miles emissions with this model, taking into account distance, the weight of the food, how it travelled and the emissions linked to each type of transport. They then added production and land-use-change emissions to get the total for the food system. Across the whole food chain, food miles came to 3 gigatonnes of CO2-equivalent, more than earlier estimates. Transport alone made up 20% of food-system emissions. Moving fruit and vegetables accounted for 36% of all food-miles emissions, around twice the emissions released while growing them. Total global food-system emissions came to 15.8 gigatonnes of CO2-equivalent, roughly 30% of all greenhouse gas emissions worldwide. The study's main finding is that moving fruit and vegetables around releases about twice the emissions of producing them in the first place.

## Does food mileage even matter compared to what you eat?

Another way to answer the question is to look at where emissions actually come from, food by food. [Our World in Data's analysis](https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local) draws on the largest review of global food systems to date, published in *Science* by Joseph Poore and Thomas Nemecek in 2018, which used data from more than 38,000 commercial farms in 119 countries. It compares the total greenhouse gas emissions per kilogram of 29 different foods, from beef at the top to nuts at the bottom, and shows at which stage of the supply chain those emissions arise, from land-use change through to transport and packaging.

The gap between foods is enormous. Producing a kilogram of beef emits around 60kg of CO2-equivalent, compared with just 1kg for peas. Animal foods generally have a far bigger footprint than plant foods. Lamb and cheese both emit more than 20kg of CO2-equivalent per kilogram. Poultry (6kg) and pork (7kg) are lower, but still higher than most plant foods.

For most foods, and especially the biggest emitters, most emissions come from land-use change (such as clearing forest for farmland) and from the farm itself, rather than from transport. Farm emissions include fertilisers, both synthetic and organic (from manure), and enteric fermentation, the methane produced in the stomachs of cattle as they digest. Together, land use and farm emissions make up more than 80% of the footprint of most foods. Transport is a small part: for most foods it is less than 10% of emissions, and for the biggest emitters it is smaller still. For beef from beef herds it is just 0.5%. Everything that happens after food leaves the farm (processing, transport, shops and packaging combined) is mostly a small share of the total.

The same pattern holds for whole diets. Researchers Vilma Sandström and colleagues [studied the footprint of diets across the EU](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211912418300361) and found that transport made up only 6% of food emissions, while dairy, meat and eggs together made up 83%. In other words, what you eat matters far more than how far it has travelled. The exception is a smaller group of highly perishable foods flown in by air, where the way they travel can still make a real difference.
