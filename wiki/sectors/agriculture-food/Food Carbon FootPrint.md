# Food Carbon Footprint: Does Food Mileage Really Matter?

## What is it

Food transport is a genuinely complicated topic, and different research has produced quite different answers to a simple-sounding question: how much does "food mileage", the distance food travels before it reaches your plate, actually contribute to food's carbon footprint?

## Is it 4%, or 20%?

One comprehensive piece of research found that food miles account for only around 4% of the total carbon footprint of food (itself estimated at 20 to 30% of an individual's overall footprint), working out to roughly 0.8% of the average footprint of someone in the US. That research also cautions that food mileage figures can be misleading, since so much depends on the mode of transport, air freight is around 75 times more emissions-heavy than sea freight, and on the specific crop involved: one UK study found that tomatoes grown abroad and trucked to the UK produced only a third of the emissions of tomatoes grown locally in a heated glasshouse.

A separate and much larger study reaches a different headline number. According to research covered by the [European Commission](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/news/field-fork-global-food-miles-generate-nearly-20-all-co2-emissions-food-2023-01-25_en), "field to fork" transport could account for as much as 20% of the total CO2 emissions from food. Today, international trade makes up 19% of all the calories consumed worldwide. Food miles refer to the distance food travels from production to consumption, and are measured in tonne-kilometres (tkm), the transport of one tonne of food over one kilometre. Assessing this properly at a global scale is difficult, since it requires enormous amounts of data covering the entire supply chain, which is why this study relied on a global multi-region model rather than simpler estimates.

The researchers calculated food-miles emissions using this global model, incorporating transport distance, food mass, transport type and the relevant emissions factor for each, and separately calculated total food-system emissions, combining food-miles emissions with production and land-use-change emissions. Their findings were striking: across the entire food chain, food miles were found to generate 3 gigatonnes of CO2-equivalent emissions, more than previously thought. Transport alone accounted for 20% of food-system emissions, and for fruit and vegetables specifically the figure was 36%, around twice the emissions released during their production. Total global food-system emissions came to 15.8 gigatonnes of CO2-equivalent, roughly 30% of all global greenhouse gas emissions. The key finding from this study is that transporting fruit and vegetables releases about twice the emissions generated by producing them in the first place.

## Does food mileage even matter compared to what you eat?

A different way to answer the question is to look at where emissions actually come from across the full range of foods. [Our World in Data's analysis](https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local), drawing on the largest meta-analysis of global food systems to date, published in *Science* by Joseph Poore and Thomas Nemecek in 2018 using data from more than 38,000 commercial farms across 119 countries, compares the total greenhouse gas emissions per kilogram of 29 different food products, from beef at the top to nuts at the bottom, and breaks down where in the supply chain those emissions originate, from land-use change through to transport and packaging.

The gap between foods is enormous: producing a kilogram of beef emits around 60kg of CO2-equivalent, compared with just 1kg for peas. Animal-based foods generally carry a far higher footprint than plant-based ones: lamb and cheese both emit more than 20kg of CO2-equivalent per kilogram, while poultry (6kg) and pork (7kg) have lower footprints that are nonetheless still higher than most plant-based foods.

For most foods, and especially the largest emitters, the bulk of emissions come not from transport but from land-use change and from processes at the farm stage itself, including the application of fertilisers (both synthetic and organic, via manure management) and enteric fermentation, the methane produced in the stomachs of cattle. Combined, land use and farm-stage emissions account for more than 80% of the footprint of most foods. Transport, by contrast, is a small contributor: for most food products it accounts for less than 10% of emissions, and the share shrinks further for the largest emitters, in beef from beef herds it is just 0.5%. Everything that happens after food leaves the farm, processing, transport, retail and packaging combined, mostly accounts for a small share of the total footprint.

This pattern holds not just for individual products but for whole diets. Researchers Vilma Sandström and colleagues [studied the footprint of diets across the EU](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211912418300361) and found that food transport accounted for only 6% of emissions, while dairy, meat and eggs together accounted for 83%. What you eat, in other words, matters far more to your food footprint than how far it travelled to reach you, though for a smaller category of highly perishable, air-freighted produce, transport mode can still make a meaningful difference.
