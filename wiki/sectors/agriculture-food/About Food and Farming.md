# About Food and Farming: Challenges, Regenerative Agriculture and Decarbonisation Levers

## What is it

The global food system, once every stage is counted, from fertiliser and chemical production, through land conversion for crops and livestock, to processing, transport, retail, consumption and waste disposal, is responsible for roughly a third of total global greenhouse gas emissions. That scale means fixing farming and food is one of the largest levers available for climate action, but the system is also, by most accounts, broken. Government subsidies help large agribusinesses extract profits at the expense of the environment, the climate and public health, while the people who grow the world's food often cannot make a living from it.

The fragility of the current model has been exposed repeatedly in recent years. Sri Lanka provided a stark, unplanned experiment in 2021, when the president announced an overnight ban on all chemical fertiliser imports, forcing an abrupt, chaotic transition to organic farming across the country. The COVID-19 pandemic then prompted researchers to call for renewed investment in local food supply chains, through supporting community-based farming, defending small producers, building narratives around the value of local food, encouraging subsistence gardening and foraging, and promoting local ecological and gastronomic knowledge. Since then, the war in Ukraine has added further shocks, disrupting the predictability, reliability, affordability and sustainability of global food production. Today's debate centres on how to switch agricultural production toward more organic, climate-friendly practices while absorbing these repeated disruptions.

## Four systemic challenges

Beyond emissions, the global food system faces four deep, interlinked problems.

**Farm and food workers are exploited.** Labour conditions across the system are often inhumane, including illegal labour, and the workers involved are frequently among the most vulnerable people in the supply chain, including migrant workers. Because so much farm labour depends on immigration, any disruption to migration flows can ripple through production.

**Healthy food is distributed inequitably.** Fruit and vegetables grown in a healthy environment tend to cost more and are often accessible mainly to wealthier consumers. Because this makes them less accessible to low-income households, [poverty is closely linked to higher rates of chronic, diet-related disease](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/malnutrition), according to the World Health Organization.

**The damage goes well beyond climate.** Food and farming also drive biodiversity loss, habitat destruction, soil erosion, pollinator collapse, and freshwater and air pollution.

**Farmers struggle to make a living.** This is not just a small-farm problem: according to the [USDA's Economic Research Service](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2021/september/off-farm-income-a-major-component-of-total-income-for-most-farm-households-in-2019/), 96% of farmers in the United States rely on off-farm income to survive financially, and for some of those households, off-farm income makes up 82% of total revenue. Farm policy, many researchers argue, needs to shift toward valuing the ecosystem services farmers provide and enabling more diversified revenue streams, rather than relying on production subsidies alone.

## Charting a path forward

Several thinkers have proposed frameworks for fixing the system. Ecologist Jonathan Foley has laid out a [five-step plan to feed the world](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/foodfeatures/feeding-9-billion/): freeze agriculture's footprint, grow more food on the farms we already have, use resources more efficiently, shift diets, and reduce waste. Researcher Raj Patel has offered a [critique of the "feeding the world" framing](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/agroecology-is-the-solution-to-world-hunger/) itself, arguing the priority should be feeding communities rather than pursuing abstract global production targets. The Global Alliance for the Future of Food has set out a [vision for food](https://story.futureoffood.org/the-politics-of-knowledge/) grounded in agroecology, regenerative approaches and indigenous foodways. Research published in *Nature* has shown that [dietary change in high-income nations alone could deliver a substantial "double climate dividend"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-021-00431-5), and journalist George Monbiot's 2022 book *Regenesis: Feeding the World Without Devouring the Planet* argues for the chance to transform not just the food system but humanity's entire relationship with the living world. A recurring theme across this work is a reminder that, for food production, "does it scale?" is [often the wrong question](https://ensia.com/voices/food-production-regenerative-agriculture-scale/) to ask.

## Regenerative agriculture and the five principles of soil health

**Regenerative agriculture** has many definitions, but one widely cited formulation, developed through academic research, describes it as an approach to farming that uses soil conservation as the entry point to regenerate multiple ecosystem services, with the aim of enhancing the environmental, social and economic dimensions of food production together. It is also sometimes referred to as carbon farming or agroecology.

Soil is the largest land-based reservoir of carbon, holding three to four times more carbon than the atmosphere. Human activities, clearing forests, converting native ecosystems to farmland, and decades of poor soil management, have created a carbon deficit in soils, turning them into a net carbon source rather than a sink. Put another way, this represents an opportunity: restoring "lost" carbon to the soil by drawing it down from the atmosphere through photosynthesis. One striking calculation, behind the **4 per mille initiative** launched in 2015 as a call to action for farmers and communities, found that increasing soil carbon sequestration globally by just 0.4% a year could offset all fossil fuel emissions. Practices that enhance this carbon uptake include cover cropping, compost application, biochar, crop rotation and wetland restoration.

Soil health rests on five core principles:

1. **Keep the soil covered.** Exposed soil is more vulnerable to winter and water erosion, evaporates moisture faster, experiences greater temperature swings, and compacts more easily.
2. **Minimise disturbance.** Biological, physical or chemical disturbance reduces the pore spaces in soil, restricts water infiltration, destroys the interlinkages that support soil life, and kills that life outright. The consequences include water erosion, wind erosion and associated air pollution, waterlogging from subsurface compaction, soil crusting that restricts plant emergence, and depletion of soil organic matter.
3. **Maximise diversity, above and below ground.** Diversified cropping systems reduce soil-borne disease and increase both water and nutrient use efficiency, along with the abundance of functional soil microorganisms.
4. **Keep living roots in the ground.** Continuous living roots, maintained through cover crops planted between harvests, ensure a steady supply of carbon into the soil via the root system.
5. **Integrate animals.** Animals, plants and soil evolved together over geological time, but confinement farming has steadily reduced the role animals play on most farms. Integrating animals does not just mean grazing cattle: it can include pollinators, birds and other wild species that control pests, for example installing owl boxes or perches for birds of prey to manage rodent populations. Grazing livestock carries several distinct benefits: fall or winter grazing converts high-carbon crop residue into low-carbon organic material, balancing the soil's carbon-to-nitrogen ratio and preparing it for no-till seeding; spring or summer grazing, in short bursts followed by long recovery periods, spurs plants to regrow and capture more sunlight and CO2; grazing reduces nutrient export from cropland by letting livestock recycle nutrients, minerals and carbon in place rather than trucking feed to a feedlot; it can substitute for herbicide in managing weeds; grazing cover crops or crop residues lets livestock off perennial grassland earlier in the season, extending its recovery period while improving the animals' diet; and it reduces the water-quality and waste problems associated with confinement.

## Can farmers be paid to store carbon?

Soil carbon can be measured, but it is difficult: results vary considerably with land type, measurement depth and season, and the carbon offset systems built around paying farmers for storage remain heavily debated. The core problem is one of "additionality": under most current offset schemes, large corporations pay for carbon offsetting rather than the state doing so through subsidies, and it is often genuinely unclear whether the carbon being purchased would have been sequestered on that land anyway, without payment. Numerous competing protocols exist, with little consistency or rigour between them.

An alternative gaining ground is to compensate farmers more broadly for the ecosystem services they deliver, sometimes called whole-farm revenue support, or to incentivise crop diversification and cover cropping directly, without tying payment to specific claimed tonnes of sequestered carbon. Different countries and NGOs are experimenting with different versions of this approach. Whatever the mechanism, the underlying practices, cover cropping, composting, rotational grazing and agroforestry, deliver benefits well beyond carbon: improved soil water-holding capacity, better crop quality, greater resilience to drought, extreme rainfall and heatwaves, improved pest management, and higher, more diversified farmer income that reduces exposure to monocrop market risk. Several tools now support this kind of measurement and planning, including the Soil Inventory Project (launched by former US Vice President Al Gore and researcher Dr Kris Covey), COMET-Farm (which estimates the greenhouse gas impact of adopting specific practices on a given acreage), the Cool Farm Tool (which also measures biodiversity and water use), and WRI's Land & Carbon Lab, a global initiative tracking land cover change and associated carbon stocks and flows.

## Eight levers for decarbonising the food system

**Chemical fertilisers, pesticides and herbicides.** Manufacturing these products is carbon-intensive, and applying them to fields routinely leads to excess nitrogen in the environment. [Only about half](https://eos.org/articles/index-suggests-that-half-of-nitrogen-applied-to-crops-is-lost) of the nitrogen fertiliser applied worldwide, most of it manufactured from fossil fuels, actually ends up fertilising crops; the rest escapes into rivers, aquifers and streams, has created a dead zone in the Gulf of Mexico roughly the size of Rhode Island and Delaware combined, and releases nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas around 265 to 300 times more potent than CO2. There is a clear opportunity here to cut use, decarbonise fertiliser production, and expand integrated pest management.

**Controlled-environment and vertical farming.** Growing crops indoors, in fully controlled environments, is often framed as a vision for the future of some crop categories. The Netherlands has become the best-known example, having built a reputation for hyper-efficient production using this model.

**Precision fermentation and alternative proteins.** Precision fermentation is a refined form of brewing, multiplying microbes to create specific products; it has long been used to make drugs and food additives, and is now being applied to develop new staple foods. Reducing red meat consumption has a real climate benefit, though simply substituting heavily fertilised soy is not equivalent to genuine regenerative farming. A large body of research finds that [vegetarians have a smaller environmental footprint, and vegans a smaller one still](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959652622043542), and that replacing animal-based ingredients is generally more effective at cutting emissions than simply sourcing ingredients locally. This has helped fuel a growing market for meat alternatives, although [that market cooled somewhat in 2022](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2022/11/12/plant-based-meat-market/). Researcher Hannah Ritchie has shown that [meat substitutes tend to have both lower emissions and a smaller land footprint](https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/carbon-footprint-meat-substitutes) than the meat they replace, though it is also worth weighing [who those animals feed, and where, from a justice perspective](https://www.aljazeera.com/opinions/2022/11/10/livestock-is-a-form-of-climate-justice-in-the-global-south), since livestock plays a different role in food security in parts of the Global South.

**Regional processing facilities.** Large-scale food processing has become increasingly concentrated and geographically distant from where food is grown, generating emissions from transport, refrigeration, cleaning and the bulk processing required to meet food safety standards.

**Transport and refrigeration.** Refrigeration and refrigerated transport account for a large share of the [carbon footprint](https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local) of some food items, partly because the refrigerants themselves are potent greenhouse gases. Eating more local, fresh food, combined with developing less harmful alternative refrigerants, could meaningfully cut these emissions.

**Shifting consumption.** Dietary change, at the individual, household or institutional level, holds substantial potential for decarbonising the food system (see [Food Carbon Footprint](Food%20Carbon%20FootPrint.md) for more on how different foods compare). One study found that [eating a plant-based diet](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/aa7541) can cut an individual's emissions by around 0.8 tonnes of CO2 a year. As a general rule, excessively meat-heavy diets need to shift in developed countries to relieve pressure on vulnerable landscapes such as the Brazilian Amazon, where deforestation driven partly by meat demand and cattle grazing continues at an alarming rate. In this sense, fixing food systems and fixing deforestation are closely linked problems.

**Eating from the sea.** Regenerative ocean farming, in particular seaweed cultivation, is gaining attention as an alternative protein source. Seaweed is high in antioxidants and protein and can be grown alongside oysters, mussels and scallops, all of which are far more sustainable than eating higher up the marine food chain, for example tuna, swordfish or halibut.

**Reducing food waste.** If food waste were a country, it would rank as the third-largest emitter in the world. Between [30 and 40%](https://www.fao.org/in-action/seeking-end-to-loss-and-waste-of-food-along-production-chain/en/) of total food production is lost or wasted before it reaches the market, according to the UN Food and Agriculture Organization, with causes ranging from farm-gate losses to waste at the household and food-service level, as tracked by [UNEP](https://www.unep.org/thinkeatsave/get-informed/worldwide-food-waste) and the [US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sustainable-management-food/wasted-food-scale). See [FACTS About Food Waste](../../circularity-waste/FACTS%20About%20Food%20Waste.md) for more detail.

## Food waste in practice: what is working

Food waste occurs at every stage, from the farm to retail to the household, and a number of initiatives are tackling it directly. South Korea has virtually eliminated food waste through a compulsory curbside composting programme. In Geneva, the nonprofit Free-Go places public fridges on the street where members of the public can share food from their homes or commercial kitchens that would otherwise go to waste. In the US, for-profit companies such as Imperfect Foods and Ugly Food sell "ugly" but perfectly good produce that retailers would otherwise reject. In Canada, nonprofits including Food Stash (Vancouver), Rescue Food (the Prairies) and Second Harvest (nationwide) collect and redistribute food that would otherwise go to landfill. Composting services such as Turn Compost (Dallas-Fort Worth) let households compost without doing it themselves, while the app Too Good To Go matches cost- and climate-conscious consumers with restaurants offering surplus food at a discount. In the UK, local "gleaning" groups connect volunteers with farmers to harvest excess fruit and vegetables that would otherwise be left in the field.

## Diversifying farms and rethinking meat: further reflections

Diversification reduces a farm's exposure to any single point of failure, whether that is weather, pests or market price. More farmers are practising agroecology than is commonly assumed, though the total land area involved remains small next to large conventional farms. A recurring question for anyone considering land management is what the native ecosystem for that land actually was, was it forest before it became farmland, for instance, and what restoring some of that might mean. In the EU, the Climate Farm Academy helps farmers navigate this kind of transition. In the US, some dairy farms have shifted away from dairy infrastructure toward non-animal alternatives, a transition that often starts from a crop failure or other shock and then expands gradually through practices like cover cropping and no-till farming.

Cultured (lab-grown) meat is one response to the scale of current meat production, in which roughly 95% of farmed animals are raised in factory farm conditions. The consensus among researchers working in this space is that cultured meat should not be seen as a single replacement for all meat production, but as one part of a more sustainable protein mix that coexists with other approaches; it also remains an energy-intensive production process in its own right. Vertical farming shows similar nuance: it makes sense for some crops, such as lettuce, but others, like carrots, still need to grow in open ground, and the economics of vertical farming remain a significant challenge to viability at scale. On the food waste side, start-ups including Too Good To Go, Mill and Bokashi Living are among those building businesses around reducing what gets thrown away.
