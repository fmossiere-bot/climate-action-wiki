# About Food and Farming: Challenges, Regenerative Agriculture and Decarbonisation Levers

## What is it

The global food system covers every stage of getting food onto our plates: making fertilisers and chemicals, clearing land for crops and livestock, processing, transport, shops, eating and throwing away. Counted together, it is responsible for roughly a third of global greenhouse gas emissions. That makes food and farming one of the largest levers we have for climate action. It is also a system that many experts describe as broken. Government subsidies help large agribusinesses make profits at the expense of the environment, the climate and public health, while the people who grow the world's food often cannot make a living from it.

Recent years have shown how fragile the current model is. In 2021 Sri Lanka's president announced an overnight ban on all chemical fertiliser imports, which forced an abrupt and chaotic switch to organic farming across the country. It became an unplanned experiment in what a sudden transition looks like. The COVID-19 pandemic then led researchers to call for more investment in local food supply chains: supporting community-based farming, defending small producers, telling the story of why local food matters, encouraging people to grow and forage their own food, and keeping local knowledge about ecology and cooking alive. The war in Ukraine added further shocks, making global food production less predictable, reliable, affordable and sustainable. The debate today is about how to move farming towards more organic, climate-friendly practices while coping with these repeated disruptions.

## Four systemic challenges

Emissions are only part of the picture. The global food system also faces four deep problems that feed into one another.

**Exploited workers.** Working conditions across the system are often inhumane, and some of the labour is illegal. The people doing this work, many of them migrants, are frequently among the most vulnerable in the whole supply chain. Because so much farm work depends on immigration, any disruption to migration can ripple through food production.

**Unequal access to healthy food.** Fruit and vegetables grown in a healthy environment tend to cost more, so they are often bought mainly by wealthier households. Low-income families get less of them, and the World Health Organization notes that [poverty is closely linked to higher rates of chronic, diet-related disease](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/malnutrition).

**Damage beyond the climate.** Food and farming also drive biodiversity loss, habitat destruction, soil erosion, the collapse of pollinators, and pollution of fresh water and air.

**Farmers who cannot make a living.** This affects more than small farms. According to the [USDA's Economic Research Service](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2021/september/off-farm-income-a-major-component-of-total-income-for-most-farm-households-in-2019/), 96% of farmers in the United States rely on income from outside the farm to get by, and for some of those households that outside income makes up 82% of the total. Many researchers argue that farm policy should pay farmers for the ecosystem services they provide, such as clean water and healthy soil, and help them build more varied sources of income, rather than relying on production subsidies alone.

## Charting a path forward

Several thinkers have proposed ways to fix the system. Ecologist Jonathan Foley has set out a [five-step plan to feed the world](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/foodfeatures/feeding-9-billion/): stop agriculture's footprint from growing, grow more food on the farms we already have, use resources more efficiently, shift diets, and reduce waste. Researcher Raj Patel [questions the "feeding the world" framing](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/agroecology-is-the-solution-to-world-hunger/) itself. He argues the priority should be feeding communities, not chasing abstract global production targets. The Global Alliance for the Future of Food has described a [vision for food](https://story.futureoffood.org/the-politics-of-knowledge/) built on agroecology, regenerative approaches and Indigenous food traditions. Research in *Nature* has shown that [changing diets in high-income countries alone could bring a substantial "double climate dividend"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-021-00431-5): lower emissions from food, plus land freed up to store carbon. Journalist George Monbiot's 2022 book *Regenesis: Feeding the World Without Devouring the Planet* argues that we have a chance to change both the food system and our wider relationship with the living world. One theme comes up again and again in this work: for food production, "does it scale?" is [often the wrong question](https://ensia.com/voices/food-production-regenerative-agriculture-scale/) to ask.

## Regenerative agriculture and the five principles of soil health

**Regenerative agriculture** has many definitions. One widely cited version, developed through academic research, describes it as farming that starts with protecting the soil and uses that to restore a wider range of natural services, improving the environmental, social and economic sides of food production together. It is sometimes also called carbon farming or agroecology.

Soil is the largest store of carbon on land, holding three to four times more carbon than the atmosphere. Human activity has drained a lot of it: clearing forests, turning natural ecosystems into farmland, and decades of poor soil management. Many soils now release more carbon than they take in. The flip side is an opportunity. Plants can pull carbon out of the air through photosynthesis and put the "lost" carbon back into the ground. The **4 per mille initiative**, launched in 2015 as a call to action for farmers and communities, is built on one calculation: raising the carbon stored in the world's soils by just 0.4% a year could offset all fossil fuel emissions. Practices that help include cover crops, adding compost, biochar (a kind of charcoal added to soil), crop rotation and restoring wetlands.

Soil health rests on five core principles:

1. Keep the soil covered. Bare soil is more easily washed or blown away in winter and by water, loses moisture faster, swings more between hot and cold, and gets compacted more easily.
2. Disturb it as little as possible. Biological, physical or chemical disturbance squeezes out the small air spaces in soil, stops water soaking in, breaks the links between living things in the soil and kills that life outright. The results include erosion by water and wind (and the air pollution that comes with dust), waterlogging from compacted layers below the surface, a hard crust that stops seedlings coming through, and the loss of soil organic matter.
3. Maximise diversity, above and below ground. Growing a mix of crops reduces soil-borne disease, helps plants use water and nutrients more efficiently, and supports more of the useful microorganisms that keep soil working.
4. Keep living roots in the ground. Cover crops planted between harvests keep roots growing all year, which means a steady flow of carbon into the soil.
5. Bring animals back in. Animals, plants and soil evolved together over millions of years, but keeping livestock in confinement has steadily pushed animals off most farms. This is about more than grazing cattle. It can include pollinators, birds and other wild species that control pests, for example owl boxes or perches for birds of prey to keep rodents down. Grazing livestock brings several separate benefits:
   - Grazing in autumn or winter turns high-carbon crop leftovers into low-carbon organic matter, which balances the soil's carbon-to-nitrogen ratio and readies it for seeding without ploughing.
   - Short bursts of grazing in spring or summer, followed by long rests, push plants to regrow and capture more sunlight and CO2.
   - Animals recycle nutrients, minerals and carbon on the spot, instead of feed being carted off by lorry to a feedlot.
   - Grazing can replace herbicide for controlling weeds.
   - Grazing cover crops or crop leftovers lets livestock come off permanent grassland earlier in the season, giving it longer to recover and giving the animals a better diet.
   - It avoids the water pollution and manure problems that come with confinement.

## Can farmers be paid to store carbon?

Soil carbon can be measured, but it is hard to do well. Results vary a lot with the type of land, how deep you measure and the time of year, and the offset schemes that pay farmers for storing carbon are still hotly debated. The central problem is "additionality": would the carbon have been stored on that land anyway, without the payment? Under most current schemes, large corporations pay for the offsets rather than the state paying through subsidies, and it is often unclear whether the carbon they buy is extra at all. There are many competing rulebooks, with little consistency or rigour between them.

An alternative that is gaining ground is to pay farmers more broadly for the ecosystem services they deliver, sometimes called whole-farm revenue support, or to reward crop diversity and cover crops directly, without linking payment to a claimed number of tonnes of stored carbon. Different countries and NGOs are testing their own versions. Whatever the payment model, the practices themselves (cover crops, composting, rotational grazing and agroforestry, which means growing trees alongside crops or livestock) bring benefits well beyond carbon. Soil holds more water, crops are of better quality, farms cope better with drought, heavy rain and heatwaves, pests are easier to manage, and farmers earn more from a wider mix of products, which makes them less exposed to the price swings of a single crop. Several tools now help with measuring and planning:

- the Soil Inventory Project, launched by former US Vice President Al Gore and researcher Dr Kris Covey
- COMET-Farm, which estimates the greenhouse gas effect of adopting particular practices on a given area of land
- the Cool Farm Tool, which also measures biodiversity and water use
- WRI's Land & Carbon Lab, a global initiative tracking changes in land cover and the carbon stored and released with them

## Eight levers for decarbonising the food system

**Chemical fertilisers, pesticides and herbicides.** Making these products takes a lot of energy and carbon, and spreading them on fields routinely leaves too much nitrogen in the environment. [Only about half](https://eos.org/articles/index-suggests-that-half-of-nitrogen-applied-to-crops-is-lost) of the nitrogen fertiliser used worldwide, most of it made from fossil fuels, actually feeds crops. The rest escapes into rivers, streams and groundwater. It has created a dead zone in the Gulf of Mexico roughly the size of the US states of Rhode Island and Delaware combined, and it releases nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas around 265 to 300 times more potent than CO2. There is a clear chance to use less, make fertiliser production cleaner, and expand integrated pest management, which uses natural predators and careful monitoring before reaching for chemicals.

**Controlled-environment and vertical farming.** Growing crops indoors, with light, heat and water fully controlled, is often presented as the future for some types of crop. The Netherlands is the best-known example and has built a reputation for very efficient production this way.

**Precision fermentation and alternative proteins.** Precision fermentation is a refined form of brewing: microbes are multiplied to make specific products. It has long been used to make medicines and food additives and is now being used to develop new staple foods. Eating less red meat brings a real climate benefit, although swapping it for heavily fertilised soya is not the same as regenerative farming. A large body of research finds that [vegetarians have a smaller environmental footprint, and vegans a smaller one still](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959652622043542), and that replacing animal ingredients usually cuts more emissions than buying local. This helped a market for meat alternatives grow, although [that market cooled somewhat in 2022](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2022/11/12/plant-based-meat-market/). Researcher Hannah Ritchie has shown that [meat substitutes tend to have both lower emissions and a smaller land footprint](https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/carbon-footprint-meat-substitutes) than the meat they replace. It is also worth asking [who those animals feed, and where, as a question of fairness](https://www.aljazeera.com/opinions/2022/11/10/livestock-is-a-form-of-climate-justice-in-the-global-south), because livestock plays a different role in food security in parts of the Global South.

**Regional processing facilities.** Large-scale food processing has become concentrated in fewer places, often far from where the food is grown. That adds emissions from transport, refrigeration, cleaning and the bulk processing needed to meet food safety standards.

**Transport and refrigeration.** Refrigeration and chilled transport make up a large share of the [carbon footprint](https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local) of some foods, partly because the refrigerant gases are themselves potent greenhouse gases. Eating more local, fresh food and developing less harmful refrigerants could cut these emissions noticeably.

**Shifting consumption.** Changing what we eat, as individuals, households or institutions such as schools and hospitals, has a lot of potential to cut food emissions (see [Food Carbon Footprint](Food%20Carbon%20FootPrint.md) for how different foods compare). One study found that [eating a plant-based diet](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/aa7541) can cut a person's emissions by around 0.8 tonnes of CO2 a year. As a general rule, very meat-heavy diets in rich countries need to change to take pressure off vulnerable landscapes such as the Brazilian Amazon, where deforestation driven partly by demand for meat and cattle grazing continues at an alarming rate. Fixing food and stopping deforestation are, in this sense, closely linked problems.

**Eating from the sea.** Regenerative ocean farming, especially growing seaweed, is getting attention as another source of protein. Seaweed is rich in antioxidants and protein and can be farmed alongside oysters, mussels and scallops. All of these are far more sustainable than eating fish higher up the marine food chain, such as tuna, swordfish or halibut.

**Reducing food waste.** If food waste were a country, it would be the third-largest emitter in the world. According to the UN Food and Agriculture Organization, between [30 and 40%](https://www.fao.org/in-action/seeking-end-to-loss-and-waste-of-food-along-production-chain/en/) of all food produced is lost or wasted before it reaches the market. The causes range from losses on the farm to waste in homes, restaurants and canteens, as tracked by [UNEP](https://www.unep.org/thinkeatsave/get-informed/worldwide-food-waste) and the [US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sustainable-management-food/wasted-food-scale). See [FACTS About Food Waste](../../circularity-waste/FACTS%20About%20Food%20Waste.md) for more detail.

## Food waste in practice: what is working

Food is wasted at every stage, from farm to shop to home, and many projects are tackling it directly:

- South Korea has almost eliminated food waste going to landfill. It banned food waste from landfill in 2005 and made separate food waste collection in paid bags compulsory in 2013, and it now recycles around 95% of its food waste into compost, animal feed and biogas.
- In Geneva, the nonprofit Free-Go puts public fridges on the street, where people can share food from their homes or from commercial kitchens that would otherwise be thrown away.
- In the US, companies such as Imperfect Foods and Ugly Food sell "ugly" but perfectly good fruit and vegetables that shops would otherwise reject.
- In Canada, nonprofits including Food Stash (Vancouver), Rescue Food (the Prairies) and Second Harvest (nationwide) collect food heading for landfill and pass it on.
- Composting services such as Turn Compost (Dallas-Fort Worth) collect household scraps so people do not have to compost themselves.
- The app Too Good To Go connects people who want to save money and cut waste with restaurants selling surplus food at a discount.
- In the UK, local "gleaning" groups link volunteers with farmers to pick surplus fruit and vegetables that would otherwise be left in the field.

## Diversifying farms and rethinking meat: further reflections

Diversifying makes a farm less dependent on any one thing going right, whether that is the weather, pests or market prices. More farmers practise agroecology than most people assume, although the land involved is still small next to large conventional farms. A useful question for anyone managing land is what grew there naturally before it was farmed. Was it forest, for instance, and what would restoring some of that mean? In the EU, the Climate Farm Academy helps farmers work through this kind of transition. In the US, some dairy farms have moved away from dairy towards non-animal alternatives. The change often starts with a crop failure or some other shock and then grows gradually through practices like cover crops and no-till farming, where the soil is not ploughed.

Cultured (lab-grown) meat is one response to the scale of today's meat production, in which roughly 95% of farmed animals are raised in factory farms. Researchers in the field broadly agree that cultured meat will not replace all meat. They see it as one part of a more sustainable mix of proteins alongside other approaches, and it is an energy-hungry process in its own right. Vertical farming is similar. It makes sense for some crops, such as lettuce, but others, like carrots, still need to grow in open ground, and making vertical farms pay at scale remains a real challenge. On the food waste side, start-ups including Too Good To Go, Mill and Bokashi Living are among those building businesses around throwing less away.
