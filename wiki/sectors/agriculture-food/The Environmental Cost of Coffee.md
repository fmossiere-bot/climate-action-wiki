### Summary

Coffee is one of the most popular drinks in the world, and its environmental footprint is bigger than most people realise. Global coffee drinking **has been rising steadily for almost 30 years**. In Canada, people drink an average of 2.7 cups a day, making coffee the country's most popular drink. Around 2.25 billion cups of coffee are thought to be drunk worldwide every day. The problems run from the farm to the cup: water use, greenhouse gas emissions, deforestation, and now growing exposure to climate change, which threatens harvests in key regions. The good news is that small changes in how you make and drink coffee can make a real difference.

---

### Key Numbers

- Around **2.25 billion cups** of coffee are drunk around the world every day
- **130 to 140 litres of water** are needed to produce a single cup of coffee
- **18,900 litres of water** are needed to produce 1 kg of coffee beans
- Coffee production accounts for **roughly 30 to 80%** of the greenhouse gas emissions across the whole coffee supply chain
- A single cup of coffee can produce up to **50 grams of CO2**, not counting packaging
- Adding milk can double that, and a large latte can reach **340 grams of CO2**
- Production in parts of Colombia's Sierra Nevada has shrunk by **35% in the last five years** because of climate change
- At least **100 million people** around the world depend on coffee farming for a living

---

### Why Coffee Has a Large Footprint

Coffee goes through many stages before it reaches your cup: growing, harvesting, processing, transport, roasting, brewing and disposal. Each step has an environmental cost, but they are not equal.

> Growing the coffee accounts for the largest share of emissions, roughly 30 to 80% of the total depending on how it is produced. **Fertiliser** is the biggest single source of emissions on the farm. Nitrogen fertilisers, which take large amounts of natural gas to make, are a major contributor.

Water use is another big concern. The Water Footprint Network puts the global average water footprint of a single 125ml cup of coffee at 140 litres, more than a 16-minute shower uses. Most coffee is grown in regions that are already **short of water**, and rising global demand is putting local supplies under more pressure. Processing coffee also produces wastewater that can pollute rivers and harm the life in them if it is not handled properly.

International shipping is responsible for around 15% of the greenhouse gases linked to coffee, and exporting adds a further 6 to 11% to total CO2 emissions.

---

### Climate Change Is Now Threatening Supply

Coffee farming is getting harder as temperatures rise. Arabica, the most popular variety, can only cope with average yearly temperatures of up to around 24 degrees Celsius. Farmers in Colombia's Sierra Nevada say temperatures now regularly reach 28 degrees in the afternoon, which would have been unusual just a few decades ago.

Warmer weather is also bringing plant diseases to places that never had them. Rust fungus, brown eye spot and borer insects are spreading to higher altitudes as the climate shifts. Farmers are being pushed to plant further up the mountains, which is harder and more expensive.

Global coffee production in 2023 was around 11 million tonnes, with Brazil, Vietnam, Indonesia, Colombia and Ethiopia as the main producers. Around 51% of the world's coffee comes from South and Central America, and these are the regions most exposed to a changing climate.

>Some farming cooperatives are already adapting. In Colombia, the Red Ecolsierra cooperative, which brings together more than 400 families, uses agroforestry: planting trees among the coffee to give shade, protect the soil and support wildlife. It is also investing in natural farm products and conservation projects, partly paid for through Fairtrade premiums and carbon credits.

### How You Brew Matters

Not all coffee has the same footprint, and the way you make it matters more than many people expect. What counts most is how much coffee you use.

Research keeps finding that **the amount of ground coffee per cup** drives emissions more than the brewing method or the packaging. That is why capsule coffee is not as bad as its reputation suggests. A capsule typically holds 6 to 14 grams of coffee, compared with 25 grams for a standard filter coffee, and the coffee saved makes up for the environmental cost of the capsule itself.

A comparison from Italian research found that a moka pot, common across Europe, produces far fewer emissions per cup than a capsule machine, mostly because of the extra packaging and waste that come with capsules. Instant coffee also does fairly well on emissions, again because it uses less coffee.

Even so, packaging can make up nearly one-third of an average coffee company's CO2 emissions. Most coffee capsules are not made from recycled materials and cannot really be recycled or composted. Whether aluminium capsules get recycled depends entirely on whether they are actually returned and processed, which varies a lot by country and supplier.

Milk rarely gets mentioned, but it matters. Milk accounts for over 75% of the total emissions of a latte. If you drink milky coffees often, switching to oat milk does far more than changing how you brew.

---

### What You Can Do

The most effective steps are simple:

**Drink a little less.** One fewer cup a day does more than any change in brewing method or packaging.

**Do not overdo the ground coffee.** If you use a filter machine or cafetière, stick to the recommended amount rather than adding extra. Wasted coffee is one of the least efficient things you can do in terms of emissions.

**Watch the milk.** Dairy milk adds a lot to the footprint of any coffee. Plant-based alternatives, especially oat milk, have much lower emissions.

**Use a reusable cup.** Disposable cups, paper ones included, end up in landfill and give off methane as they break down. A reusable cup is one of the easiest changes to make.

**Look for certified coffee.** Fairtrade, Rainforest Alliance and Bird-Friendly labels do not remove coffee's footprint, but they point to better farming and more careful use of land.

---

### Coffee, farmers and the climate

Coffee is not one of the biggest parts of a person's carbon footprint, but most of us drink it every day, so it is worth thinking about. The farming communities that grow it are already paying for a changing climate through lower harvests, higher costs and growing uncertainty about the future of their crops.

The link between coffee and climate runs both ways. Badly managed coffee farming adds to deforestation and emissions. Done well, with shade trees and agroforestry, it can support wildlife and help store carbon. Backing those better practices, through what we buy and the labels we look for, makes a difference.
