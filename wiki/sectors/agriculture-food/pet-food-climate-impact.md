# Pet food and climate: the overlooked slice of the agricultural footprint

## What is it

Pet food is a sizeable part of farming's footprint that rarely gets discussed. Around the world, pet dogs and cats eat so much meat and animal by-products that, if the pet-food industry were measured as a country, it would show up as a significant emitter. A peer-reviewed analysis published in PLOS One in 2025, by Brociek, Gardner and colleagues at the University of Nottingham, found that **plant-based dry dog foods** can meet dogs' nutritional needs almost as well as meat-based ones. There are two specific gaps, iodine and vitamin B, and supplements can close them. The finding eases the discomfort many vegan or vegetarian owners feel about feeding meat to their omnivorous pets, and it opens up a real climate lever that was previously thought to be marginal.

## Why it matters

The scale is bigger than most people think. Pet food emissions have been estimated at the equivalent of a middle-sized country. Any shift in that category, even replacing some of the meat with plant-based alternatives, makes a difference to total food-system emissions.

Many pet owners are already inclined to act. People who eat a plant-based diet for ethical or climate reasons often keep feeding meat to their pets because they assumed the alternative was less nutritious. For dogs, this paper changes that assumption.

Pet-food labelling is also weaker than shoppers assume. The Brociek study found that **none** of 25 commercial dry dog foods tested (19 meat, 6 plant-based) fully met official canine nutritional guidelines, even though they were sold as "nutritionally complete". That is a consumer protection issue as much as a climate one.

## Dogs and cats are different

Dogs are **omnivores**. They can digest and get nutrients from a wide range of plant and animal foods. The 2025 Brociek study found that all 25 dog foods tested, plant-based ones included, had acceptable levels of protein, fatty acids and essential amino acids. With added iodine and vitamin B, plant-based dog food works nutritionally.

Cats are **obligate carnivores**, meaning they must eat meat. They need nutrients that are hard or impossible to get from plants in a form their bodies can use:

- **Taurine** (an essential amino acid, needed for the heart and eyes)
- **Arachidonic acid** (a fatty acid cats cannot make themselves)
- **Pre-formed vitamin A** (cats cannot easily convert beta-carotene from plants)
- **Vitamin B12** and others

Commercial vegan cat foods exist, but vets are far more divided about them, and there is less peer-reviewed evidence that they reliably meet cats' needs. The Brociek paper only covered dogs, so its findings **should not be applied to cats**. Owners thinking about plant-based cat food should talk to a vet with nutrition training rather than relying on research about dogs.

## What the 2025 study found

- **25 commercial dry dog foods analysed**: 19 meat-based, 6 plant-based (2 vegetarian, 4 vegan)
- **All 25 foods had acceptable levels** of protein, fatty acids and essential amino acids
- **None of the 25 fully met all official nutritional guidelines**, and this applied to meat and plant foods alike
- **Iodine gap:** most foods, including 5 of 6 plant-based ones, were low in iodine. Seaweed is a simple supplement that closes this gap
- **Vitamin B gap:** plant-based foods fell particularly short. Deficiency affects the skin, nerves and digestion, so manufacturers should add it
- The study notes that most pet foods worldwide **are not independently tested for nutrition**, even when they follow the rules of bodies such as the European Pet Food Industry Federation or the US Association of American Feed Control Officials

## Practical implications for a plant-based pet-food shopper (dogs)

- **Buy from brands that are open about supplements.** Look specifically for added iodine (often from seaweed) and added vitamin B complex
- **Choose shop-bought plant-based food over home-made.** Lucia Casini (University of Pisa) warns that home-made plant-based pet food is especially likely to miss essential nutrients
- **A vet's help is useful,** especially when moving an existing pet from meat-based to plant-based food
- **"Nutritionally complete" is the minimum, not a guarantee of quality**, and this study found that true for both plant and meat foods

## Where this fits in the bigger food picture

Pet food sits alongside our own food choices in the wider story of farming's footprint. Cutting beef and dairy in a household's own diet has a bigger overall effect than changing what goes in the dog's bowl. But pet food used to be treated as off limits, because of the belief that **cats and dogs need meat**. For dogs at least, peer-reviewed research now shows that belief is wrong, as long as the food is properly supplemented.

Together with the [EAT-Lancet planetary diet](eat-lancet-planetary-diet-2025.md), [beef and climate change](beef-and-climate-change.md) and [solar-foods / electro-agriculture](../../solutions/food/solar-foods-electro-agriculture.md), pet food becomes one more lever in building a food system that does not depend on livestock at scale.

<!-- tag: individual-actions -->
**For a pet-owning household:** if you already feed your dog ordinary commercial kibble, switching to a well-supplemented plant-based commercial brand is an easy climate win, as long as iodine and vitamin B are clearly added. For cats, do not switch without a vet's advice.
