# Pet food and climate: the overlooked slice of the agricultural footprint

## What is it

Pet food is a sizeable but under-discussed part of the agricultural footprint. Globally, pet dogs and cats consume meat and animal by-products at a scale that would put the pet-food industry on the map as a country-equivalent emitter if it were measured as one. **A peer-reviewed analysis published in PLOS One in 2025 (Brociek, Gardner and colleagues at the University of Nottingham) found that plant-based dry dog foods can meet dogs' nutritional needs almost as well as meat-based options — with two specific gaps (iodine and vitamin B) that supplementation can close.** The finding reconciles the ethical awkwardness many vegan or vegetarian owners feel about feeding meat to their omnivore pets, and opens a real climate lever that was previously assumed to be marginal.

## Why it matters

Three overlapping reasons:

1. **The scale is bigger than most people think.** Pet food emissions have been estimated at the equivalent of a middle-sized country. Any shift in that category — even partial substitution of meat with plant-based alternatives — matters for the overall food-system emissions picture.
2. **Many pet owners are already minded to act.** Owners who choose plant-based diets for themselves on ethical or climate grounds often continue to feed meat to their pets because the alternative was assumed to be nutritionally inferior. This paper changes that assumption for dogs.
3. **The pet-food industry's labelling is weaker than consumers assume.** The Brociek study found that **none** of 25 commercial dry dog foods tested (19 meat, 6 plant-based) fully met official canine nutritional guidelines despite being sold as "nutritionally complete". That is a consumer-protection story as much as a climate story.

## Dogs vs cats — a crucial distinction

**Dogs are omnivores.** They can digest and extract nutrients from a wide range of plant and animal sources. The 2025 Brociek study found all 25 tested dog foods — plant-based included — had acceptable concentrations of protein, fatty acids and essential amino acids. Plant-based dog food, with iodine and vitamin B supplementation, is nutritionally viable.

**Cats are obligate carnivores.** They require nutrients that are difficult or impossible to get from plants in bioavailable form:

- **Taurine** (essential amino acid, needed for heart and eye function)
- **Arachidonic acid** (fatty acid cats cannot synthesise)
- **Pre-formed vitamin A** (cats cannot efficiently convert plant-based beta-carotene)
- **Vitamin B12** and others

Commercial vegan cat foods exist but are far more controversial among veterinarians, and there is less peer-reviewed evidence that they meet cats' nutritional needs reliably. **The Brociek paper covered dogs only; its findings should not be extrapolated to cats.** Owners considering plant-based cat food should consult a veterinarian with nutrition training, not generalise from the dog literature.

## What the 2025 study found

- **25 commercial dry dog foods analysed** — 19 meat-based, 6 plant-based (2 vegetarian, 4 vegan)
- **All 25 foods had acceptable concentrations** of protein, fatty acids and essential amino acids
- **None of the 25 fully met all official nutritional guidelines** — a finding that applies to meat and plant foods alike
- **Iodine gap:** most foods, including 5 of 6 plant-based ones, were low in iodine. **Seaweed is a simple supplement** that closes this gap
- **Vitamin B gap:** came up particularly short in plant-based foods. Deficiency affects skin, nerves and digestive systems — manufacturers should supplement
- The study notes that most pet foods worldwide **do not go through independent nutritional testing**, even when labelled by regulatory bodies like the European Pet Food Industry Federation or the US Association of American Feed Control Officials

## Practical implications for a plant-based pet-food shopper (dogs)

- **Buy from manufacturers with transparent supplementation.** Look explicitly for added iodine (often seaweed-based) and added vitamin B complex
- **Favour commercially-prepared plant-based food over home-prepared.** Lucia Casini (University of Pisa) warns that home-made plant-based pet food is particularly likely to miss essential nutrients
- **Veterinary supervision helps.** Especially when transitioning an existing pet from meat-based to plant-based food
- **The "nutritionally complete" claim is a floor, not a ceiling** — true for both plant and meat foods per this study

## Where this fits in the bigger food picture

Pet food sits alongside human food choices in the overall agricultural-footprint story. Changing a household's diet to reduce beef and dairy has a bigger absolute impact than switching the dog's bowl, but **pet food was previously treated as untouchable because of the "cats and dogs need meat" assumption**. For dogs at least, that assumption is now peer-reviewed-wrong (with supplementation).

Alongside [EAT-Lancet planetary diet](eat-lancet-planetary-diet-2025.md), [beef and climate change](beef-and-climate-change.md) and [solar-foods / electro-agriculture](../../solutions/food/solar-foods-electro-agriculture.md), pet food becomes one more lever in the "food system without livestock-at-scale" story.

<!-- tag: individual-actions -->
**For a pet-owning household:** if you already feed your dog standard commercial kibble, swapping to a well-supplemented plant-based commercial brand is a low-friction climate win, provided iodine and vitamin B are explicitly supplemented. For cats, do not swap without veterinary advice.
