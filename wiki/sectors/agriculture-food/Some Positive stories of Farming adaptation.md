# Some Positive Stories of Farming Adaptation

## Solar power doubled mango production, and farmers' incomes, in Chamwino, Tanzania

In this dry Tanzanian region, where a reliable water supply is hard to come by, solar-powered water pumps have replaced diesel pumps, bringing greater stability and reliability while cutting both cost and environmental impact. In 2019, the [World Resources Institute](https://www.wri.org/insights/solar-pumps-mango-production-chamwino-tanzania) partnered with Tanzania's Traditional Energy Development Organization (TaTEDO) to identify areas where decentralised renewable energy could support agriculture. Through this initiative, the local agricultural cooperative (AMCOS) received funding and technical assistance to install solar-powered water pumps capable of irrigating 80 hectares (200 acres), along with a drip irrigation system covering a further 10 hectares (25 acres).

The results were dramatic: with a reliable, affordable water supply, mango yields skyrocketed. Between 2021 and 2022, production more than doubled, from an average of 40 fruits per tree to 97, and farmers who adopted drip irrigation saw over 120 mangoes per tree in the same year. Average incomes from mango sales nearly doubled too, rising from around TZS 150,000 (US$56) to TZS 297,000 (US$112) per month. On top of the yield gains, the switch from diesel saved the cooperative over TZS 2.3 million (US$900) a year in fuel and maintenance costs.

Chamwino's water shortages are far from unique. Growing populations, competing demands and poor water management have pushed Tanzania past the World Bank's "water stressed" threshold, and across Africa as a whole, around one in three people face water scarcity, according to the WHO. Shifting weather patterns linked to climate change are making the problem worse: Tanzania has experienced seven significant droughts in the past 15 years. Scaling this kind of success further will take more than the pumps themselves: farmers still need training in best practices such as irrigation scheduling and intercropping, and in modern tools like pest management systems and specialised pruning equipment, and plans to build a wider capacity-building network between farmers and supportive stakeholders have so far been delayed.

## Chemical-free farming brings life back to rural France

Faced with widespread chemical pollution and a dramatic collapse in biodiversity, farmers and scientists in western France have spent more than 30 years testing a genuinely different farming model: no pesticides, no synthetic fertilisers, just nature. It has worked, delivering steady yields, stable incomes and a thriving ecosystem, in what has become a one-of-a-kind collaboration in Europe between researchers and hundreds of participating farmers.

The starting point for the project was a stark decline in biodiversity, particularly among birds and insects, with heavy pesticide use and vast monocultures stripped of hedgerows identified as the primary causes: local bird numbers have been falling by around 1% a year, and insect numbers by closer to 2.5% a year, in part because insects simply have nowhere left to live and breed. In response, the collaboration has focused on **agroecology**, farming that works with natural processes to eliminate toxic chemicals without sacrificing harvests or income. Central practices include soil conservation and deliberately relying on pollinators, whose growing presence has measurably boosted yields, alongside monitoring indicator species like earthworms to track underlying soil health. In the rapeseed sector specifically, farmers have seen yields rise by nearly 40% on plots where pollinator numbers were high.

The project also sits inside a live political debate in France over whether to reintroduce a controversial pesticide, acetamiprid, a conflict that captures the wider tension between maximising short-term yield and profit and protecting public health and nature over the long run. The researchers involved are candid that the transition to sustainable agroecology is not simple: it is a genuinely complex process that requires both time and financial support to work.
