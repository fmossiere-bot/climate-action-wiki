# Some Positive Stories of Farming Adaptation

## Solar power doubled mango production, and farmers' incomes, in Chamwino, Tanzania

Chamwino is a dry region of Tanzania where a reliable water supply is hard to find. There, solar-powered water pumps have replaced diesel ones, making the water supply steadier and more dependable while cutting both costs and environmental harm. In 2019, the [World Resources Institute](https://www.wri.org/insights/solar-pumps-mango-production-chamwino-tanzania) teamed up with Tanzania's Traditional Energy Development Organization (TaTEDO) to find places where small, local renewable energy systems could support farming. Through this work, the local farming cooperative (AMCOS) received money and technical help to install solar pumps able to water 80 hectares (200 acres), plus a drip irrigation system covering another 10 hectares (25 acres).

With water they could rely on and afford, mango yields soared. Between 2021 and 2022, production more than doubled, from an average of 40 fruits per tree to 97. Farmers who also used drip irrigation got over 120 mangoes per tree that year. Average monthly income from mango sales nearly doubled as well, from around TZS 150,000 (US$56) to TZS 297,000 (US$112). On top of that, moving away from diesel saved the cooperative over TZS 2.3 million (US$900) a year in fuel and maintenance.

Chamwino is far from the only place short of water. Growing populations, competing demands and poor water management have pushed Tanzania past the World Bank's "water stressed" threshold. Across Africa, around one in three people face water scarcity, according to the WHO. Changing weather patterns linked to climate change are making things worse: Tanzania has had seven major droughts in the past 15 years. Spreading this success will take more than pumps. Farmers still need training in good practice, such as when to water and how to grow crops side by side, and in modern tools like pest management systems and specialist pruning equipment. Plans for a wider network to help farmers and their supporters build these skills have so far been delayed.

## Chemical-free farming brings life back to rural France

Facing widespread chemical pollution and a steep loss of wildlife, farmers and scientists in western France have spent more than 30 years testing a different way of farming: no pesticides, no synthetic fertilisers, just nature. It has worked. Yields are steady, incomes are stable and the local ecosystem is thriving. The project is a one-of-a-kind partnership in Europe between researchers and hundreds of farmers.

It began with a sharp fall in wildlife, especially birds and insects. Heavy pesticide use and huge single-crop fields with the hedgerows ripped out were found to be the main causes. Local bird numbers have been falling by around 1% a year, and insect numbers by closer to 2.5% a year, partly because insects have nowhere left to live and breed. In response, the partnership has focused on **agroecology**: farming that works with nature to get rid of toxic chemicals without losing harvests or income. Key practices include protecting the soil and relying on pollinators, whose growing numbers have measurably raised yields. Farmers also keep an eye on indicator species such as earthworms, which show how healthy the soil is. In rapeseed, farmers have seen yields rise by nearly 40% on plots where there were plenty of pollinators.

The project sits in the middle of a live political debate in France over whether to bring back a controversial pesticide, acetamiprid. The row captures a wider tension between getting the most yield and profit in the short term and protecting people's health and nature over the long run. The researchers are open about the fact that moving to agroecology is not simple. It is a complex process that needs both time and financial support to work.
