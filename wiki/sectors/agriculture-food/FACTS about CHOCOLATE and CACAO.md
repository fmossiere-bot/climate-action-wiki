# FACTS about Chocolate and Cacao

## What is it

Cacao appears in far more of our food than the chocolate bar most people picture, and few crops raise as many overlapping questions at once: is it good for you, is it good for the planet, and is it produced ethically? The answers pull in different directions. Chocolate's biggest environmental and social problem is not really its carbon footprint, it is the combination of low farmer wages and heavy water use in already water-stressed growing regions, and the industry has a documented history of child labour and unfair pay. About three in every four cacao beans grown worldwide come from Africa, chiefly Ghana and Ivory Coast, and deforestation linked to rising chocolate demand remains an ongoing concern.

## Carbon and water footprints

Chocolate's carbon footprint is smaller than its reputation suggests: a typical 40g chocolate bar has a lower carbon footprint than a 250g punnet of strawberries. Water is a bigger concern. A 100g chocolate bar requires around 1,700 litres of water to produce, much of it in regions where water is already scarce.

## The ethical problem

Reports of rampant human rights and environmental abuses in the chocolate industry first emerged more than 20 years ago. Millions of farming families in Ivory Coast and Ghana, where most of the world's commodity cocoa is grown, subsisted on less than $1 a day, with children performing forced labour, including wielding machetes and spraying toxic agrichemicals, instead of attending school, while vast areas of tropical forest were cleared to make room for more cocoa.

In 2000, chocolate processors and manufacturers formed the World Cocoa Foundation to lead the industry toward a fairer, more sustainable cocoa sector, and many companies launched their own reform programmes: Nestlé's Cocoa Plan, Mars's Cocoa for Generations, Mondelez's Cocoa Life, and Hershey's Cocoa For Good. On paper, these suggest steady progress. In practice, industry watchdogs broadly agree that little real headway has been made toward a fair and sustainable cocoa sector. According to the [Cocoa Barometer report](https://voicenetwork.cc/cocoa-barometer/) from the Voice Network, a consortium of NGOs and trade unions, deforestation continues at an alarming rate in Ghana and Ivory Coast, and [child labour remains widespread](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2020/oct/20/chocolate-industry-slammed-for-failure-to-crack-down-on-child-labour) on cocoa farms, arguably even more so than when it was first exposed two decades ago. The underlying driver of both problems is that the vast majority of West African cocoa farmers earn well below a living income.

The price data tells the story starkly. Cocoa's benchmark New York Mercantile Exchange price has averaged roughly $2,400 a metric tonne for five decades; it fluctuates, but that average has barely moved even as chocolate bars in shops have become significantly more expensive. [According to Fairtrade](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2021/jun/opinion-why-efforts-cote-divoire-and-ghana-help-cocoa-farmers-havent-worked), the price of cocoa accounted for up to 50% of the retail value of a chocolate bar in the 1970s, fell to 16% by the 1980s, and today accounts for around just 6% of the value of every chocolate bar sold.

## Can chocolate also be good for you?

Cacao does carry some genuine physiological and emotional benefits, in moderation. It is rich in antioxidants, particularly the flavonoid epicatechin, which help neutralise free radicals and support cellular health. Flavonoids in cacao are also linked to improved blood flow, lower blood pressure and reduced LDL cholesterol, and regular intake of dark chocolate (70% cacao or more) is associated with a reduced risk of heart disease. Cacao boosts cerebral blood flow and contains theobromine and small amounts of caffeine, mild stimulants that can support focus and alertness, along with magnesium, iron, zinc, copper and potassium, nutrients important for muscle relaxation, nervous system function and energy production. Its polyphenols have anti-inflammatory effects that may support immune health.

The emotional effects are notable too: cacao stimulates the release of endorphins, dopamine and serotonin, and contains phenylethylamine (PEA), sometimes nicknamed the "love chemical," which can create feelings of excitement and pleasure. Its magnesium content can help relax the nervous system, and dark chocolate has been shown to lower cortisol levels in some studies. In its raw, ceremonial form, ceremonial-grade cacao has long been used in spiritual and community settings, with participants often reporting feelings of grounding and connection. To capture these benefits without the downsides, the advice is straightforward: choose dark chocolate (70%+) or raw ceremonial-grade cacao, avoid heavily processed chocolate loaded with added sugar, dairy or artificial additives, and treat it as a mindful, occasional pleasure rather than a habit.

## Rising prices: yield loss meets climate change

Cacao prices have been climbing steadily since August 2022, reaching unprecedented highs in late 2024 and sending shockwaves through the global confectionery market, according to [VOA News](https://www.voanews.com/a/cocoa-prices-triple-in-one-year-as-climate-change-hits-crops/7538765.html). Cacao prices had historically fluctuated between $2,000 and $4,000 per metric tonne; the 2023 average was $3.28 per kilogram. By December 2024, prices had soared to almost $12,000 per metric tonne, an 88% increase in just three months and nearly triple the price of a year earlier.

The most significant driver of this surge is the impact of climate change on cacao production in West Africa, which supplies most of the world's cacao. Excessive rainfall in Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire in late 2023 triggered outbreaks of swollen shoot virus and black pod disease, both of which damage cacao pods and reduce yields, compounding the effects of El Niño to produce several years of below-average harvests. Several other factors have layered on top of this: steadily increasing global demand for cocoa; cacao's limited geography, since it can only be grown near the equator, making supply vulnerable to localised disruption; market speculation, as news of crop failures encouraged traders to bet on further price rises; decades of underinvestment in cacao farmland caused by historically low prices, which has left yields and soil quality depressed; and the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR), which restricts the sale of cocoa grown on deforested land and has added further pressure to supply. Together, these factors have created what amounts to a perfect storm in the cacao market.

## Ethical and lower-impact alternatives

A handful of companies are trying different routes through this landscape. **Planet A Foods**, a Munich-based food tech start-up, [raised $30 million](https://techcrunch.com/2024/12/05/planet-a-foods-nabs-30m-to-make-tons-more-cocoa-free-chocolate/) in Series B funding in December 2024 to scale up ChoViva, a cocoa-free chocolate made from sunflower seeds and oats, from 2,000 to over 15,000 tonnes of annual production. ChoViva offers up to an 80% lower carbon footprint than traditional chocolate and sidesteps the deforestation and emissions tied to cocoa farming entirely. It is currently sold in Germany, Austria and Switzerland, with plans to expand into the UK, France and the US, where the company intends to open its first production facility.

**Tony's Chocolonely** presents itself as a mission-driven company working toward "100% slave-free" cocoa, criticising an industry it says is "dominated by a handful of chocolate giants that profit from keeping the cocoa purchasing price as low as possible." It holds Fairtrade and B-corp certifications but relies for manufacturing on Barry Callebaut, one of the world's largest chocolate processors, a relationship that saw Tony's [dropped in 2021](https://www.slavefreechocolate.org/ethical-chocolate-companies) from Slave Free Chocolate's list of ethical companies. That same year, Barry Callebaut, along with Nestlé, Mars and Hershey, [faced a lawsuit](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2021/feb/12/mars-nestle-and-hershey-to-face-landmark-child-slavery-lawsuit-in-us) in the US brought by eight children who said they had been used as slave labour on cocoa plantations; the US Supreme Court ultimately ruled the companies could not be sued. Tony's has said the delisting reflected Barry Callebaut's broader associations, not its own practices, and that its strategy is deliberately to stay "inside the lion's den": using Barry Callebaut's facilities, with dedicated storage vessels and production lines that keep Tony's beans fully segregated from other supply, to prove that ethical, fully traceable chocolate can be scaled through a mainstream global processor. The company has been a Barry Callebaut partner since 2005 and publishes its sourcing criteria and agroforestry policy through its Tony's Open Chain reporting programme, giving it one of the more transparent disclosure records in the industry against standard ESG measures: biodiversity impact (deforestation and habitat loss risk in cocoa-growing regions), climate impact (greenhouse gas disclosure across scopes 1 to 3 and a decarbonisation strategy), sustainable land use, water usage, waste and circular economy practices, and certifications such as Fairtrade and Rainforest Alliance.

**Beyond Good** takes a different approach, producing single-origin chocolate bars from cocoa grown in Madagascar. Rather than working through the usual long chain of middlemen in the cocoa trade, the company says it buys beans directly from local farmer cooperatives and transports them to its own manufacturing facility in Antananarivo, Madagascar's capital, paying farmers directly and manufacturing close to the source instead of shipping raw beans abroad for processing.
