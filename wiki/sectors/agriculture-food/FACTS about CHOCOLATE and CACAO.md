# FACTS about Chocolate and Cacao

## What is it

Cacao turns up in far more of our food than the chocolate bar most of us picture. Few crops raise so many questions at once: is it good for you, is it good for the planet, and is it produced fairly? The answers pull in different directions. Chocolate's biggest environmental and social problems are less about carbon and more about low pay for farmers and heavy water use in regions that are already short of water. The industry also has a documented history of child labour and unfair pay. About three in every four cacao beans grown worldwide come from Africa, mainly Ghana and Ivory Coast, and forest clearance linked to rising demand for chocolate is still a concern.

## Carbon and water footprints

Chocolate's carbon footprint is smaller than its reputation suggests. A typical 40g chocolate bar has a lower carbon footprint than a 250g punnet of strawberries. Water is the bigger worry. Making a 100g chocolate bar takes around 1,700 litres of water, much of it in places where water is already scarce.

## The ethical problem

Reports of widespread human rights abuses and environmental damage in the chocolate industry first came out more than 20 years ago. Millions of farming families in Ivory Coast and Ghana, where most of the world's commodity cocoa is grown, were living on less than $1 a day. Children were doing forced labour, including handling machetes and spraying toxic farm chemicals, instead of going to school. Meanwhile, large areas of tropical forest were cleared to plant more cocoa.

In 2000, chocolate processors and manufacturers set up the World Cocoa Foundation to lead the industry towards a fairer, more sustainable cocoa sector. Many companies launched their own programmes: Nestlé's Cocoa Plan, Mars's Cocoa for Generations, Mondelez's Cocoa Life and Hershey's Cocoa For Good. On paper, this looks like steady progress. In practice, industry watchdogs broadly agree that little real headway has been made. According to the [Cocoa Barometer report](https://voicenetwork.cc/cocoa-barometer/) from the Voice Network, a group of NGOs and trade unions, deforestation continues at an alarming rate in Ghana and Ivory Coast, and [child labour remains widespread](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2020/oct/20/chocolate-industry-slammed-for-failure-to-crack-down-on-child-labour) on cocoa farms, arguably more so than when it was first exposed two decades ago. Behind both problems is the same cause: most West African cocoa farmers earn well below a living income.

The price data makes this plain. The benchmark New York Mercantile Exchange price for cocoa has averaged roughly $2,400 a metric tonne for five decades. It goes up and down, but that average has barely moved, even as chocolate bars in the shops have become much more expensive. [According to Fairtrade](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2021/jun/opinion-why-efforts-cote-divoire-and-ghana-help-cocoa-farmers-havent-worked), cocoa made up as much as 50% of the shop price of a chocolate bar in the 1970s. By the 1980s that had fallen to 16%, and today it is around 6%.

## Can chocolate also be good for you?

In moderation, cacao does have some real benefits for body and mood. It is rich in antioxidants, especially a flavonoid called epicatechin, which help protect cells from damage. Flavonoids in cacao are also linked to better blood flow, lower blood pressure and lower LDL ("bad") cholesterol, and eating dark chocolate (70% cacao or more) regularly is associated with a lower risk of heart disease. Cacao increases blood flow to the brain. It contains theobromine and small amounts of caffeine, mild stimulants that can help with focus and alertness. It also provides magnesium, iron, zinc, copper and potassium, which matter for relaxing muscles, the nervous system and energy. Its polyphenols reduce inflammation, which may support the immune system.

There are emotional effects too. Cacao triggers the release of endorphins, dopamine and serotonin, and contains phenylethylamine (PEA), sometimes nicknamed the "love chemical", which can bring feelings of excitement and pleasure. Its magnesium can help calm the nervous system, and some studies have found that dark chocolate lowers levels of cortisol, a stress hormone. Ceremonial-grade cacao, in its raw form, has long been used in spiritual and community settings, and people taking part often describe feeling grounded and connected. To get the benefits without the downsides, the advice is simple: choose dark chocolate (70%+) or raw ceremonial-grade cacao, avoid heavily processed chocolate full of added sugar, dairy or artificial additives, and enjoy it as an occasional treat rather than a habit.

## Rising prices: yield loss meets climate change

Cacao prices have been rising steadily since August 2022 and reached record highs in late 2024, shaking the global sweets and chocolate market, according to [VOA News](https://www.voanews.com/a/cocoa-prices-triple-in-one-year-as-climate-change-hits-crops/7538765.html). Historically, cacao traded between $2,000 and $4,000 per metric tonne, and the 2023 average was $3.28 per kilogram. By December 2024, the price had shot up to almost $12,000 per metric tonne. That was an 88% rise in just three months, and nearly three times the price of a year earlier.

The biggest driver is climate change hitting cacao production in West Africa, which supplies most of the world's cacao. Very heavy rain in Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire in late 2023 set off outbreaks of swollen shoot virus and black pod disease. Both damage cacao pods and cut yields, and together with the effects of El Niño they led to several years of below-average harvests. Other factors added to the pressure:

- global demand for cocoa keeps growing
- cacao only grows near the equator, so supply is easily disrupted by local problems
- news of crop failures encouraged traders to bet on prices rising further
- decades of low prices meant little investment in cacao farms, leaving yields and soil quality poor
- the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) restricts the sale of cocoa grown on cleared forest land, which has squeezed supply further

Taken together, these created a perfect storm in the cacao market.

## Ethical and lower-impact alternatives

A few companies are trying different paths. **Planet A Foods**, a food tech start-up based in Munich, [raised $30 million](https://techcrunch.com/2024/12/05/planet-a-foods-nabs-30m-to-make-tons-more-cocoa-free-chocolate/) in Series B funding in December 2024. The money is to scale up ChoViva, a cocoa-free chocolate made from sunflower seeds and oats, from 2,000 to over 15,000 tonnes a year. ChoViva has up to an 80% lower carbon footprint than traditional chocolate and avoids the deforestation and emissions of cocoa farming altogether. It is sold in Germany, Austria and Switzerland, and the company plans to expand into the UK, France and the US, where it intends to open its first production site.

**Tony's Chocolonely** presents itself as a mission-driven company working towards "100% slave-free" cocoa. It criticises an industry it says is "dominated by a handful of chocolate giants that profit from keeping the cocoa purchasing price as low as possible." It holds Fairtrade and B-corp certifications, but its chocolate is made by Barry Callebaut, one of the world's largest chocolate processors. Because of that relationship, Tony's was [dropped in 2021](https://www.slavefreechocolate.org/ethical-chocolate-companies) from Slave Free Chocolate's list of ethical companies. The same year, Barry Callebaut, along with Nestlé, Mars and Hershey, [faced a lawsuit](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2021/feb/12/mars-nestle-and-hershey-to-face-landmark-child-slavery-lawsuit-in-us) in the US brought by eight children who said they had been used as slave labour on cocoa plantations. A federal judge dismissed that case in June 2022, and an appeals court upheld the dismissal in July 2025. It is a different case from Nestlé USA v. Doe, a lawsuit by six Malian men against Nestlé USA and Cargill, in which the US Supreme Court ruled in June 2021 that the claims could not go ahead as filed, because the abuse took place abroad and the only US link alleged was general corporate activity. Tony's says the delisting was about Barry Callebaut's wider links, not its own practices. Its strategy is to stay "inside the lion's den" on purpose. It uses Barry Callebaut's factories, with its own storage tanks and production lines that keep Tony's beans fully separate from other supply, to show that ethical, fully traceable chocolate can be made at scale by a mainstream global processor. Tony's has worked with Barry Callebaut since 2005. Through its Tony's Open Chain reporting programme it publishes its sourcing criteria and agroforestry policy, which gives it one of the more transparent records in the industry on the usual ESG measures: biodiversity impact (the risk of deforestation and habitat loss in cocoa regions), climate impact (greenhouse gas reporting across scopes 1 to 3, meaning its own emissions, its energy and its whole supply chain, plus a plan to cut them), sustainable land use, water use, waste and circular economy practices, and certifications such as Fairtrade and Rainforest Alliance.

**Beyond Good** does things differently. It makes single-origin chocolate bars from cocoa grown in Madagascar. Instead of going through the usual long chain of middlemen in the cocoa trade, the company says it buys beans directly from local farmer cooperatives and takes them to its own factory in Antananarivo, Madagascar's capital. Farmers are paid directly, and the chocolate is made close to where the cocoa grows rather than shipping raw beans abroad for processing.
