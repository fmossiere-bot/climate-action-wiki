# The Environmental Impact of Digital Activities

## We live in a digital world

Our world runs on digital technology: computers for work, entertainment, keeping in touch, streaming video, running complex calculations, and even using AI to save energy. Most of us now own several connected devices besides a phone or laptop, such as speakers, TVs and watches.

We spend roughly **a third of our lives looking at a screen**, and the smartphone takes up most of that time. The average person uses one for around 3 hours 15 minutes a day, and people in Ireland check their phones an estimated 50 to 100 times a day (source: Deloitte consumer research). All of this has an environmental cost. Digital products and services accounted for an estimated 3.8% of total global greenhouse gas emissions in 2021. That share is growing, especially as AI use spreads, and is projected to reach 14% by 2040.

## Digital transformation for sustainability

Digital technology is a source of emissions, but it is also a tool for cutting them. It can help societies run more efficiently, bring education and support to underserved communities, monitor biodiversity more accurately, and reduce water use and waste. Digital transformation means much more than scanning paper documents and making them searchable. It means renewing and developing technology, and AI is a large part of that opportunity. Digital technology could have a positive effect on an estimated **70% of the 169 targets across the 17 UN Sustainable Development Goals** (source: UN Digital for Sustainability).

At the same time, going digital uses a lot of energy and materials. Left unmanaged, it can fuel unsustainable consumption and widen existing inequalities. So digital and green goals need to move together, and a sustainable digital transformation has to be both at once. Otherwise digitalisation risks speeding up the drivers of the **Triple Planetary Crisis** (climate change, nature loss and pollution), the very problems it promises to help solve.

That outcome can be avoided. One often-cited example comes from course material on climate-efficient digital product design. If YouTube cut unused image data and offered audio-only streaming for videos that are really just music, it could save an estimated **500,000 tonnes of CO2**. That is more than it would save by running its servers entirely on renewable energy. Small design choices, repeated across billions of users, can matter more than they first seem to.

## How much searching, streaming and scrolling adds up to

Google alone handles an estimated **8.5 billion searches a day** (source: internetlivestats.com), up from around 35 million a day in 2000 and roughly 1 billion a day in 2010. Note that Google's target for 2025 was carbon neutrality, not net zero. Carbon neutrality usually relies on offsetting or carbon capture and only counts CO2, not the other greenhouse gases, so the two terms are not the same thing.

Searching is only one of many digital habits. An estimated 84% of users run more than three searches a day, and 63% of all searches now happen on mobile, which shows how fully phones have become our default way of looking things up. More broadly, people spend **around 3 hours 15 minutes a day** on their phones on average, and check them around 58 times a day (source: Exploding Topics). Streaming, social media and other everyday digital activities all rely on the same infrastructure underneath: software, processing power and data centres.

For a sense of scale, **100 Google searches produce around 20 grams of CO2** (Search Engine Land), and one hour of video streaming produces about 36 grams of CO2 (Wired). A separate IEA estimate puts 30 minutes of streaming at around 20 grams of CO2, which is broadly in line with the Wired figure once you allow for the different time periods. The IEA's article also has a calculator you can use to estimate your own digital footprint.

## A small but fast-growing slice of emissions

Digital software is still a small share of global greenhouse gas emissions today, but that share is growing quickly. [The Environmental Impact of Bitcoin](The%20Environmental%20Impact%20of%20Bitcoin.md) shows Bitcoin transaction volumes growing 27% year on year. The fast spread of data centres has also led some countries, including Ireland, to pause and ask whether further growth puts their renewable energy timelines at risk (see [IRELAND Data Centre Dilemma](../../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Data%20Centre%20Dilemna.md)).

Our appetite for connectivity, instant information and entertainment shows no sign of slowing. That is why digital emissions are forecast to reach **14% of all global greenhouse gas emissions by 2040**. A large part of that growth will come from AI, which is a good reason to build efficiency and sustainability into digital products now, while the industry is still taking shape. Unless real limits are put on the growth of infrastructure, both technical efficiency and changes in how people use technology will be needed to stop digital activity eating up a large part of the world's remaining emissions budget (source: Harvard Business Review, "How Green Is Your Software?").

## Companies working on the problem

A small but growing group of companies build tools to help people and businesses manage their digital footprint. **elow** (elow.energy) offers a service for both consumers and businesses that automatically reduces emissions from everyday digital use and reports back on the size of a user's digital footprint.

Making devices last longer helps too. Choosing refurbished devices over new ones, and designing hardware to be repaired rather than replaced, follows the same logic that makes keeping a phone in use for longer such an effective step (see [Carbon Footprint of an iPhone](Carbon%20Footprint%20of%20an%20iPhone.md) for the Fairphone comparison and the numbers behind it).
