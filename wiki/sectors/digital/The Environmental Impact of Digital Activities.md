# The Environmental Impact of Digital Activities

## We live in a digital world

Whether it's a computer for work, entertainment, staying in touch with people, streaming video, running complex calculations, or optimising energy efficiency with AI, our world runs on digital technology. Most of us now own several connected devices beyond a phone or laptop: speakers, TVs, watches, and more.

We spend roughly **a third of our lives looking at a screen**, and the smartphone dominates that time: the average person uses one for around **3 hours 15 minutes a day**, and in Ireland, people check their phones an estimated **50 to 100 times a day** (source: Deloitte consumer research). All of this usage comes at an environmental cost. Digital products and services accounted for an estimated **3.8% of total global greenhouse gas emissions in 2021**, a share that is growing, particularly as AI use expands, and is projected to reach **14% by 2040**.

## Digital transformation for sustainability

Digital technology is not only a source of emissions, it is also a tool for cutting them. Digital tools can help societies become more efficient, extend education and support to underserved communities, monitor biodiversity more accurately, and reduce water use and waste. Digital transformation is about far more than scanning paper documents and making them searchable: it means renewing and developing new technology, and AI represents a significant part of that opportunity. It is estimated that digital technology could positively influence **70% of the targets across the 169 UN Sustainable Development Goals** (source: UN Digital for Sustainability).

At the same time, digitisation is inherently energy- and material-intensive, and left unmanaged it can fuel unsustainable consumption and widen existing inequalities. Digital and green goals therefore need to move together: a genuinely sustainable digital transformation has to be both digital and green at once. Left unchecked, digitalisation risks accelerating the drivers of the **Triple Planetary Crisis**: climate change, nature loss, and pollution, the exact opposite of what it promises to help solve.

That future is not inevitable. One often-cited example, drawn from course material on climate-efficient digital product design, is that if YouTube reduced unused image data and offered audio-only streaming for videos that are really just music, it could save an estimated **500,000 tonnes of CO2**, more than it would save by switching its servers entirely to renewable energy. Small design choices, multiplied across billions of users, can matter more than they first appear to.

## How much searching, streaming and scrolling adds up to

Google alone handles an estimated **8.5 billion searches a day** (source: internetlivestats.com), up from around 35 million a day in 2000 and roughly 1 billion a day in 2010. It's worth noting that Google has targeted carbon **neutrality**, not net-zero, by 2025: carbon neutrality typically relies on offsetting or carbon capture and only accounts for CO2 emissions, not the full range of other greenhouse gases, so the two terms are not interchangeable.

Searching is only one of many digital habits competing for our attention. An estimated **84% of users run more than three searches a day**, and **63% of all searches now happen on mobile**, reflecting how completely mobile devices have taken over as our default way of getting information. More broadly, people spend an average of **more than 3 hours a day** on their phones, checking them around **58 times a day** on average (source: Exploding Topics). Streaming, social media and other everyday digital activities all rely on the same underlying infrastructure: software, processing power, and data centres.

For a sense of scale, **100 Google searches produce around 20 grams of CO2** (Search Engine Land), and **one hour of video streaming produces approximately 36 grams of CO2** (Wired). A separate estimate from the IEA puts 30 minutes of streaming at around 20 grams of CO2, broadly consistent with the Wired figure once time periods are accounted for; the IEA's article also includes a calculator readers can use to estimate their own digital footprint.

## A small but fast-growing slice of emissions

Digital software's share of global greenhouse gas emissions is still small today, but it is growing quickly. [The Environmental Impact of Bitcoin](The%20Environmental%20Impact%20of%20Bitcoin.md) shows Bitcoin transaction volumes growing 27% year on year, and the rapid multiplication of data centres has pushed some countries, including Ireland, to pause and reassess whether continued growth threatens their renewable energy transition timelines (see [IRELAND Data Centre Dilemma](../../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Data%20Centre%20Dilemna.md)).

Our appetite for connectivity, instant information, entertainment and gratification shows no sign of slowing, which is why digital emissions are forecast to reach **14% of all global greenhouse gas emissions by 2040**. A significant share of that growth will come from AI specifically, which only underlines how important it is to build efficiency and sustainability into digital products now, while the industry is still being shaped. Unless meaningful safeguards are put on infrastructure growth, both technical efficiency and user behaviour change will be essential to stop digital activity becoming a major source of the world's remaining emissions budget (source: Harvard Business Review, "How Green Is Your Software?").

## Companies working on the problem

A small but growing group of companies are building tools specifically to help individuals and businesses manage their digital footprint. **elow** (elow.energy) offers a digital solution for both consumers and businesses that automatically reduces emissions from everyday digital use and reports back on the impact of a user's digital footprint.

Longevity-focused approaches matter here too: choosing refurbished devices over new ones, and designing hardware for repair rather than replacement, follows the same logic that makes keeping a phone in use for longer such an effective lever (see [Carbon Footprint of an iPhone](Carbon%20Footprint%20of%20an%20iPhone.md) for the Fairphone comparison and the numbers behind it).
