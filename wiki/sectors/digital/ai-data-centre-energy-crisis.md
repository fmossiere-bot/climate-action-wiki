# AI Data Centres and the Energy Crisis

## What is it

The fast growth of AI infrastructure is running into climate targets. **Data centres built to run AI need far more power than conventional server farms**, and in both the UK and Ireland the grid cannot keep up. As a result, more and more of them are turning to fossil gas, and not as a backup but as their main, permanent power source. At the same time, governments have consistently underestimated the real carbon cost of this building boom.

## Why it matters

AI data centres are changing how whole countries use energy. Ireland already uses 22% of its electricity for data centres, more than all urban homes combined. The UK government raised its estimate of AI-related carbon emissions by a factor of over 100 after the original figure turned out to be a serious undercount. Left unchecked, this building boom could undo clean energy progress in both countries, push up household bills and lock grids into decades of fossil fuel use.

## The UK: gas as a permanent solution
<!-- tag: uk -->

The UK National Grid has a backlog of 100 GW of data centre projects waiting to connect, far more than it can take. Facing waits of several years for a grid connection, over 100 data centre operators asked to connect to the gas network in the two years to 2026. Together these requests come to more than 15 TWh a year, enough to power London for four and a half months. Some operators want 100 MW+ of gas power permanently, not as a stopgap.

The UK's Clean Power 2030 target aims to bring unabated gas (gas burned without capturing its CO2) below 5% of electricity supply. The regulator, Ofgem, acknowledged this raises "an interesting question about what that means for the target." Meanwhile, 11 US data centres built for Meta, OpenAI, Microsoft and xAI are reportedly on track to emit more carbon than the whole of Morocco.

## Emissions 100× larger than thought
<!-- tag: uk -->

In April 2026, the UK government quietly raised its estimate of the carbon impact of its planned AI data centre building programme by a factor of more than 100. The original figure, a maximum of 0.142 million tonnes of CO₂ a year, had been published in the UK Compute Roadmap. It was deleted after an investigation by Foxglove and Carbon Brief showed it to be a serious undercount.

The revised estimate is **34–123 million tonnes of CO₂ over 2025–2035**, equal to 0.9–3.4% of the UK's projected total emissions over that period. Reaching the lower end depends on AI becoming more efficient and the grid decarbonising faster.

Foxglove's head of strategy said: *"The government has a legally binding commitment to reach net zero by 2050. This already sat awkwardly alongside its hell-for-leather embrace of a hyperscale AI data centre buildout, which unchecked could double the electricity consumption of the entire country."*

## Ireland: a 'hidden data centre tax'
<!-- tag: ireland -->

Ireland shows most clearly what unmanaged data centre growth means for ordinary households. Data centres used **22% of Ireland's electricity in 2025**, more than all urban homes combined. A 2026 report commissioned by Friends of the Earth Ireland and Beyond Fossil Fuels found this had:

- **Drained €715 million from the Irish economy** over 2015–2023
- Added a cumulative average of **€360 to each household's electricity bill** over the same period
- Future projections: a further **€295–€644 per household** cumulatively from 2025 to 2034

The mechanism is simple. Data centres need a lot of electricity, all the time, and cannot easily reduce it. That demand increases the number of hours when gas sets the price in the Irish electricity market, which raises bills for everyone. The report called this a "hidden data centre tax."

Ireland requires new data centres to get 80% of their energy from additional renewable capacity, the strictest rule in Europe. Industry groups dispute the finding on household costs, pointing to their economic contribution and corporate tax payments.

## Slough: a hyperscale heat island
<!-- tag: uk -->

Reporting from the UK's June 2026 heatwave added a local side to the data centre debate: **the heat coming off the clusters themselves**. Slough, 16 km west of Heathrow, hosts an estimated 30 to 40 large data centres totalling roughly 1 gigawatt of capacity, the largest hub in Europe. They are owned by operators such as Equinix and Digital Realty and serve Amazon, Google, Oracle and Microsoft. More expansion is already planned on the same site.

A preprint (a study not yet peer reviewed) led by Andrea Marinoni at the University of Cambridge finds that data centres raise temperatures in their immediate surroundings by **an average of 2°C, and as much as 9°C**. The study uses decades of satellite data and allows for the effects of urban growth and climate change, and the effect holds in other countries too (Brazil, Spain). Marinoni notes that his data covers an older generation of facilities, typically up to 100 MW, while Slough is about 10× that size: *"Slough is almost like an experiment by itself. The new investments in data centres are bringing to life a new generation of data centres."*

On 24 June 2026, the weather station closest to the Slough tech park recorded **36.7°C**, several degrees hotter than other stations in the town. The pattern held all week. Local residents describe the heat as "pinching your body" and have noticed the area is always warmer than nearby Windsor.

The heat comes from the **cooling systems** that keep AI chips and tightly packed server racks at safe temperatures: the energy used to chill the inside of the building is pushed outside as warm air. The UK government has proposed schemes to recover that waste heat for district heating, but for now most of it is simply released into the air.

## Key facts

- UK: 100+ gas connection requests from data centre operators, totalling **15+ TWh/year**, enough to power London for 4.5 months.
- UK: the government revised its AI emissions estimate from 0.142m to **34–123m tonnes CO₂ over 2025–35**, a 100× increase.
- Slough hosts an estimated **30–40 data centres totalling roughly 1 GW**, the largest hub in Europe.
- Cambridge research (Marinoni et al., 2026 preprint): data centres raise local temperatures by **2°C on average and up to 9°C** in their immediate surroundings.
- Ireland: data centres used **22% of national electricity** in 2025, more than all urban homes.
- Ireland: data centres added a cumulative **€360 per household (2015–2023)** to electricity bills.
- Ireland: proposed new data centres could need up to **5,800 MW**, almost equal to Ireland's entire peak demand of 6,000 MW.
- Globally: nearly **7,000 of 8,808 data centres** are built in places poorly suited to their climate.

## UK data centres and water: 'fatally flawed' government forecasts
<!-- tag: uk -->

In July 2026, **Water UK**, the trade body for water companies in Britain and Northern Ireland, sent written evidence to MPs calling the government's water forecasts "fatally flawed" because they explicitly leave out datacentre demand. The trade body put it this way: *"There appears to be an assumption that the country will always have enough water for its economic needs. Nothing could be further from the truth."*

The details:

- **125 datacentres are proposed or under construction in the Affinity Water region alone**. Affinity Water serves Slough, which has the UK's highest concentration of datacentres
- Individual datacentres in that region are asking for up to **3 million litres of water a day**, the peak water demand of 3,500 homes
- Because of Environment Agency permitting rules, datacentres currently have to **use drinking water only**, not recycled water
- UK datacentres currently use **~6.6 million litres a day**. With the government's plan to triple datacentre capacity by 2030, that rises to **~19.8 million litres a day**
- A May 2026 House of Lords report says England is on track for a **5 billion litres a day shortfall in public water supply by 2055**
- More than **three-quarters of UK datacentres** are in the south and east of England, the country's most water-stressed regions, which currently have hosepipe bans

Water UK deputy director Jon Chappel called it *"unforgivable"* and *"a genuine failing of government."* The trade body has asked ministers to publish a **drought hierarchy**, a formal ranking of which sectors get water first in a shortage, but none has been produced.

There is a further complication: **datacentres have been designated critical national infrastructure**. Oliver Hayes (Global Action Plan) warns this may leave water companies powerless to limit datacentres' water use in a drought, and that households could be put behind datacentres "running chatbots, or enabling people to do benign and very wasteful, pointless things." The national policy statement for AI growth, which was due in February 2026 and was meant to set out these trade-offs openly, has been delayed.

Similar tensions have come up in Ireland (see [Ireland Data Centre Dilemma](../../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Data%20Centre%20Dilemna.md)). Taken together, the [Slough heat-island research](../../sources/slough-datacentre-heat-island-2026.md) (electricity + waste heat) and the Water UK submission (water) show the UK facing pressure on three fronts: the grid, households and the water table.

*Source: [UK datacentre water shortage (source summary)](../../sources/uk-datacentre-water-shortage-guardian-2026.md); Guardian, Pippa Neill, 21 July 2026.*

## July 2026: Meta quits RE100 as tech pivots to gas

The trend goes beyond grid and water pressures. In **July 2026, Meta withdrew from RE100**, the Climate Group initiative (based in the UK) under which 444 major companies, including Apple, Google and Microsoft, pledge to use 100% renewable electricity. Meta had joined in 2016. The Climate Group's statement was blunt: *"Meta has withdrawn from the RE100 initiative, as it is no longer able to meet the technical criteria due to investments made in new gas power."*

The reason is that Meta has struck deals paying utilities to build **new natural gas plants**, including 10 to power its Hyperion data centre in Louisiana alone. Meta says it still matches **100% of its electricity with clean power on an annualised basis** through long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs), but RE100's technical criteria now ask for more than that.

Meta is not alone. Microsoft signed a deal with Chevron to supply gas-generated electricity to a West Texas data centre, and Google has been linked to similar partnerships. Natural gas has long been sold as a "bridge fuel", but methane leaks during extraction, and burning gas releases nitrogen oxides, sulphur, mercury and particulates. The difference between annual matching and 24/7 hourly matching (clean power every hour, not just enough over a year) is now the main dividing line in how companies count clean energy, and one of the biggest tech firms has decided it cannot meet the stricter standard.

*Source: [Meta RE100 exit (source summary)](../../sources/meta-quits-re100-rte-2026.md); RTÉ News, 25 July 2026.*

## July 2026: the chemistry of "waterless" cooling, PFAS in the pipes

Datacentre cooling has been discussed almost entirely as a trade-off between water and heat. In July 2026, a Guardian investigation added a third issue: **the chemicals used in the alternative**.

Google and others have promoted **two-phase cooling systems** as a way to use less water than conventional evaporative cooling. Few users realise that these systems use a **PFAS "forever chemical" gas** as the refrigerant, pumped through copper tubing. The industry says it is a "closed loop" and very little escapes, but some does leak, and the coolant has to be disposed of at the end of its life.

Chemours has asked the US EPA to fast-track approval of **Opteon 2P50**, a new PFAS coolant designed for this use. It is the first chemical fast-tracked under a Trump executive order calling for quicker review of datacentre chemicals. In response, a coalition of 17 environmental groups led by Earthjustice filed formal comments in July 2026 asking the EPA to reject the application. They allege that Chemours relied on industry-friendly science, used a species of lab rat less affected by PFAS than humans are, and left out key data on toxicity.

The concern also covers what the coolant breaks down into: **TFA (trifluoroacetic acid)**. The European Chemical Agency classified TFA as a hazardous chemical in July 2026, citing probable thyroid disruption, reduced sperm quality and liver toxicity. Unlike most of the world, the US EPA still does not classify TFA as a PFAS.

Lenny Siegel of Chips Communities United put it like this: *"The datacenter industry knows it is under fire for its enormous use of water so they cast themselves as the good guys by using alternative cooling, but they're not telling [the public] that they're using hazardous chemicals. Some are toxic, some are greenhouse gases and some are both."*

If two-phase cooling spreads as planned, the AI building boom becomes a **public health issue for drinking water** as well as a question of grids, water supply and gas. Earthjustice has signalled it will go to court if Opteon 2P50 is approved without a proper review of the health risks, which could test the limits of Trump's executive order.

*Source: [PFAS in datacentre cooling (source summary)](../../sources/pfas-datacentre-cooling-guardian-2026.md); Guardian, Tom Perkins, 27 July 2026.*

## August 2026: two datacentres, more emissions than ExxonMobil's whole UK operation
<!-- tag: uk -->

Analysis by the non-profit **Foxglove** puts a number on the UK gas-for-datacentres trend described above. Two planned sites in England, Wapseys Wood (Buckinghamshire) and Quest Park (Bedfordshire), will together use 1.3GW of power. Once fully running, they will emit more than **4.5 million tonnes of CO2 a year**, more than all of ExxonMobil's UK emissions (3.9 million tonnes in 2023, according to Ember).

Both sites want their own **gas-fired power stations on site** rather than wait years for a grid connection. They are among **315 datacentres** currently queuing for UK grid access, representing 73GW of demand, almost double the UK's entire peak winter demand. Both are also among the first projects fast-tracked through the Nationally Significant Infrastructure Project (NSIP) regime, which skips local planning authorities and leaves the decision to central government. The government disputed Foxglove's figures, saying they assume an unrealistic 100% utilisation. Foxglove and Friends of the Earth reply that this assumption is standard industry practice and reflects what operators actually aim for.

Caroline Lucas called the projected emissions "absolutely horrifying". ClientEarth's Sam Hunter-Jones argued that "every megawatt powering a datacentre... is a megawatt that could not be used to replace fossil fuels elsewhere."

*Source: [UK datacentres vs ExxonMobil (source summary)](../../sources/uk-datacentres-exxonmobil-emissions-guardian-2026.md); Guardian, Pippa Neill, 25 August 2026.*
