# Digital Sustainability for Pollution Action

## What is it

**Pollution**, by its very nature, denotes an excess of substances in environments where they do not belong. These substances are called pollutants, and they can be either natural or created by human activity. Approximately 9 million people lose their lives annually due to pollution's effects, according to The Lancet Planetary Health, underlining the urgency of pollution prevention for the well-being of both current and future generations.

## Why it matters

Rapid industrial development in the 1960s and 1970s was boosted by processes highly dependent on chemicals. Around the same period, the Green Revolution spread from developed to developing countries, advancing an agricultural model based on widespread use of chemical fertilisers and pesticides, seen as essential to increasing productivity. What was initially received as a positive development for industrial and agricultural activity was soon recognised as a double-edged sword: in 1962, Rachel Carson's book *Silent Spring* exposed the environmental impacts of the indiscriminate use of synthetic pesticides in the US, after she studied the deaths of birds and a rise in "uncommon" cancers and diseases in communities near where these pesticides were used.

The impact of pollution is felt most acutely by low- and middle-income countries, particularly in the Global South. Oxfam's report *Climate Equality: A Planet for the 99%*, published ahead of COP28, found that the richest 1% of the world's population produced as much carbon pollution in 2019 as the five billion people who made up the poorest two-thirds of humanity.

There has been real progress too. The Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer (1985) and the Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer (1987) have been instrumental in phasing out more than 99% of the chemicals that destroy the stratospheric ozone layer, which protects life on Earth by absorbing harmful ultraviolet radiation from the sun. The UN System Common Approach Towards a Pollution-Free Planet now aims for a unified, ambitious objective: a pollution-free planet that ensures the health and well-being of people and the environment. [IQAir's interactive map](https://www.iqair.com/) is one example of how digital tools can enhance data gathering and decision-making, offering real-time air pollution data.

## Digital Transformation for Pollution Prevention and Elimination

Addressing pollution presents a paradox: it is nearly impossible to eliminate without severely impacting our ability to survive, because societal infrastructure, spanning production, distribution and consumption, necessarily generates some pollution. The challenge is not to halt these cycles entirely, which is impractical, but to find a viable balance, managing systems in a way that maintains supplies of goods and services while keeping pollution levels below thresholds that pose serious risks to health, worsen climate change, and accelerate biodiversity loss.

Systemic shifts, including the transition towards circular economies, the promotion of sustainable lifestyles, and green energy transitions at both individual and institutional level, alongside targeted government policies, can significantly prevent pollution at the source. These approaches are more impactful than post-facto clean-up, recycling, treatment or disposal of pollutants, and digital transformation equips societies with the tools to implement these systemic changes.

**Transition towards a circular economy.** Today's economy is largely based on a linear "take-make-use-dispose" model. According to the Circularity Gap Report 2024, between 2016 and 2021 the global economy consumed 582 billion tonnes of materials, nearly as many materials as the 740 billion tonnes consumed across the entire 20th century. There is an urgent need to move away from the linear model and transition to a regenerative growth model, in which the value of products, materials and resources is maintained in the economy for as long as possible. The One Planet Network's report *Harnessing Digital Technologies for a Circular Economy* estimates that AI could drastically minimise the unnecessary plastic present in 30% of packaging.

**Promotion of sustainable lifestyles.** There is large inequality in the material consumption associated with lifestyle: the emissions share of the richest, highest-emitting 10% of individuals ranges from 36-49% of the global total. Beyond individual will, lifestyle choices are also conditioned by social norms. Digital applications embedded in e-commerce platforms, covering product comparability, ethical nudging, gamification, carbon footprint calculators, positive feedback loops and green activations, can support a shift in awareness of how individual actions contribute to the Triple Planetary Crisis.

**Transition to low-carbon energy.** Digital transformation can do a great deal to reduce energy consumption while the world moves towards more renewable energy production. The IEA estimates that using technology already available could improve efficiency by 3,070 terawatt-hours (TWh), more than 12% of 2018 global electricity consumption.

## Digital Transformation for Pollution Reduction

Beyond prevention, digital tools also help reduce pollution levels and clean up pollutants already in the environment:

- **Monitoring and reducing air pollution.** IoT sensors and AI-powered data analytics enable pollution sources to be identified and changes tracked over time through real-time monitoring, enhancing decision-making and enabling proactive interventions to protect public health and the environment.
- **Preserving freshwater.** Ecosystems critical to supplying humanity with fresh water are degrading due to climate change, overexploitation and pollution at an unprecedented rate. Satellite imaging, drones and advanced sensor networks allow water bodies to be monitored with precision, detecting contaminants and aiding targeted conservation efforts.
- **Safeguarding oceans and marine health.** Oceans and coastal waters receive much of their waste and pollutants, including plastic debris, nutrients, oil, heavy metals and radioactive waste, from land-based sources. It is estimated that more than 8 million tonnes of plastic end up in the oceans each year, the equivalent of emptying a rubbish lorry of plastic every minute. Satellite monitoring, underwater drones and sensor networks allow precise tracking of ocean conditions, providing real-time data on water quality, marine biodiversity, and pollutant levels, including the distribution and concentration of plastic debris.
- **Tackling chemical pollution.** Chemicals are integral to modern life, used across sectors from agriculture to manufacturing, with more than 95% of all manufactured goods relying on chemical technologies. The evidence for chemical risks is constantly growing, with an estimated 1.6 million deaths in 2016 attributed to exposure to selected chemicals. IoT devices, remote sensors and real-time data analytics give precise insight into chemical levels in water, soil and air, helping identify pollution sources and concentrations.
- **Addressing land and soil pollution.** Thousands of years of human activity, the improper disposal of hazardous and urban waste, urban sprawl, industrial operations, transportation, mining, military activities and conflict have all contributed to land and soil pollution. Over 75% of the Earth's land area is now considered degraded, with projections suggesting this could rise to 95% by 2050 if current trends persist. Remote sensing, Geographic Information Systems (GIS) and drones significantly improve the accurate mapping and monitoring of contaminated sites, enabling effective strategies for detecting, preventing and remediating land and soil pollution.
- **Optimising waste management.** By 2025, the world's cities alone are projected to produce 2.2 billion tonnes of waste annually, more than three times the amount generated in 2009, with mining contributing significantly to the problem. Between 400,000 and 1 million people die in the Global South each year from diseases related to mismanaged waste. AI-powered inventory management systems can reduce food waste through improved tracking and smart logistics, while real-time data analytics can optimise waste collection routes, reducing fuel consumption and emissions, and smart sensors can monitor waste levels to prevent overflows and enable timely pickups.
