# Digital Sustainability for Pollution Action

## What is it

**Pollution** means too much of a substance in a place where it does not belong. These substances are called pollutants, and they can be natural or made by people. Approximately 9 million people die each year from the effects of pollution, according to The Lancet Planetary Health. That is why preventing pollution matters so much, for people alive today and for future generations.

## Why it matters

Fast industrial growth in the 1960s and 1970s relied heavily on chemicals. Around the same time, the Green Revolution spread from rich countries to developing ones. It brought a farming model built on widespread use of chemical fertilisers and pesticides, which were seen as essential to growing more food. What first looked like progress for industry and farming soon showed its other side. In 1962, Rachel Carson's book *Silent Spring* exposed the environmental damage caused by careless use of synthetic pesticides in the US. She had studied bird deaths and a rise in "uncommon" cancers and diseases in communities near where these pesticides were used.

Pollution hits low- and middle-income countries hardest, especially in the Global South. Oxfam's report *Climate Equality: A Planet for the 99%*, published ahead of COP28, found that the richest 1% of the world's population produced as much carbon pollution in 2019 as the **five billion people** who made up the poorest two-thirds of humanity.

There has been real progress too. The Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer (1985) and the Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer (1987) have helped phase out more than 99% of the chemicals that destroy the ozone layer high in the atmosphere, which protects life on Earth by absorbing harmful ultraviolet radiation from the sun. The UN System Common Approach Towards a Pollution-Free Planet now sets one shared, ambitious goal: a pollution-free planet where people and the environment stay healthy. [IQAir's interactive map](https://www.iqair.com/) is one example of how digital tools improve data gathering and decisions, with real-time air pollution data.

## Digital Transformation for Pollution Prevention and Elimination

Pollution poses a dilemma. It is almost impossible to eliminate without badly affecting our ability to live, because the systems society runs on, from production to distribution and consumption, always create some pollution. Stopping those systems entirely is not realistic. The task is to find a workable balance: keep goods and services flowing while holding pollution below the levels that seriously harm health, worsen climate change and speed up biodiversity loss.

Bigger changes to whole systems can do a lot to stop pollution at the source. These include moving to circular economies, encouraging sustainable lifestyles, and switching to green energy, by individuals and institutions alike, backed by targeted government policies. These approaches achieve more than cleaning up, recycling, treating or disposing of pollutants afterwards, and digital tools give societies the means to put them in place.

**Transition towards a circular economy.** Today's economy mostly follows a straight line: take, make, use, throw away. According to the Circularity Gap Report 2024, between 2016 and 2021 the global economy used **582 billion tonnes** of materials, almost as much as the 740 billion tonnes used in the whole of the 20th century. We urgently need to move from that linear model to a regenerative one, in which products, materials and resources keep their value in the economy for as long as possible. The One Planet Network's report *Harnessing Digital Technologies for a Circular Economy* estimates that AI could greatly reduce the unnecessary plastic found in 30% of packaging.

**Promotion of sustainable lifestyles.** How much people consume varies hugely with lifestyle. The richest, highest-emitting 10% of individuals account for between 36-49% of global emissions. Lifestyle choices are shaped by social norms as well as personal will. Digital features built into online shopping, such as product comparisons, gentle ethical nudges, game-like rewards, carbon footprint calculators, positive feedback and green prompts, can help people see how their own actions feed into the Triple Planetary Crisis (climate change, nature loss and pollution).

**Transition to low-carbon energy.** Digital tools can do a lot to cut energy use while the world shifts towards more renewable energy. The IEA estimates that technology already available could save **3,070 terawatt-hours (TWh)** through efficiency, more than 12% of global electricity consumption in 2018.

## Digital Transformation for Pollution Reduction

As well as preventing pollution, digital tools help reduce it and clean up pollutants already in the environment:

- **Monitoring and reducing air pollution.** IoT sensors (small connected devices) and AI data analysis make it possible to pinpoint pollution sources and track changes over time in real time. That leads to better decisions and earlier action to protect public health and the environment.
- **Preserving freshwater.** The ecosystems that supply humanity with fresh water are being degraded by climate change, overuse and pollution faster than ever before. Satellite images, drones and advanced sensor networks can monitor water bodies precisely, detect contaminants and help target conservation.
- **Safeguarding oceans and marine health.** Much of the waste and pollution in oceans and coastal waters comes from land, including plastic debris, nutrients, oil, heavy metals and radioactive waste. More than 8 million tonnes of plastic are estimated to end up in the oceans each year, the same as emptying a rubbish lorry of plastic every minute. Satellite monitoring, underwater drones and sensor networks track ocean conditions precisely, giving real-time data on water quality, marine life and pollution levels, including where plastic debris is and how concentrated it is.
- **Tackling chemical pollution.** Chemicals are part of modern life, used in everything from farming to manufacturing, and more than 95% of all manufactured goods rely on chemical technologies. Evidence of chemical risks keeps growing: an estimated 1.6 million deaths in 2016 were linked to exposure to selected chemicals. IoT devices, remote sensors and real-time data analysis show chemical levels in water, soil and air precisely, helping identify where pollution comes from and how concentrated it is.
- **Addressing land and soil pollution.** Thousands of years of human activity have polluted land and soil, through careless disposal of hazardous and urban waste, urban sprawl, industry, transport, mining, military activity and conflict. Over 75% of the Earth's land area is now considered degraded, and this could rise to 95% by 2050 if current trends continue. Remote sensing, Geographic Information Systems (GIS, digital mapping) and drones make it much easier to map and monitor contaminated sites accurately, which helps detect, prevent and clean up land and soil pollution.
- **Optimising waste management.** The world produced about 2.1 billion tonnes of municipal waste in 2023, and this is projected to reach 3.8 billion tonnes a year by 2050, with mining adding a lot to the problem. Between 400,000 and 1 million people die in the Global South each year from diseases linked to badly managed waste. AI stock management systems can reduce food waste through better tracking and smarter logistics. Real-time data can plan better waste collection routes, saving fuel and emissions, and smart sensors can check how full bins are, so they are emptied on time and do not overflow.

Digital technology's own environmental costs (its energy and material use, e-waste, and its effect on the digital divide) are covered in more detail in [Digital Transformation for Sustainable Development](Digital%20Transformation%20for%20Sustainable%20Development.md), alongside its potential for tackling pollution described here.
