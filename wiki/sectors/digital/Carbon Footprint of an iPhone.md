## What is it

An iPhone is a highly sophisticated piece of technology, and behind the glass and aluminium sits a long global supply chain. The **carbon footprint of an iPhone** is the total greenhouse gas released across its whole life: digging metals out of the ground, assembling the device in factories, flying it across oceans, charging it every day, and finally disposing of it when it breaks or goes out of date.

One iPhone's lifetime carbon footprint is somewhere between 55 kg and 120 kg of CO2, depending on the model and storage capacity. On its own, that is comparable to burning a few gallons of petrol. Multiplied by the billions of smartphones in use worldwide, it becomes a significant source of global emissions.

## Why it matters

Digital convenience has a physical cost. Between **75% and 95% of a phone's lifetime emissions** are released before you open the box. Extracting the raw materials, including 16 of the 17 known rare-earth metals, damages ecosystems, and the energy needed to make microchips rivals the annual emissions of entire countries.

Manufacturer reports also tend to understate the real impact. They often leave out the energy used by mobile networks and cloud data centres each time we stream a video, back up photos or load an app. And as electronic waste piles up in landfill, it can leach toxic materials into soil and water. Knowing this can help people resist upgrading too early. Keeping a phone for five years instead of three, or choosing a brand designed for repair, can cut a person's annual smartphone carbon footprint by more than 30%.

## Manufacturing dominates the footprint

Manufacturing is by far the biggest single contributor. Up to **95% of an iPhone's total emissions** happen during production, not daily use. For scale, the global carbon footprint of smartphone manufacturing is roughly equal to the annual CO2 emissions of the Philippines, a country of more than 100 million people.

Averaged over a typical four-year life, an iPhone's emissions break down like this: production and packaging 81%, energy and charging during use 16%, global shipping 2%, and recycling or disposal at the end less than 1%. Storage matters too, because more storage takes more energy to make. An iPhone 14 Pro with 128 GB creates around **65 kg of CO2**, while the 1 TB version rises to 116 kg.

Estimated lifetime CO2 emissions by model: iPhone 8, 57 kg; iPhone SE 2020, 57 kg; iPhone 13 Mini, 61 kg; iPhone 14, 61 kg; iPhone 12 Mini, 62 kg; iPhone XR, 62 kg; iPhone 13, 64 kg; iPhone 12, 68 kg; iPhone 13 Pro, 69 kg; iPhone XS, 70 kg; iPhone 11, 70 kg; iPhone X, 79 kg; iPhone 11 Pro Max, 80 kg.

## Usage emissions are routinely understated

Manufacturer reports have been criticised for underestimating usage emissions, because they leave out the mobile networks and cloud services a phone relies on. Apple's iPhone 13 "Product Environmental Report" put just 10 kg of the phone's 64 kg lifetime footprint down to usage. Once mobile data and cloud storage are counted, analysts estimate that roughly 50 kg of hidden CO2 should be added, bringing real usage emissions closer to 60 kg per device.

The iPhone 14 shows the same pattern. Apple's official breakdown is 79% source materials and production, 2% packaging and shipping, 18% phone usage, and under 1% end of life. Independent estimates that include the wider network and cloud footprint put real four-year usage emissions closer to **313 kg of CO2**, several times the official figure. The gap shows why a product's full footprint has to include the parts the manufacturer does not directly control.

## Charging adds up at global scale

One phone's charging footprint is small. Billions of devices charging every day is another matter. Data from 2021 put **global smartphone charging emissions at 8,088,324 tonnes of CO2 equivalent per year**, roughly the same as putting 1,758,311 petrol cars on American roads. The cloud storage and computing behind each device adds more demand: smartphone cloud services are estimated to need around 30 billion watts (30 gigawatts) of power, a continuous draw rather than a yearly total.

8 Billion Trees offers some comparisons for that yearly charging footprint: a bullet train carrying 2 billion passengers, the energy to light the Eiffel Tower 23,597,630 times, the energy to produce 242,165,389 pairs of denim jeans, or the energy to cook 1,394,538,620 barbecue meals in the UK.

## Corporate responses differ sharply

Manufacturers are not equally open or ambitious about this footprint. **Apple** uses 100% recycled tin, tungsten, rare-earth elements and gold in key components, alongside 35% recycled plastic, and has committed to a carbon-neutral supply chain by 2030. Samsung relies far less on certified recycled metals. It uses recycled ocean plastics mainly for small structural parts, such as the inner shell of its S Pen stylus, and has not announced a firm date for a supply-chain carbon target.

## Longevity is the biggest lever available to consumers

Because manufacturing dominates the footprint, how long a phone stays in use matters more than almost anything else an owner can control. **Fairphone**, a company based in Amsterdam, builds phones with easily replaceable parts to extend their usable life and push back against planned obsolescence. On its most recent model, 70% of materials and components come from Fairtrade sources and/or recycling. Fairphone is aiming for net-zero by 2045 on scope 1 and 2 emissions. Scope 3, where roughly 98% of its footprint sits, is being tackled by working with suppliers to increase recycled content over time.

The number to remember: because around 75% of a phone's emissions come from production, keeping a phone for **5 years instead of 3** cuts its annual carbon footprint by more than 30%. A phone built to last, and used for longer, beats almost anything else an individual owner can do.
