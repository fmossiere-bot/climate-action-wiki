# Digital Transformation for Sustainable Development

## What is it

The world is at a turning point in how it handles technology. Societies can use digital technologies to build a sustainable future, or they can let digitalisation run unchecked and speed up the causes of the Triple Planetary Crisis: climate change, nature loss and pollution. Digital tools can do a lot to advance the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. Left unchecked, they can also make worse the very problems they could help solve.

Three terms are often mixed up. **Digitization** means converting information from analogue to digital form. Digitalisation means using digital technologies to turn products and services into digital ones, making them more efficient and opening up new ideas. Digital transformation is broader still: a change across the economy, society and the environment, set off by digitalisation on a large scale. It happens as new possibilities open up when non-digital processes are replaced with digital ones, or older technology with the next generation. The result is something new, rather than a digital version of the old way of doing things.

For sustainability, the change needs to be digital and green at the same time. Digital technology can help with around 70% of the 169 Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) targets. But going digital also uses a lot of energy and materials, feeds unsustainable consumption and deepens inequalities.

## Why it matters

There is growing evidence that a global economy built on maximising shareholder value has come at a huge cost to people and the planet, even though it has delivered strong growth and high living standards for many. Getting digital transformation right takes deliberate choices rather than adopting whatever comes along. Projects need to involve many groups, above all local communities. Both the public and private sectors need people who understand digital technology and sustainability. Policymakers need to understand the environmental opportunities and risks of digital transformation. Entrepreneurs, computer scientists and engineers need to understand the environmental impact of what they build. And digital and sustainability skills need to be part of university courses and vocational training.

## What is CODES

The **Coalition for Digital Environmental Sustainability (CODES)** aims to steer the digital age towards a fair and sustainable transition, through three shifts across whole systems:

1. Bring digital transformation and sustainability together around a common purpose, through standards, training and joint action
2. Reduce the harm done by the digital age, such as emissions, e-waste and the digital divide
3. Speed up digital innovation for sustainability, through innovation labs, the circular economy and a just transition

## Mitigating the Negative Impacts of Digital Transformation

### Energy use and emissions

Computers, mobile phones, internet servers and crypto-mining operations need huge amounts of energy. Data centres in particular use vast amounts of energy and large quantities of water, which can strain local water supplies (see [IRELAND Datacentre Dilemma](../../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Data%20Centre%20Dilemna.md) for what this looks like in practice). The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) estimated that ICT's share of global electricity consumption was projected to reach **3.2% in 2020**, and that share is forecast to keep rising. This matters most for data centres and the recent AI boom, which is straining power supplies in the US and slowing the shift to renewable energy elsewhere, including in Ireland. Total ICT electricity demand is rising, and data centres are likely to take a growing share of it in the coming years. Tackling this needs sustainable, local renewable energy to power data centres, better carbon footprint accounting, and more green data centres as part of a sustainable digital transformation.

### Waste generation

As well as the energy to run devices and data centres, making them and keeping up with rising demand requires mining metals on a large scale. Extraction of minerals and metals such as graphite, lithium and cobalt could increase by 500% by 2050, with serious harm to land, water, air and biodiversity. When badly governed, mining can destroy landscapes, pollute groundwater and force indigenous people from their land. In the end, these minerals and metals, fused together inside electronic devices, often end up in large e-waste dumps (see [Carbon Footprint of an iPhone](Carbon%20Footprint%20of%20an%20iPhone.md) for what this looks like for a single device). In 2019, a record **53.6 million tonnes** of e-waste was produced worldwide. This is expected to more than double, to around 120 million tonnes, by 2050. Most of it usually ends up in developing and least developed countries.

### Addressing consumption drivers

Digital consumption has never been higher, and one-click buying on platforms like Amazon has made the underlying problem of overconsumption worse.

### Tackling misinformation

True stories take roughly six times longer to reach 1,500 people than false stories take to reach the same number. As COVID-19 showed, misinformation travels fast, and correcting it afterwards with fact-checking has largely not worked. Once people have been exposed to a falsehood, it is hard to undo. A bit like vaccination, "**prebunking**" warns people in advance and exposes them to weakened doses of misinformation, which can help build "mental antibodies" against fake news.

### Guarding against the digital divide

Internet access is unequal depending on where people live, their age, gender and income. This gap is growing and is holding back the benefits of digital transformation for whole groups of people. If nothing is done, the gap between poorly connected and highly digital communities will keep widening, deepening existing inequalities.

### Preventing rights violations

Three areas linked to environmental and social sustainability need particular attention in the digital world:

1. Resource conflicts. In many regions, indigenous or local communities who want to protect the natural resources they live from are in conflict with mining operations. These mines extract the metals and minerals, such as cobalt, graphite, copper and rare earth metals, that are needed to make the electronics behind digital transformation.
2. Digital surveillance. Several countries are cracking down on activists and journalists by watching their personal and working lives.
3. Violation of digital privacy. As more websites, apps and software reach users worldwide, many actors are using them to collect information and data about people.

## Accelerating Innovation

Some notable examples of digital innovation helping the planet:

- [Climate TRACE](https://www.climatetrace.org/) uses satellite images, other remote sensing, artificial intelligence and shared data science expertise to track human-caused greenhouse gas emissions as they happen.
- [Destination Earth](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/destination-earth), part of the European Commission's Green Deal and Digital Strategy, aims to build a highly accurate digital model of the whole Earth, to monitor and predict how natural events and human activities affect each other.
- NVIDIA's [Earth-2 (E-2)](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/2021/11/12/earth-2-supercomputer/) is planned as the world's most capable AI supercomputer dedicated to predicting climate change, creating a digital twin of Earth in the Omniverse.

Digital transformation can also help drive the shift to a sustainable circular economy. Smart, connected and monitored products make it easier to scale up circularity. A [**Digital Product Passport**](https://wupperinst.org/en/a/wi/a/s/ad/7784), which tracks a product's climate, environmental and social impacts along its whole value chain, could do a great deal to speed up that shift.

### Empowering sustainable consumption

Unsustainable consumption comes from rising demand for fossil fuels, growing carbon emissions and deepening inequalities, all of which harm people and the planet. As global greenhouse gas emissions rise from pollution and industrial processes, so do global temperatures, and extreme weather such as heatwaves, droughts and irregular rainfall becomes more frequent. This puts extra pressure on farming and food production. Combined with overconsumption and economic shocks, it pushes up food prices, making food harder to afford and less secure. In 2019, the amount of materials used to meet global demand for goods and services, known as Domestic Material Consumption, came to **95.1 billion tonnes**. Most of it is not properly managed, creating more and more waste and pollution.
