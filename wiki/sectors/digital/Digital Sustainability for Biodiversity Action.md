# Digital Sustainability for Biodiversity Action

## What is it

By 2030, the world will need more resources, including more water, food and energy, and that growing demand will make biodiversity loss worse. The challenge is to fit human development together with care for the environment. A resilient future for the planet and its communities depends on conserving biodiversity, restoring it and using it sustainably.

**Ecosystems** and **biodiversity** are related but not the same. The biodiversity we see today is the result of around 3.5 billion years of evolution (the Earth itself is about 4.5 billion years old) and, more and more, of human influence too. It is the connected web of life that humans are part of, and it keeps running the processes that support all life on Earth, including ours.

## Why it matters

Human activity is speeding up biodiversity loss. The WWF's 2022 Living Planet Report found an average **69% decline** in populations of mammals, birds, fish, reptiles and amphibians since 1970. Our well-being, health and economic prospects depend on biodiversity and natural systems, and the evidence clearly shows biodiversity in decline (see also [what's happening to coral reefs](../../concepts/key-ideas/FACTS%20on%20EVERYTHING%20-%20Under%20the%20Sky%20we%20Make.md#coral-reef-nearly-gone)).

There is some good news. Global deforestation between 2010 and 2020 was about a third lower than in the previous decade. Protected areas have grown a lot over the past decade, from about 10% to 15% of land and from about 3% to 7% of marine areas. Key Biodiversity Areas, the sites that do most to maintain biodiversity worldwide, have seen a 15% increase in protection.

## The Global Biodiversity Framework

On 19 December 2022, 196 countries agreed the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF). It sets out a path towards a world living in harmony with nature by 2050, and a framework for global action to get there. The framework calls for at least 30% of the world's land, inland waters, coastal areas and oceans to be well conserved and managed, and for 30% of degraded ecosystems to be restored, by 2030. This is often called the **"30x30" target**. In adopting the GBF, all parties agreed to set national targets based on its 23 global targets. Success depends on how well countries plan, monitor, report on and review their progress at home, with a review each year at the relevant COP.

Digital tools can help a lot here. They make it easier to collect, analyse and share environmental data, and to roll out solutions that reduce threats to biodiversity quickly and at scale. The [UN Biodiversity Lab](https://unbiodiversitylab.org/en/) is one example: an interactive monitoring map that is already doing this.

## Conservation of Biodiversity

Conservation means actively protecting the biodiversity that already exists in ecosystems. It includes setting up and managing protected areas, passing laws to prevent over-exploitation, and education to help people understand why biodiversity matters. Biodiversity gives us direct benefits such as food, fibre, firewood and medicines. It also gives indirect ones through ecosystem services such as pollination, pest control, a steadier climate and flood control.

The **Biodiversity Intactness Index (BII)**, run by the Natural History Museum, tracks biodiversity change using data on how many plants, fungi and animals there are worldwide. It shows how local biodiversity on land responds to human pressures such as changing land use and more intensive farming. Only areas that have kept enough natural biodiversity score above 90%, the proposed planetary boundary for this measure.

**Land and freshwater.** Forests are among the largest stores of carbon and biodiversity on Earth. They help limit climate change and provide goods, services and livelihoods. According to WWF's 2023 Extracted Forests report, mining and the infrastructure around it are among the largest drivers of deforestation and biodiversity loss, with approximately 20% of mining sites in biodiversity hotspots. Some 84% of global direct mining-related deforestation (MRD) over the past 20 years happened in just 10 countries.

**Oceans.** Record sea surface temperatures have been linked to marine heatwaves, periods of unusually warm ocean water. These can badly damage ocean ecosystems and biodiversity, and they hit people too, through fisheries, aquaculture, tourism and other industries. A **Digital Twin of the Ocean (DTO)**, a detailed virtual model of the ocean updated almost in real time, is helping people make science-based decisions to protect marine environments.

**Food systems.** Global food systems are a direct driver of biodiversity loss. Food production is degrading or destroying natural habitats and pushing species towards extinction. This comes from turning natural or semi-natural land into farmland, and from unsustainable farming practices on farms, across landscapes and globally. Digital tools such as GPS-guided tractors and IoT sensors (small connected sensors) make precision agriculture possible. Farmers use fewer resources and do less damage, which disturbs habitats less and helps farming and nature exist side by side.

**Cities and infrastructure.** Urban ecosystems, with intensive land use, broken-up green spaces and introduced species, often have lower biodiversity than natural areas. Urbanisation is the third most common threat to species on the IUCN Red List. It pushes out native plants and animals, reducing their numbers and variety. The physical and chemical barriers it creates also stop species moving around, cutting them off from food, shelter and mates. Urban green spaces, including parks, gardens and green roofs, are important habitats for many native species and support pollinators, birds and other animals.

## Ecosystem Restoration

Restoration means repairing ecosystems that human activity has damaged or changed. Through hands-on work such as replanting forests, rehabilitating habitats and bringing back native species, it aims to undo environmental damage.

**Land and freshwater.** Around 50% of the world's population lives with severe water stress for at least one month a year, and 25 countries, home to a quarter of the world's people, have highly stressed water supplies. Digital tools can help restore land and freshwater through precision reforestation: AI analysis helps choose the best places to plant, supporting the recovery of varied ecosystems and better water quality. Danone-AQUA and Microsoft's partner Jejak.in, for example, use digital tools to measure, monitor and support the cleanup of Indonesia's Citarum River.

**Oceans.** Restoring ocean ecosystems matters for the planet and for people, because oceans regulate the Earth's climate, support biodiversity and provide essential resources. According to the IPCC, the ocean has absorbed about 30% of human-made carbon dioxide since pre-industrial times, which has made seawater more acidic and threatens marine life. Overfishing, pollution and habitat destruction have driven biodiversity loss, with marine species populations falling by nearly 70% between 1970 and 2010, according to WWF. Digital tools help with coral reef mapping: remote sensing and machine learning track coral health and show where to focus repairs. Archireef, for instance, is restoring marine ecosystems by 3D-printing reef tiles.

**Food systems.** Returning farmland to natural ecosystems helps protect biodiversity, bring back ecosystem services and limit climate change. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), natural ecosystems have been converted to farmland at a huge rate, with approximately **12 million hectares** lost each year. Digital tools support restoration by encouraging agroecological practices. AI-guided precision farming reduces farming's footprint, improves soil health and helps bring native plants and animals back onto farmland.

**Cities and infrastructure.** Restoring urban ecosystems can do a lot for people's well-being. It improves air quality, eases the urban heat island effect (cities being hotter than the countryside around them), and gives people places to relax and learn. Restored urban spaces such as green roofs, parks and tree-lined streets store carbon, helping offset urban emissions. They also make cities more resilient to climate change by lowering temperatures and improving local microclimates. Urban planners increasingly use AI to design green spaces that help native plants return and that sit comfortably alongside local wildlife.

## Sustainable Use of Biodiversity

Sustainable use means using natural resources responsibly, so that we meet today's needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs. It goes further than conservation and restoration because it deals with human development as a whole. It covers practices that keep ecosystems healthy and productive in the long term, and it recognises that ecological processes, society's needs and the economy are all linked.

**Earth Overshoot Day** marks the date in each year when humanity's demand for nature's resources and services exceeds what the Earth can regenerate in that year. In 2023, it fell on 2 August.

**Land and freshwater.** Fast urbanisation, industrial growth and more intensive farming have set off a chain of environmental effects, degrading land and making water scarcer. This overuse brings soil erosion, deforestation and drained aquifers (underground water stores), harming food security and communities as well as the environment. Around **1.6 billion people** depend on forests for their livelihoods, and forests hold the highest levels of biodiversity on land, so sustainable land use matters directly for people. AI, remote sensing and data analysis let us monitor and manage land and water with great precision, so decisions can be made earlier and on better information. Smart water systems use IoT devices to track water quality, use and leaks, helping save water.

**Oceans.** The UN's Sustainable Development Goals Report 2023 describes the oceans as in a state of emergency, because of more eutrophication (nutrient pollution that causes algal blooms), acidification, warming and plastic pollution. Overfishing has depleted more than a third of global fish stocks. Digital tools help use ocean biodiversity sustainably through marine spatial planning, underwater drones and AI analysis, which help manage fisheries responsibly, prevent overfishing and protect marine habitats.

**Food systems.** The world is far off track on its goal to halve food waste and losses per person by 2030. Much of the problem comes from inefficient supply chains and distribution, which lose food at every stage from farm to plate. AI analysis helps reduce food waste through better supply chain management and stock tracking, while digital tools cut water use and chemical inputs and make farming more sustainable overall. TCS Research's digital twin platform, for example, helps tackle food waste by estimating the freshness of fruit and vegetables in real time.

**Cities and infrastructure.** Cities, growing quickly and filling with more people, drive unsustainable use of resources. The rising demand for energy, water and raw materials for infrastructure, housing and transport puts heavy pressure on ecosystems.
