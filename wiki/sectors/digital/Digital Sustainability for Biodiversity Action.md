# Digital Sustainability for Biodiversity Action

## What is it

By 2030, the world will require ever more resources, including more water, food and energy, and these growing requirements will exacerbate biodiversity loss. A key challenge is reconciling human development with environmental stewardship, and the only way to ensure a resilient future for the planet and its communities is through the conservation, restoration and sustainable use of biodiversity.

**Ecosystems** and **biodiversity** are related but distinct: the biodiversity seen today is the result of 4.5 billion years of evolution, and increasingly, of human influence too. It constitutes the interconnected web of life within which humans play an integral role, and is essential to the processes that support all life on Earth, including human life.

## Why it matters

Human action is accelerating biodiversity loss. The WWF's 2022 Living Planet Report found an average 69% decline in the population of mammals, birds, fish, reptiles and amphibians since 1970. Human well-being, health and economic prospects are critically dependent on biodiversity and natural systems, and the evidence unequivocally shows biodiversity in decline (see also [what's happening to coral reefs](../../concepts/key-ideas/FACTS%20on%20EVERYTHING%20-%20Under%20the%20Sky%20we%20Make.md#coral-reef-nearly-gone)).

There are glimmers of hope. The rate of deforestation fell globally by about a third between 2010 and 2020 compared with the previous decade, and the planet's protected areas have expanded significantly over the past decade, from about 10% to 15% of land and from about 3% to 7% of marine areas. Key Biodiversity Areas, sites that play a significant role in maintaining biodiversity worldwide, have seen a 15% increase in protection.

## The Global Biodiversity Framework

On 19 December 2022, 196 countries reached agreement on the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF). This sets an ambitious path towards a global vision of a world living in harmony with nature by 2050, and provides a framework for global action on biodiversity, paving the way for a nature-positive and sustainable world. The framework calls for the effective conservation and management of at least 30% of the world's lands, inland waters, coastal areas and oceans, and the restoration of 30% of degraded ecosystems, by 2030 (often referred to as the "30x30" target). In adopting the GBF, all parties committed to setting national targets to implement it, based on 23 global targets. Its success depends on countries' ability to effectively plan, monitor, report on and review implementation at the national level, with review happening each year at the relevant COP.

Digital transformation can play a pivotal role in advancing the GBF's objectives, by enhancing the capacity to collect, analyse and disseminate crucial environmental data, and by deploying solutions that reduce threats to biodiversity at speed and scale. The [UN Biodiversity Lab](https://unbiodiversitylab.org/en/) is one example of an interactive monitoring map already doing this.

## Conservation of Biodiversity

Conservation is the proactive effort to protect and maintain existing biodiversity within ecosystems. It involves establishing and managing protected areas, implementing legal measures to prevent over-exploitation, and running educational initiatives to raise awareness of biodiversity's significance. Beyond the direct benefits of biodiversity, food, fibre, firewood and pharmaceuticals, there are indirect benefits delivered through ecosystem services such as pollination, pest control, climate moderation and flood control.

The **Biodiversity Intactness Index (BII)**, maintained by the Natural History Museum, measures biodiversity change using abundance data on plants, fungi and animals worldwide, showing how local terrestrial biodiversity responds to human pressures such as land use change and intensification. Only the areas that have retained enough natural biodiversity have a BII above 90%, the proposed planetary boundary for this measure.

**Land and freshwater.** Forests are among the largest carbon and biodiversity reservoirs on Earth, crucial for mitigating climate change and providing essential goods, services and livelihoods. According to WWF's 2023 Extracted Forests report, mining and its associated infrastructure is one of the largest drivers of deforestation and biodiversity loss, with approximately 20% of mining sites situated in biodiversity hotspots. Some 84% of global direct mining-related deforestation (MRD) over the past 20 years occurred in just 10 countries.

**Oceans.** Unprecedented sea surface temperatures have been associated with marine heatwaves, periods of unusually high ocean temperatures that can have significant, sometimes devastating, impacts on ocean ecosystems and biodiversity, and can carry serious socio-economic consequences through effects on fisheries, aquaculture, tourism and other industries. A Digital Twin of the Ocean (DTO), a multi-dimensional, near real-time virtual representation of the ocean, is helping to inform decisions backed by science and data to protect marine environments.

**Food systems.** Global food systems are a direct driver of accelerating biodiversity loss. Food production is degrading or destroying natural habitats and contributing to species extinction, driven by the continued conversion of natural or semi-natural ecosystems into managed ones, and the use of unsustainable agricultural practices at farm, landscape and global level. Digital technologies such as GPS-guided tractors and IoT sensors enable precision agriculture, minimising resource use and reducing environmental impact, which in turn supports biodiversity conservation by mitigating habitat disruption and fostering coexistence between agriculture and natural habitats.

**Cities and infrastructure.** Urban ecosystems, characterised by intensified land use, green space fragmentation and the introduction of non-native species, often have lower biodiversity than natural environments. Urbanisation is the third most prevalent threat to species on the IUCN Red List: it displaces native flora and fauna, diminishing species abundance and diversity, while the physical and chemical barriers it creates hinder species movement, limiting access to food, shelter and breeding partners. Urban green spaces, including parks, gardens and green roofs, function as crucial habitats for diverse native species and support pollinators, birds and other animals.

## Ecosystem Restoration

Restoration is a targeted approach to repairing and rejuvenating ecosystems that have been degraded or altered by human activities. Through active interventions such as reforestation, habitat rehabilitation and the reintroduction of native species, restoration seeks to reverse the negative impacts of environmental damage.

**Land and freshwater.** Around 50% of the world's population lives under highly water-stressed conditions for at least one month a year, and 25 countries, home to a quarter of the world's population, sit in areas with highly stressed water access. Digital transformation can facilitate ecosystem restoration in land and freshwater systems through precision reforestation technologies: AI-driven analysis aids in selecting optimal planting locations, supporting the revival of diverse ecosystems and improved water quality. Danone-AQUA and Microsoft's partner Jejak.in, for example, deploy digital solutions to measure, monitor and support the cleanup of Indonesia's Citarum River.

**Oceans.** Ocean ecosystem restoration is crucial for both planetary and human well-being, given the critical role oceans play in regulating the Earth's climate, supporting biodiversity and providing essential resources. According to the IPCC, the ocean has absorbed about 30% of anthropogenic carbon dioxide since the pre-industrial era, causing acidification that threatens marine life. Overfishing, pollution and habitat destruction have driven biodiversity loss, with marine species populations declining by nearly 70% between 1970 and 2010, according to WWF. Digital transformation supports restoration through coral reef mapping technologies, where remote sensing and machine learning help monitor coral health and target interventions to restore damaged reefs. Archireef, for instance, is restoring marine ecosystems by 3D-printing reef tiles.

**Food systems.** Restoring agricultural land to natural ecosystems is vital for preserving biodiversity, restoring ecosystem services and mitigating climate change. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the conversion of natural ecosystems to farmland has been staggering, with approximately 12 million hectares lost annually. Digital tools contribute to restoration by promoting agroecological practices: AI-guided precision farming minimises the ecological footprint, enhances soil health, and supports reintroducing native flora and fauna to agricultural landscapes.

**Cities and infrastructure.** Urban ecosystem restoration can yield significant benefits for human well-being, improving air quality, mitigating urban heat island effects, and providing recreational and educational opportunities. Restored urban ecosystems, such as green roofs, parks and tree-lined streets, contribute to carbon sequestration, helping offset urban emissions, while also enhancing climate resilience by reducing temperatures and improving urban microclimates. Urban planning tools increasingly leverage AI to design green spaces that support the reintroduction of native plant species and create environments that coexist harmoniously with local wildlife.

## Sustainable Use of Biodiversity

Sustainable use involves the responsible, balanced use of natural resources to meet current needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own. It goes beyond conservation and restoration by addressing the broader context of human development, encompassing practices that keep ecosystems healthy and productive over the long term, and recognising the interconnectedness of ecological processes, societal needs and economic activity.

**Earth Overshoot Day** marks the date when humanity's demand for ecological resources and services in a given year exceeds what the Earth can regenerate in that year. In 2023, it fell on 2 August.

**Land and freshwater.** The rapid pace of urbanisation, industrial expansion and agricultural intensification has set in motion a cascade of environmental repercussions, triggering land degradation and intensifying water scarcity. This overexploitation poses severe threats, including soil erosion, deforestation and aquifer depletion, impacting not only the environment but also food security and community well-being. Around 1.6 billion people's livelihoods depend on forests, where the highest levels of terrestrial biodiversity are found, making sustainable land use critical to human well-being. AI, remote sensing and data analytics allow land and water resources to be monitored and managed with unprecedented precision, enabling proactive, informed decision-making, while smart water management systems use IoT devices to monitor water quality, consumption patterns and leaks, promoting efficient use and conservation.

**Oceans.** The UN's Sustainable Development Goals Report 2023 finds oceans in a state of emergency due to increased eutrophication, acidification, ocean warming and plastic pollution, with overfishing depleting more than a third of global fish stocks. Digital transformation supports sustainable use of ocean biodiversity through marine spatial planning, underwater drones and AI-driven analysis, which help monitor and manage fisheries responsibly, preventing overfishing and protecting marine habitats.

**Food systems.** The world is severely off track in its efforts to halve per capita food waste and losses by 2030, a problem driven significantly by inefficient supply chain and distribution systems that cause considerable food loss at every stage, from production to consumption. AI-driven analytics help minimise food waste through efficient supply chain management and inventory tracking, while digital tools optimise water usage, reduce chemical inputs and enhance overall agricultural sustainability. TCS Research's digital twin technology platform, for example, helps tackle food waste by estimating the freshness of fruits and vegetables in real time.

**Cities and infrastructure.** Cities, with their rapid urbanisation and expanding populations, are drivers of unsustainable resource use. The rising demand for energy, water and raw materials to support infrastructure, housing and transportation places immense stress on ecosystems.
