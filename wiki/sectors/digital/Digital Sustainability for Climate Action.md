# Digital Sustainability for Climate Action

## What is it

This page looks at how digital technology and climate change are connected, and at how much difference going digital can make to climate action, both in cutting emissions (mitigation) and in coping with the changes already happening (adaptation).

It covers four things: how digital tools can enable, scale up and speed up mitigation and adaptation; where digital technology and climate change meet across key systems; how digital technology itself harms the environment and the climate; and how to get the most out of digital tools for climate action while keeping that harm small.

The World Economic Forum's Exponential Climate Action Roadmap estimates that digital technologies could help cut global carbon emissions by **up to 15%**, around a third of the reduction needed by 2030. That would come from solutions in energy, manufacturing, agriculture and land use, buildings, services, and transport and traffic management. Digital technologies also matter for adaptation. They give government agencies, humanitarian groups and ordinary people timely information for early warnings and better disaster management, so they can make better decisions. New technologies that can scale, combined with nature-based solutions, could help the world jump ahead to something cleaner and more resilient. The downsides of using much more technology need to be kept in view alongside the benefits.

## Enabling Climate Change Mitigation through Digital Transformation

Every modelled global pathway that limits warming to 1.5°C needs fast, deep and, in most cases, immediate cuts in greenhouse gas emissions across all sectors this decade. Independent tracking by the [Climate Action Tracker](https://climateactiontracker.org/) shows that countries' current pledges and policies still fall well short of the Paris Agreement goals.

Digital tools can help close that gap in several places:

- According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), better energy efficiency could cut global energy-related carbon emissions by 40% by 2040. Smart grids and IoT devices (small connected sensors and machines) can help use energy better, waste less and run more efficiently.
- Using renewable energy could reduce CO2 emissions by 70% by 2050.
- Carbon accounting is still imprecise, and accurate numbers are needed to track progress and hold people to account. AI-driven monitoring ("AI of Things") can help close this gap.
- A circular economy could reduce annual CO2 emissions from key industrial materials by 40% by 2050.

## Digital Transformation Poses Challenges Too

Global data centre electricity demand tripled between 2000 and 2018, and is expected to triple again by 2030. E-waste keeps growing fast: **53.6 million tonnes** were produced worldwide in 2019, much of it unrecorded and unaccounted for. Throwing electronics away harms the environment, and it also wastes materials that could have been recovered and recycled.

## Enabling Climate Change Adaptation through Digital Transformation

Digital tools can support climate adaptation quickly and at scale, reducing loss of life, hardship and damage to the environment.

### Early warning systems save lives

Countries with limited early warning coverage can have up to **eight times higher** disaster death rates than countries with substantial to full coverage, which shows how much these systems are worth. Around a third of the world's population, mostly in the least developed countries, is still not covered by early warning systems. Digital tools can reach remote areas and cover disaster-prone regions properly. Satellite images, geographic information systems (GIS, digital mapping) and IoT devices can gather large amounts of data on different hazards, pick out vulnerable areas and track environmental conditions in real time. SMS alerts, mobile apps and social media can then get warnings to people in time, so they can prepare and respond better. One example is FEWEAS (Flood Early Warning and Early Action System), an app that monitors a river basin in Indonesia.

### Climate information and data management

Climate data is scattered across many platforms and formats, which makes it hard to piece together. Digital tools can help bridge this gap, so decision-makers have what they need to plan good adaptation strategies. They make it possible to build decision-support tools that help policymakers, planners and practitioners make evidence-based choices on infrastructure, water management, targeted adaptation and resilience to climate change. Real-time satellite data, for instance, supports mangrove conservation in Zanzibar. Useful data portals include the [World Bank Climate Change Knowledge Portal](https://climateknowledgeportal.worldbank.org/), which provides global data on past and future climate, vulnerabilities and impacts; the [WRI climate data platform](https://www.wri.org/data/overview-100-climate-data-platforms), which links to more than 80 climate data platforms; and [Climate Watch](https://www.climatewatchdata.org/), which tracks progress against the Paris Agreement.

### Remote sensing and mapping

Remote sensing means collecting data about places or objects from a distance, using satellites or other instruments, including aerial photography, satellite images and radar. It makes it possible to map, monitor and model changes in land use, vegetation and land cover. That information is essential for understanding how climate change affects ecosystems and biodiversity. Compared with observing land on the ground, remote sensing covers much wider areas.

### Climate resilient infrastructure

Much of today's infrastructure (buildings, roads, bridges, airports, railway lines, power stations and telecom towers) was built before climate change and adaptation were taken seriously in design, so it is vulnerable to today's weather. By building remote sensing, IoT devices and satellite images into infrastructure systems, engineers and planners can spot weak points, check structures are sound, and design infrastructure that copes better.

### Virtual and augmented reality

Virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) could change how climate impacts are shown, turning abstract projections into something decision-makers and the public can see and feel.

### Community engagement

Community engagement matters for change across society because it puts people at the centre. Local, grassroots action is often the first spark for larger movements, working alongside top-down rules and political action. Involving a wide range of community groups also helps fairness, because everyone gets a voice. Government acting alone, by contrast, can produce badly planned policies that meet resistance. Engagement builds a strong social mandate, which opens the way for ambitious climate policies and helps businesses, governments and civil society agree on choices that benefit the wider community.
