# Digital Sustainability for Climate Action

## What is it

This module examines the relationship between digital transformation and climate change, exploring the transformational shift digitalisation can bring to climate action, in both climate change mitigation and adaptation.

By the end of it, the aim is to understand the role of digital transformation in enabling, scaling and accelerating measures for climate change mitigation and adaptation, appreciate the linkages between digital transformation and climate change across key systems, understand how digital technologies themselves create negative environmental and climate impacts, and reflect on how to unlock the full potential of digital transformation for climate action while minimising those negative impacts.

The World Economic Forum's Exponential Climate Action Roadmap estimates that digital technologies can help reduce global carbon emissions by up to 15%, around a third of the reduction required by 2030, through solutions in energy, manufacturing, agriculture and land use, buildings, services, and transportation and traffic management. Digital technologies are also crucial to climate adaptation, as they support government agencies, humanitarian organisations and citizens in making better decisions through timely, effective information for early warning and enhanced disaster management. Scalable new technologies, coupled with nature-based solutions, can enable a leapfrog to a cleaner, more resilient world, though the potential pitfalls of scaling up technology use need to be kept in view alongside its benefits.

## Enabling Climate Change Mitigation through Digital Transformation

Global modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C require rapid, deep and, in most cases, immediate greenhouse gas emissions reductions across all sectors this decade. Independent tracking by the [Climate Action Tracker](https://climateactiontracker.org/) shows that current country pledges and policies still fall well short of what is needed to meet the Paris Agreement goals.

Several opportunities stand out for where digital transformation can help close that gap:

- According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), improving energy efficiency could cut global energy-related carbon emissions by 40% by 2040. Smart grid systems and IoT devices can help optimise energy use, reduce waste and improve efficiency.
- The use of renewable energy could reduce CO2 emissions by 70% by 2050.
- Carbon accounting and management remain imprecise, and accurate numbers are needed to track progress and create accountability. AI-driven monitoring ("AI of Things") can help close this gap.
- A circular economy could reduce annual CO2 emissions from key industry materials by 40% by 2050.

## Digital Transformation Poses Challenges Too

Global data centre electricity demand tripled between 2000 and 2018, and is expected to triple again by 2030. E-waste also continues to increase significantly, with 53.6 million metric tonnes produced globally in 2019, much of it unregistered and unaccounted for. The disposal of electronic waste not only has negative environmental impacts, but also represents a missed opportunity for resource recovery and recycling.

## Enabling Climate Change Adaptation through Digital Transformation

Digital transformation can be leveraged to support climate change adaptation efforts at scale and speed, minimising loss of life, hardship, and damage to the environment.

### Early warning systems save lives

Countries with limited early warning coverage can have up to eight times higher disaster mortality than countries with substantial to comprehensive coverage, underlining the benefits of strengthening these systems. Around a third of the world's population, mainly in least developed countries, is still not covered by early warning systems. Digital technologies can help reach remote areas and provide comprehensive coverage in disaster-prone regions: satellite imagery, geographic information systems (GIS) and IoT devices can gather vast amounts of data on various hazards, identify vulnerable areas, and monitor environmental parameters in real time, while SMS alerts, mobile apps and social media platforms can deliver timely notifications, enabling people to prepare and respond more effectively. One example is FEWEAS (Flood Early Warning and Early Action System), an app monitoring a river basin in Indonesia.

### Climate information and data management

Climate data is scattered across many different platforms and formats, making it a genuine puzzle to piece together. Digital transformation can help bridge this gap, ensuring decision-makers have the pieces needed to devise effective climate change adaptation strategies. Digital technologies enable the development of decision-support tools to assist policymakers, planners and practitioners in making informed, evidence-based decisions on infrastructure development, water management, targeted adaptation strategies, and improved resilience in the face of climate change. Real-time satellite data, for instance, underpins mangrove conservation efforts in Zanzibar. Useful data portals include the [World Bank Climate Change Knowledge Portal](https://climateknowledgeportal.worldbank.org/), which provides global data on historical and future climate, vulnerabilities and impacts, the [WRI climate data platform](https://www.wri.org/data/overview-100-climate-data-platforms), which links to more than 80 climate data platforms, and [Climate Watch](https://www.climatewatchdata.org/), which tracks progress against the Paris Agreement.

### Remote sensing and mapping

Remote sensing involves acquiring data about objects or scenes from a distance, using satellites or other instruments, including aerial photography, satellite imagery and radar. This enables mapping, monitoring and modelling of changes in land use, vegetation and land cover, information that is crucial for understanding the impact of climate change on ecosystems and biodiversity. Compared to direct land observation, remote sensing offers much wider spatial coverage, making it possible to monitor larger areas.

### Climate resilient infrastructure

Much existing infrastructure, buildings, roads, bridges, airports, railway lines, power stations and telecom towers, was built before climate change and climate adaptation were serious design considerations, leaving it vulnerable to today's weather events. By integrating remote sensing, IoT devices and satellite imagery into infrastructure systems, engineers and planners can detect vulnerabilities, monitor structural integrity, and design more resilient infrastructure.

### Virtual and augmented reality

Virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) have real potential to revolutionise how climate impacts are visualised, making abstract projections tangible for decision-makers and the public.

### Community engagement

Community engagement is vital for systemic change, as it puts people at the centre of societal transformation. Grassroots-level actions are the initial sparks for transformative movements, complementing top-down mandates and political action, and engaging diverse community groups helps ensure equity by giving everyone a voice. Isolated government action, by contrast, can lead to poorly planned policies that face resistance. Engagement fosters a strong social mandate, paving the way for transformative climate policies and encouraging businesses, governments and civil society to align on choices that benefit the wider community.
