Bitcoin mining uses huge amounts of energy and water. A single transaction uses enough water to fill a swimming pool and has the same carbon footprint as 700,000 Visa transactions. Bitcoin's global electricity use is growing by 27% a year.

Banning mining in one country does not help. It moves the problem somewhere with dirtier energy, as China's ban showed. The argument that mining runs on renewables is only partly true, because most mining still happens in countries that depend on fossil fuels.

Other cryptocurrencies have shown that cutting energy use by 99% is technically possible. Bitcoin has chosen not to follow. Without stronger regulation, carbon pricing or pressure from users, its environmental footprint will keep growing.

### Key Numbers

- **16,000 litres of water** are used per Bitcoin transaction, roughly enough to fill a swimming pool
- One Bitcoin transaction has a carbon footprint equal to 700,000 Visa transactions or 50,000 hours of YouTube
- Bitcoin mining has produced an estimated 200 million tonnes of CO2 since it began, with 92% of that in the last four years
- Bitcoin uses around 0.6-0.7% of total global electricity, and already 2.3% in the United States
- Its electricity use is growing by 27% a year
- For comparison, in Ireland data centres (not Bitcoin mining) used 23% of metered electricity in 2025, up from 18% in 2022

---

### What Is the Problem?

Bitcoin relies on a process called mining, in which banks of specialised computers check transactions and create new coins. This takes enormous amounts of energy and water, and the person making a transaction sees none of it.

The scale is hard to picture. Since Bitcoin was launched, its total carbon emissions are estimated at **200 million tonnes of CO2**. Most of this happened recently, as more people started using it. The water figures are just as large: each transaction uses roughly as much water as a swimming pool holds.

The growth rate is the main worry. This footprint is not stable or under control. It is growing fast, and the recent approval of Bitcoin exchange traded funds (ETFs) in the United States is likely to push demand up further.

---

### Why Banning or Taxing Does Not Solve It

Several countries have responded by restricting or taxing Bitcoin mining. **China brought in a broad ban, and the US government has looked at taxation** as a way to limit the environmental harm.

The trouble is that banning mining in one country does not reduce global emissions. The activity just moves elsewhere, often to countries with weaker environmental rules and more reliance on fossil fuels.

China's ban shows this clearly. Much of the mining that left China moved to Kazakhstan, which depends heavily on coal and other fossil fuels for its electricity. At its peak in 2021, Kazakhstan hosted around 18% of the global Bitcoin mining industry; by the end of 2025 its share had fallen to about 2%. The emissions did not disappear. They moved somewhere with a higher carbon intensity.

---

### The Renewable Energy Argument Has Limits

Some people argue that Bitcoin mining can be made clean by running it on renewable energy. This is partly true, but it needs a careful look.

> Around 40% of Bitcoin mining took place in the United States and Kazakhstan at the end of 2025 (about 38% and 2%). Both countries generate a large share of their electricity from fossil fuels. The claim that mining runs on renewables applies to a limited portion of the industry.

There is also a wider problem: competition for clean energy. When data centres and mining operations use large amounts of renewable electricity, that power is no longer available to households or other businesses. Ireland is already seeing this. New permits for data centres have been paused because the sector's electricity demand has grown so fast that it risks crowding out other users.

Places like Iceland are different. There, electricity comes almost entirely from geothermal and hydropower, and supply is less tight. But Iceland is an exception, not a model that can be copied worldwide.

---

### Is There a Better Way?

Other cryptocurrencies have shown that Bitcoin's approach is not the only one. **Ethereum, one of the largest digital currencies after Bitcoin, changed its underlying system in 2022 and cut its energy use by more than 99.9% overnight.** Newer currencies, such as Ecocoin, are being designed from the start to use little energy and no water.

These examples show that cryptocurrency systems can be built with a much smaller environmental footprint.

Bitcoin, however, has shown little interest in changing. Its community argues that switching systems would weaken the security and decentralisation that define it. That may be a real technical limit. It is worth knowing, though, that early messages suggest Bitcoin's original developers knew about the environmental concerns as far back as 2009 and chose not to act on them.

---

### What Would Actually Help?

There is no simple fix, but several things could make a real difference:

- Stronger carbon pricing that reflects the true cost of energy-hungry mining
- Rules requiring mining operations to find and bring their own renewable energy, rather than drawing from existing grids
- Pressure from users and investors on exchanges and funds to account for environmental impact
- Continued work on more efficient protocols and alternative currencies

Cryptocurrency is unlikely to disappear. But like any other energy-hungry sector, it will need to become more efficient if it is to have a credible place in a lower-carbon economy.
