# Digital4Sustainability Module 1: Digital Transformation for Sustainable Development

## What is it

This is a pivotal moment for how the world manages technology. Societies can either choose to harness digital technologies to build a sustainable future, or allow unchecked digitalisation to accelerate the drivers of the Triple Planetary Crisis: climate change, nature loss and pollution. Digitalisation can be a powerful driver for the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, but left unchecked, it can also accelerate the very problems it could help solve.

**Digitization** entails converting information from analogue to digital form. **Digitalisation** is the use of digital technologies to turn products and services into a digital format, driving efficiency and innovation. **Digital transformation** is broader still: a systems-level economic, societal and environmental transformation triggered by widespread digitalisation. It unfolds as new possibilities emerge from replacing non-digital processes with digital ones, or replacing older technology with next-generation digital technology that enables genuinely new innovation and creativity, rather than simply enhancing and supporting traditional methods.

From a sustainability perspective, the transformation needs to be both digital and green at once. Digital technology can positively influence around 70% of the 169 Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) targets, but digitisation is at the same time inherently energy- and material-intensive, and is fuelling unsustainable consumption and exacerbating inequalities.

## Why it matters

The evidence keeps mounting that a global economic system based on maximising shareholder value, while it has driven impressive growth rates and brought high living standards to many, has come at an enormous cost to people and the planet. Getting digital transformation right requires deliberate choices, not passive adoption. Projects need to involve many stakeholders, and critically, local communities, while both the public sector and the private sector need to build competencies that span digital and sustainability literacy. Policymakers need to understand the environmental opportunities and risks of digital transformation, while entrepreneurs, computer scientists and engineers need to understand the environmental impact of their own products and services. Digital and sustainability literacy also needs to be built into academic curricula and vocational training.

## What is CODES

The **Coalition for Digital Environmental Sustainability (CODES)** aims to steer the digital age towards a just and sustainable transition, through three systemic shifts:

1. Align digital transformation and sustainability around a common purpose, through standards, training and joint action
2. Mitigate the negative impacts of the digital age, such as emissions, e-waste and the digital divide
3. Accelerate digital innovation for sustainability, through innovation labs, the circular economy and a just transition

## Mitigating the Negative Impacts of Digital Transformation

### Energy use and emissions

Computers, mobile phones, internet servers and crypto-mining operations require enormous amounts of energy. Data centres in particular use vast amounts of energy and large quantities of water, which can strain local water supplies (see [IRELAND Datacentre Dilemma](../../ireland-hub/DRAFT%20-%20IRELAND%20Data%20Centre%20Dilemna.md) for what this looks like in practice). The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) estimated that ICT's share of global electricity consumption was projected to reach 3.2% in 2020, and that share is forecast to keep rising. This matters especially for data centres and the recent AI boom, which is straining power access in the US and slowing the renewable energy transition elsewhere, including in Ireland. Total ICT electricity demand is rising, and data centres are likely to account for a growing share of that demand in the coming years. Addressing this requires sustainable, decentralised renewable energy infrastructure to power data centres, better carbon footprint accounting, and more green data centres as part of a sustainable digital transformation.

### Waste generation

Beyond the energy needed to run devices and data centres, manufacturing them and meeting rising demand requires large-scale metal extraction. To meet this growing demand, extraction of minerals and metals such as graphite, lithium and cobalt could increase by 500% by 2050, severely impacting land, water, air and biodiversity. When poorly governed, mining can destroy natural landscapes, pollute groundwater, and displace indigenous people. Ultimately, these minerals and metals, fused together into electronic devices, often end up in large e-waste dumping sites (see [Carbon Footprint of an iPhone](DRAFT%20-%20Carbon%20Footprint%20of%20an%20iPhone.md) for what this looks like for a single device). In 2019, a record 53.6 million metric tonnes of e-waste was produced globally; this figure is expected to more than double, to around 120 million tonnes, by 2050. It is usually the developing and least developed parts of the world where this e-waste ends up.

### Addressing consumption drivers

Digital consumption has never been higher, and the emergence of one-click buying on platforms like Amazon has exacerbated the underlying problem of overconsumption.

### Tackling misinformation

True stories take roughly six times longer to reach 1,500 people than false stories do to reach the same number. As seen during COVID-19, misinformation travels fast, and trying to correct it after the fact with fact-checking has proven largely ineffective. Debunking and fact-checking fake news can lack effectiveness because once people have been exposed to a falsehood, it is difficult to correct. Analogous to medical immunisation, "**prebunking**", pre-emptively warning people and exposing them to weakened doses of misinformation, can help cultivate "mental antibodies" against fake news.

### Guarding against the digital divide

Access to the internet is unequal across location, age, gender and socio-economic status. This is widening a gap and slowing the benefits of digital transformation for entire demographics. If left unaddressed, the gap between under-connected and hyper-digitised communities will keep widening, exacerbating existing inequalities.

### Preventing rights violations

Three domains linked to environmental and social sustainability need specific attention in the digital space:

1. **Resource conflicts.** Many regions are witnessing conflicts between indigenous or local communities who want to preserve the natural resources that maintain their livelihoods, and mining operations extracting the metals and minerals, such as cobalt, graphite, copper and rare earth metals, that are vital to producing the electronics fuelling digital transformation.
2. **Digital surveillance.** Several nations are cracking down on activists and journalists through surveillance of their personal and professional lives.
3. **Violation of digital privacy.** As more websites, applications and software gain a global footprint, they are being exploited by many actors to collate information and data about people.

## Accelerating Innovation

Some notable examples of digital innovation supporting the planetary fight:

- [Climate TRACE](https://www.climatetrace.org/) harnesses satellite imagery, other forms of remote sensing, artificial intelligence and collective data science expertise to track human-caused greenhouse gas emissions as they happen.
- [Destination Earth](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/destination-earth), part of the European Commission's Green Deal and Digital Strategy, aims to develop a highly accurate digital model of the Earth at a global scale, to monitor and predict the interaction between natural phenomena and human activities.
- NVIDIA's [Earth-2 (E-2)](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/2021/11/12/earth-2-supercomputer/) plans to be the world's most powerful AI supercomputer dedicated to predicting climate change, creating a digital twin of Earth in the Omniverse.

Digital transformation can also be the lever that drives a much-needed transition to a sustainable circular economy. Intelligent assets, connectivity and monitoring play a crucial role in scaling circularity, and a [**Digital Product Passport**](https://wupperinst.org/en/a/wi/a/s/ad/7784) that tracks a product's climate, environmental and social impacts throughout its value chain could be a game changer for rapidly scaling this transition.

### Empowering sustainable consumption

Unsustainable consumption results from rising demand for fossil fuels, growing carbon emissions, and deepening inequalities, all of which have profound negative implications for people and the planet. As global greenhouse gas emissions increase due to pollution and industrial processes, so do global temperatures, and extreme weather events such as heatwaves, droughts and irregular rainfall patterns become more frequent. These events place an extra burden on agriculture and food production, which raises food prices when combined with overconsumption and economic shocks, compromising food accessibility and undermining food security. In 2019, the amount of materials used to meet global demand for goods and services, known as Domestic Material Consumption, amounted to 95.1 billion metric tonnes. Most of this is not adequately managed, generating increasing waste and pollution.
