# FACTS about AI, Emissions Impacts and Mitigation Potential

AI is often discussed as either a climate saviour or a climate villain, and the honest picture sits somewhere in between: it has real, quantified potential to help cut emissions in specific applications, while its own energy and water footprint, especially from data centres, is growing fast enough to worry regulators, utilities and Big Tech's own sustainability teams. This page collects the facts on both sides.

## AI's mitigation potential

The [International Center for Environmental Technology Transfer (ICEF)](https://www.icef.go.jp/roadmap/) estimates that **AI has the potential to help mitigate 5 to 10% of global greenhouse gas emissions by 2030**. This figure comes from ICEF's Artificial Intelligence for Climate Change Mitigation Roadmap, the twelfth in a series of roadmaps the organisation has produced on climate mitigation technologies, alongside topics such as ammonia and biomass.

## Satellite detection of methane leaks

One concrete example of AI-adjacent technology helping mitigation is **MethaneSAT**, a satellite developed by the Environmental Defense Fund (EDF) with a consortium of universities and aerospace firms. It orbits the planet 15 times a day, gathering data on methane emissions from human and natural sources. EDF's Steven Hamburg says the satellite was designed explicitly to produce policy-relevant data tracking methane emissions from the oil and gas industry globally.

Assessing countries' progress on methane pledges has historically been difficult, because leaks are hard to track and aerial or on-the-ground surveys are expensive, and not permitted everywhere. MethaneSAT joins a growing constellation of methane-sensing instruments in orbit: the European Space Agency's TROPOMI senses emissions across large regions, while Canadian company GHGSat runs 11 instruments focused on identifying specific point sources. MethaneSAT sits between these two scales, aiming to quantify emissions across whole oil and gas production areas and map their likely sources. (Source: New Scientist.)

## AI and flying: contrail avoidance

Contrails have a complex, still-debated effect on the climate, covered in more depth in [Flight Carbon Footprint and Offset Guide](../transport/Flight%20Carbon%20Footprint%20and%20Offset%20Guide.md#What%20about%20Contrails%3F). Google is one of the only flight carbon calculators that integrates contrails into its emissions estimates, though as that page explains, doing so reliably is tricky.

One general estimate suggests re-routing aircraft to avoid the specific atmospheric conditions that cause contrails, mainly by changing altitude, could increase fuel consumption by around 5% while cutting contrail formation by around 50%.

**Google Project Contrails**, a Google AI initiative, goes further: it uses machine learning on weather, satellite and flight data to predict where contrails are likely to form, letting pilots adjust flight paths to avoid them with a much smaller fuel penalty. In test flights with American Airlines, AI predictions **reduced contrails by 54%**, at a fuel cost of just **0.3% more fuel across an airline's fleet**. The estimated cost of this avoidance is **$5 to $25 per tonne of CO2e**, making it a comparatively cheap climate intervention. The project is still under development, with plans to expand its models to more geographical areas. (Sources: Google Research; Sustainability Magazine.)

AI may also help in other ways: reducing the standard landing approach distance from 2.5 to 2 miles, which sounds simple but is operationally difficult, would cut the fuel burned by planes circling or waiting to land. AI-managed traffic flow during the step-down descent approach could help achieve this. At airports, sensors can detect emissions from planes idling at the gate, since a parked plane burns an estimated 60 to 80 gallons of fuel an hour, and prompt engines to be shut down sooner, which also improves air quality for ground staff. (Source: video report, see Sources.)

Put together, these various AI-enabled interventions have been estimated to have the potential to cut air transport industry emissions by as much as 50% by 2030.

## The cost side: AI's own energy and water footprint

AI's own infrastructure carries a real climate cost. Data centres running AI workloads are straining electricity grids badly enough that some countries have begun blocking planning permits for new sites. In Ireland, data centres already use more than **18% of national electricity**, a figure expected to reach **32% by 2026** (see [IRELAND Data Centre Dilemma](../../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Data%20Centre%20Dilemna.md) for the fuller picture).

Training large AI models is highly resource-intensive, and cooling the data centres that run them is highly water-intensive. There are also open questions about generative AI's rapid growth into simple everyday tasks that could just as easily be handled by a standard web search. Companies have also been criticised for not disclosing accurate figures on AI's environmental cost, which makes it harder for the public and policymakers to make informed decisions.

In April 2025, Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark estimated that **50 gigawatts of new power capacity will be needed by 2027** to support AI, roughly equivalent to building 50 new nuclear power plants. (Source: CNBC.)

## Google and Microsoft revise their climate targets

Google and Microsoft, along with their cloud computing peers, have both published reports acknowledging they are not on track to meet their own sustainability targets, largely because AI's energy demand outpaced what their existing renewable energy contracts could cover. Both companies have turned increasingly to nuclear power purchase agreements as a response (see [Google Emissions, "Turning to nuclear power"](../../concepts/company-evaluations/GOOGLE%20Emissions.md#turning-to-nuclear-power)).

Google's data centre energy consumption rose 17% in the most recent year measured at the time of reporting. Key findings from that period:

- **Google's emissions rose nearly 50% since 2019, including 13% in a single year (2023).**
- **Microsoft's emissions rose nearly 30% since 2020.**
- Both companies attribute the rise to higher energy consumption for AI data centres.
- **Microsoft's energy intensity (electricity used per dollar of revenue) rose 20%.**
- **Google's carbon intensity (emissions per dollar of revenue) climbed 101% since 2019.**
- Both companies are investing in efficiency improvements for AI model training, but the long-term impact remains uncertain.
- The rapid expansion of hyperscale data centres for AI has raised broader concerns about whether the tech industry can still meet its climate goals, and whether efficiency gains will simply enable more processing rather than net emissions savings.

## US data centre energy consumption

A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report found that data centre electricity use in the United States rose from **1.9% of total annual electricity consumption in 2018 to 4.4% in 2023**, more than doubling. The same report projects this could reach **between 6.7% and 12% of total US electricity consumption by 2028**, driven substantially by AI.

A separate analysis of **2,132 US data centres (78% of the national total)** found that data centre emissions have tripled since 2018, reaching **105 million metric tonnes of CO2** for the 12 months ending August 2024, around **2.18% of national emissions** (for comparison, domestic commercial airlines emit roughly 131 million tonnes). Energy consumption by these centres has also doubled since 2018, now accounting for **4.59% of all US energy use**. The researchers note that the ongoing shift to more complex "multimodal" AI, involving images, video and audio, requires exponentially larger data volumes, and that **95% of US data centres are located in areas with dirtier-than-average energy sources**, giving them a **48% higher carbon intensity** than if they were evenly distributed. They have built a public data portal to track these emissions, though they anticipate little regulatory action in the near term. (Sources: ResearchGate; MIT Technology Review.)

## Water and voices in the debate

AI's water footprint is also significant. Ecologie360 reports that a single AI conversation uses around **1 litre of water** for cooling, and that Google's data centres consumed **28 billion litres of water in 2022**, up 82% from 2018. Bringing greater efficiency to AI models will not fully solve this on its own, since gains in efficiency tend to be offset by increased use (the Jevons Paradox again). Chip and processing power supply constraints are another limiting factor, though the emergence of more efficient training approaches like DeepSeek's may be shifting that conversation.

Sasha Luccioni, AI researcher and Climate Lead at Hugging Face, offers a widely cited framing of the debate. Her view is that individual ChatGPT use is not, on its own, a major problem today, but could become one if it replaces simple tasks like calculations that need very little compute. The bigger issue, she argues, is large models that consume large amounts of energy, and the fact that data centres are absorbing renewable energy capacity that would otherwise support the broader energy transition. On the case for AI as an efficiency tool, she is sceptical that efficiency gains alone are a strong argument, since increased efficiency tends to increase demand rather than reduce total energy use. She has called for greater corporate accountability, arguing that on climate and other issues, "we just don't have the numbers to make informed decisions." A similar argument features in the Hard Fork podcast's coverage of AI's environmental impact: hosts have cited estimates that a single ChatGPT query can use around ten times the energy of a standard Google search, and that training one large AI model can produce emissions comparable to several hundred long-haul flights and several times the lifetime emissions of a car, while questioning how many everyday AI use cases (image generation, grocery lists, and similar) genuinely need the technology.

## Signs of hope

As with cryptocurrency, some AI developers are already demonstrating dramatic reductions in water and electricity use through better design and greater efficiency, and these are worth supporting where they can be identified.

France has also positioned itself as a hub for AI investment. Startups including ContentSquare, Dataiku, Ivalua, Meero, Mirakl and Spendesk have strengthened the country's wider deep-tech landscape, and Mistral AI's record-breaking **€385 million funding round** highlighted the scale French AI companies can now reach. (Source: Business France.)

## Reference figures

A few standalone figures are useful for putting individual AI and digital activities in context:

- **100 Google searches produce around 20 grams of CO2.** (Search Engine Land)
- **One hour of streaming video produces approximately 36 grams of CO2.** (Wired)
- **Generating 1,000 images with an advanced AI model produces roughly the same emissions as driving an average car for 4.1 miles.** (MIT Technology Review)
