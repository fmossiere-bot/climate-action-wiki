# FACTS about AI, Emissions Impacts and Mitigation Potential

AI is often described as either a climate saviour or a climate villain. The real picture sits somewhere in between. AI has real, measured potential to help cut emissions in specific uses. At the same time, its own energy and water footprint, mostly from data centres, is growing fast enough to worry regulators, utilities and Big Tech's own sustainability teams. This page collects the facts on both sides.

## AI's mitigation potential

The [Innovation for Cool Earth Forum (ICEF)](https://www.icef.go.jp/roadmap/) estimates that **AI could help cut 5 to 10% of global greenhouse gas emissions by 2030**. The figure comes from ICEF's Artificial Intelligence for Climate Change Mitigation Roadmap, the twelfth in a series of roadmaps the organisation has written on climate mitigation technologies, alongside topics such as ammonia and biomass.

## Satellite detection of methane leaks

One concrete example of AI-related technology helping cut emissions is **MethaneSAT**, a satellite developed by the Environmental Defense Fund (EDF) with a group of universities and aerospace firms. It circles the planet 15 times a day, collecting data on methane emissions from human and natural sources. EDF's Steven Hamburg says the satellite was designed specifically to produce data that policymakers can use to track methane emissions from the oil and gas industry worldwide.

Checking countries' progress on their methane pledges has always been hard. Leaks are difficult to track, and surveys from the air or on the ground are expensive and not allowed everywhere. MethaneSAT joins a growing group of methane-sensing instruments in orbit. The European Space Agency's TROPOMI senses emissions across large regions, while the Canadian company GHGSat runs 11 instruments that pinpoint individual sources. MethaneSAT sits between these two scales: it aims to measure emissions across whole oil and gas production areas and map where they probably come from. (Source: New Scientist.)

## AI and flying: contrail avoidance

Contrails, the white trails planes leave in the sky, have a complex effect on the climate that is still debated. This is covered in more depth in [Flight Carbon Footprint and Offset Guide](../transport/Flight%20Carbon%20Footprint%20and%20Offset%20Guide.md#What%20about%20Contrails%3F). Google is one of the only flight carbon calculators that includes contrails in its emissions estimates, although, as that page explains, doing so reliably is tricky.

One general estimate suggests that re-routing aircraft away from the specific conditions that create contrails, mainly by changing altitude, could increase fuel use by around 5% while cutting contrail formation by around 50%.

**Google Project Contrails**, a Google AI project, goes further. It uses machine learning on weather, satellite and flight data to predict where contrails are likely to form, so pilots can adjust their route to avoid them with a much smaller fuel penalty. In test flights with American Airlines, AI predictions reduced contrails by 54%, at a cost of just 0.3% more fuel across an airline's fleet. The estimated cost of this avoidance is **$5 to $25 per tonne of CO2e**, which makes it a comparatively cheap climate measure. The project is still being developed, with plans to extend its models to more parts of the world. (Sources: Google Research; Sustainability Magazine.)

AI may help in other ways too. Shortening the standard landing approach from 2.5 to 2 miles sounds simple but is hard to do in practice. It would cut the fuel burned by planes circling or waiting to land, and AI-managed traffic flow during the step-down descent could help make it happen. At airports, sensors can detect emissions from planes idling at the gate (a parked plane burns an estimated 60 to 80 gallons of fuel an hour) and prompt crews to shut engines down sooner, which also improves air quality for ground staff. (Source: video report, see Sources.)

Taken together, these AI-enabled measures have been estimated to have the potential to cut the air transport industry's emissions by as much as 50% by 2030.

## The cost side: AI's own energy and water footprint

AI's own infrastructure has a real climate cost. Data centres running AI are straining electricity grids so much that some countries have started refusing planning permission for new sites. In Ireland, data centres used **23% of metered electricity in 2025**, up from 18% in 2022 and 5% in 2015, according to the Central Statistics Office. They are forecast to reach about a third of national electricity use by the end of the decade (see [IRELAND Data Centre Dilemma](../../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Data%20Centre%20Dilemna.md) for the fuller picture).

Training large AI models uses a lot of resources, and cooling the data centres that run them uses a lot of water. There are also open questions about generative AI spreading into simple everyday tasks that a standard web search could handle just as well. Companies have been criticised for not publishing accurate figures on AI's environmental cost, which makes it harder for the public and policymakers to make informed decisions.

In April 2025, Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark estimated that **50 gigawatts of new power capacity** will be needed by 2027 to support AI, roughly the same as building 50 new nuclear power plants. (Source: CNBC.)

## Google and Microsoft revise their climate targets

Google and Microsoft, along with other cloud computing companies, have published reports admitting they are not on track to meet their own sustainability targets. The main reason is that AI's energy demand grew faster than their existing renewable energy contracts could cover. Both companies have turned more and more to nuclear power purchase agreements in response (see [Google Emissions, "Turning to nuclear power"](../../concepts/company-evaluations/GOOGLE%20Emissions.md#turning-to-nuclear-power)).

Google's data centre energy use rose 17% in the most recent year measured at the time of reporting. Key findings from that period:

- Google's emissions rose nearly 50% since 2019, including 13% in a single year (2023).
- Microsoft's emissions rose nearly 30% since 2020.
- Both companies put the rise down to higher energy use for AI data centres.
- Microsoft's energy intensity (electricity used per dollar of revenue) rose 20%.
- Google's carbon intensity (emissions per dollar of revenue) climbed 101% since 2019.
- Both companies are investing in making AI model training more efficient, but the long-term effect is uncertain.
- The rapid growth of very large ("hyperscale") data centres for AI has raised wider concerns about whether the tech industry can still meet its climate goals, and whether efficiency gains will simply allow more processing instead of cutting emissions overall.

## US data centre energy consumption

A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report found that data centres' share of US electricity rose from **1.9% of total annual electricity consumption in 2018 to 4.4% in 2023**, more than doubling. The same report projects this could reach between 6.7% and 12% of total US electricity consumption by 2028, largely because of AI.

A separate analysis of 2,132 US data centres (78% of the national total) found that data centre emissions have tripled since 2018. They reached **105 million tonnes of CO2** for the 12 months ending August 2024, around 2.18% of national emissions. For comparison, domestic commercial airlines emit roughly 131 million tonnes. Energy use by these centres has also doubled since 2018 and now makes up 4.59% of all US energy use. The researchers note that the move to more complex "multimodal" AI, which handles images, video and audio, needs far larger amounts of data, growing exponentially. They also found that 95% of US data centres are in areas with dirtier-than-average energy, giving them a 48% higher carbon intensity than if they were spread evenly. They have built a public data portal to track these emissions, though they expect little action from regulators in the near term. (Sources: ResearchGate; MIT Technology Review.)

## Water and voices in the debate

AI also uses a lot of water. Ecologie360 reports that a single AI conversation uses around **1 litre of water** for cooling, and that Google's data centres used 28 billion litres of water in 2022, up 82% from 2018. Making AI models more efficient will not solve this on its own, because efficiency gains tend to be cancelled out by more use (the Jevons Paradox again). Limits on the supply of chips and processing power are another constraint, though more efficient training methods such as DeepSeek's may be changing that conversation.

Sasha Luccioni, an AI researcher and Climate Lead at Hugging Face, offers a widely quoted way of looking at the debate. In her view, individual ChatGPT use is not a major problem today on its own, but it could become one if it replaces simple tasks, such as calculations, that need very little computing. The bigger issue, she argues, is large models that use large amounts of energy, and data centres taking up renewable energy that would otherwise support the wider energy transition. She is sceptical of the argument that AI will pay for itself through efficiency, because greater efficiency tends to increase demand rather than cut total energy use. She has called for more corporate accountability, arguing that on climate and other issues, "we just don't have the numbers to make informed decisions." The Hard Fork podcast made a similar case in its coverage of AI's environmental impact. The hosts cited estimates that a single ChatGPT query can use around ten times the energy of a standard Google search, and that training one large AI model can produce emissions comparable to several hundred long-haul flights and several times the lifetime emissions of a car. They also questioned how many everyday AI uses (image generation, grocery lists and so on) really need the technology.

## Signs of hope

As with cryptocurrency, some AI developers are already showing large cuts in water and electricity use through better design and greater efficiency. Where they can be identified, they are worth supporting.

France has also set itself up as a hub for AI investment. Start-ups including ContentSquare, Dataiku, Ivalua, Meero, Mirakl and Spendesk have strengthened the country's wider deep-tech scene, and Mistral AI's record **€385 million funding round** showed the scale French AI companies can now reach. (Source: Business France.)

## Reference figures

A few standalone figures help put individual AI and digital activities in context:

- 100 Google searches produce around 20 grams of CO2. (Search Engine Land)
- One hour of streaming video produces approximately 36 grams of CO2. (Wired)
- Generating 1,000 images with an advanced AI model produces roughly the same emissions as driving an average car for 4.1 miles. (MIT Technology Review)
