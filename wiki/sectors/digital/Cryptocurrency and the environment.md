## What is cryptocurrency mining, and why does it matter?

Most talk about cryptocurrency and the environment focuses on Bitcoin, but it helps to start wider. There are now **over 10,000 active cryptocurrencies**. The ones with the biggest environmental footprint have one thing in common: they use a system called proof-of-work to check transactions.

Under proof-of-work, computers race each other to solve complex mathematical puzzles. The first to solve the puzzle gets to add the next block of transactions to the chain, and earns a coin reward for doing so. The catch is that this race never stops. It runs worldwide, 24 hours a day, and uses huge amounts of electricity. The more the coins are worth, the more miners join, and the more energy is used.

The rest of this article looks at how big that energy use is, and what it means for the climate, water supplies and waste.

## The global carbon footprint of crypto


Cryptocurrency mining now accounts for around 0.6-0.7% of global electricity consumption and around 0.2% of global CO2-equivalent emissions, according to the Centre for International Governance Innovation. Those shares sound small, but the absolute numbers are large, and they are rising.

Bitcoin alone used approximately 121 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity in 2023, and projections point to over 130 TWh by 2025. Across all proof-of-work cryptocurrencies, the mining industry produces an estimated 139 million tonnes of CO2-equivalent a year once every greenhouse gas source is counted.

The IMF has warned that by 2027, crypto mining could account for 0.7% of total global CO2 emissions. That looks like a small share, but it would still be more than the entire annual emissions of many countries.

> 139 million tonnes CO₂-equivalent: the estimated annual GHG footprint of the global cryptocurrency mining industry, according to recent research


The growth does not follow a straight line. A 400% rise in Bitcoin's price between 2020 and 2021 was followed by a 140% jump in the network's electricity use. The pattern repeats: higher prices attract more miners, more miners need more energy, and more energy means more emissions. Crypto's environmental impact is closely tied to its market value, and that value has been climbing.

## Where the mining happens and why that matters



Electricity is not all the same. A mining operation running on Norwegian hydropower has a very different carbon footprint from one running on coal in **Kazakhstan**, so where mining happens matters a great deal.

The **US is now the largest single mining location, with roughly 38% of global Bitcoin** mining activity at the end of 2025. Kazakhstan, Russia, Canada and Malaysia are the other major hubs. **China, which once dominated the sector, banned mining in 2021**, and the ban had consequences nobody intended.

When China closed its doors, mining operations moved fast. **Kazakhstan became a main destination almost overnight, at one point hosting around 18% of global Bitcoin mining.** The reasons were simple: plenty of coal and gas power, cheap electricity, cold winters that cut cooling costs, and rules that at first welcomed the industry. For miners, it ticked every box.

The result was a more carbon-intensive global network, because Kazakhstan's grid runs largely on fossil fuels. The new arrivals also strained the country's electricity system, causing power shortages and leading the authorities to restrict mining. Kazakhstan shows a wider pattern: mining goes where energy is cheap, and cheap energy is usually fossil fuel energy.

> _"Because countries use different sources of energy to generate electricity, their electricity production impacts on climate, water, and land are not the same." (United Nations University research, 2023)

## Venezuela: a different kind of crypto story


Venezuela shows what can go wrong when a government uses digital currency as a way round political and financial problems, rather than as a new tool.

Facing economic collapse and international sanctions, President Maduro launched the Petro in 2018. It was a state-backed cryptocurrency, supposedly tied to oil reserves, and was presented as a way to get around US sanctions and steady the economy. Even Venezuela's own central bank refused to accept it. After six years of failed attempts, the Petro was quietly shut down in 2024.

What came next was arguably more worrying. Between 2023 and 2024, Venezuelan authorities seized approximately $500 million worth of Bitcoin from mining operations, reportedly moving it into state control. In May 2024, the government banned private Bitcoin mining entirely. It blamed pressure on the electricity grid, while at the same time gathering crypto assets under state management.

Venezuela is not a major mining hub in the way Kazakhstan is. It is more a warning about how cryptocurrency can be used to dodge financial oversight, fund authoritarian governments and evade sanctions. Without proper rules, the line between innovation and exploitation is thin.

## Deregulation and financial risk: a growing concern

The environmental problems with cryptocurrency are real. There is also a second set of risks that deserves as much attention: the financial and governance risks that come with weak oversight.

Cryptocurrency has long been used for money laundering, fraud and sanctions evasion. Not by every user, but at a scale that is impossible to ignore. In 2025, crypto-linked illicit financial flows reached an estimated $158 billion globally, an all-time high and more than triple the 2024 figure, according to Kroll's financial compliance research.

The timing matters. In early 2025, the US Department of Justice disbanded its dedicated cryptocurrency enforcement unit. The IRS team overseeing anti-money laundering compliance at crypto firms dropped to its lowest staffing level since 2017. Several enforcement actions against crypto companies were quietly dropped.

Financial compliance analysts describe the result as "a more permissive environment for illicit actors."

> $158 billion: estimated global illicit crypto-linked financial flows in 2025, an all-time high (Kroll, 2026)


This matters for businesses and sustainability professionals in a way that is not always obvious. Supply chain due diligence, Scope 3 reporting and anti-corruption checks are all areas where exposure to crypto assets, whether through payments, investments or treasury holdings, can create hidden risks. If your pension fund holds Bitcoin ETFs, or a supplier accepts crypto payments, the financial and reputational exposure is there whether or not it appears in a standard risk register.

## Bitcoin versus the rest: why the design choice matters


It is worth being precise here, because not all cryptocurrencies have the same footprint.

Bitcoin still uses proof-of-work. Its community has consistently resisted switching to a lower-energy system, arguing that the current one is central to its security and decentralisation. A single Bitcoin transaction in 2025 is estimated to use around 707 kWh of electricity, roughly what an average European household uses in two months.

**Ethereum, the second-largest cryptocurrency by market value, made a different choice**. In September 2022, it switched from proof-of-work to proof-of-stake, a system where validators put up existing coins as a stake instead of competing with computing power. The effect was immediate: Ethereum's energy consumption dropped by more than 99.9% overnight. Ethereum transactions can now be validated on an ordinary laptop.

>  **More than 99.9%: reduction in Ethereum's energy consumption after switching to proof-of-stake in 2022 (ethereum.org, citing the Crypto Carbon Ratings Institute)**

> Other cryptocurrencies, including Solana, Cardano and Avalanche, were built on proof-of-stake from the start. The technology for this change exists and has been proven at scale. Bitcoin could switch too. Staying with proof-of-work is a deliberate choice, not a technical limit.

Early correspondence from Bitcoin's pseudonymous creator Satoshi Nakamoto, now in the public domain, suggests that concerns about Bitcoin's environmental impact were raised as early as 2009 and dismissed. Whether that makes Bitcoin's footprint more or less acceptable is something each person will judge differently. But it does make the "we didn't know" argument harder to defend.

## The water and waste nobody talks about

Carbon gets most of the attention, but cryptocurrency mining also has large water and waste footprints that are rarely discussed.

Water first. Mining hardware gives off heat all the time, and cooling it takes water, both at the mining sites and at the thermal power plants supplying the electricity. Research published in Cell Reports Sustainability estimated that Bitcoin mining alone used over 1,600 gigalitres of water in 2021, rising to an estimated 2,237 gigalitres by 2023. A single Bitcoin transaction uses on average around 16,000 litres of water, roughly enough to fill a garden swimming pool.

Then waste. Mining relies on specialised chips called ASICs, which stop being profitable quickly as competition on the network grows. The average mining chip is replaced after around 1.3 years. It still works, but it is too slow to make money. Research published in Resources, Conservation, and Recycling estimated that this creates around 30,700 tonnes of e-waste a year, comparable to all the personal computing waste the Netherlands produces each year.

> 16,000 litres: the estimated average water use per single Bitcoin transaction, according to research in Cell Reports Sustainability

E-waste is a real problem. Many of the materials in mining hardware, such as lead, cadmium and lithium, are toxic. Less than 20% of global e-waste is currently recycled, and mining hardware is mostly built for a single purpose, so few parts can be reused.

## 2026 update: can quantum computing clean up crypto mining?

A 2026 experiment suggests quantum computers could one day cut the energy cost of cryptocurrency mining sharply, although there are big caveats.

Colton Dillion at Postquant Labs set up an experimental blockchain called **Quip**, running since April 2026. Bitcoin's proof-of-work is a brute-force race. Quip's validation task is instead an optimisation problem, the kind of complex scheduling or portfolio calculation that quantum computers handle better than conventional machines. A D-Wave Advantage2 quantum computer joins the network alongside ordinary computers to compete for blocks.

The early results are notable. Advantage2 is only available for about five minutes a day, yet it wins 92% of the blocks it competes for. It does so using roughly **12.5 watts**, compared with approximately 1,334 watts for a conventional machine working on the same problem: about 100 times less power. A conventional machine able to beat Advantage2 would need around 300 times more power than the quantum machine uses.

This hints at a future where proof-of-work cryptocurrencies move away from energy-hungry brute-force computing towards optimisation problems that quantum hardware can solve far more efficiently. Quip is also designed to be quantum-safe from the start. Most existing blockchains are not, and their encryption could eventually be broken by large enough quantum computers.

The caveats matter. Olivier Ezratty of the Quantum Energy Initiative points out that quantum hardware is very expensive to build and maintain, and that energy savings per calculation may not lead to lower total energy costs at scale. The experiment is also tiny: a single machine, for a few minutes a day. Scaling it up to a real financial network would be a very different challenge.
