Waste to energy plants burn household, farm and industrial waste at high temperatures to make electricity and heat. The idea makes some sense: it tackles two problems at once, cutting landfill and producing power. In practice it is more complicated.

> Project Drawdown, one of the most rigorous databases of climate solutions, rates waste to energy as "[not recommended](https://drawdown.org/explorer/deploy-waste-to-energy) " as a climate solution. The main problem is that burning waste releases greenhouse gases, much as burning fossil fuels does, and it is much less energy efficient than most alternatives. In regions where a lot of plastic waste is burned, emissions can even be higher than if the waste had simply gone to landfill.

The business model also has a built-in conflict. Waste to energy plants need a steady, reliable supply of waste to run efficiently. That creates the wrong incentive: **instead of cutting waste at source**, the system depends on keeping it coming. Some EU countries have taken this to its logical end and import waste from other countries to keep their incinerators going. That works directly against the circular economy principles the EU is trying to build in.

> Air pollution is another serious issue. Even with advanced filters, incineration releases pollutants including fine particles, heavy metals and toxic compounds. In many countries these plants tend to be built near lower-income or more vulnerable communities, which raises real questions of environmental justice.

Waste to energy incineration should be kept separate from other heat-based technologies that sometimes get grouped with it. Pyrolysis and gasification use heat to break down organic material without burning it directly. Project Drawdown assesses them separately, and they do considerably better on emissions and energy efficiency. They are different things, and the difference matters when weighing up policy or investment decisions.

Incineration may still have a limited role in dealing with what is left after recycling, hazardous waste or medical waste, particularly in dense cities where there is very little land for landfill. Even then, it needs strict regulation and constant monitoring, and should be a last resort rather than a mainstream answer.

So **waste to energy is a way of managing waste, not a climate solution**. Recycling, composting, producing less waste in the first place, and newer technologies like pyrolysis all deliver better results for emissions.
