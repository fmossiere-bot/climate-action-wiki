The Jevons Paradox was first described in 1865 to explain why more efficient steam engines led to _more_ coal being burned, not less. Today it is the main economic idea people use to understand the energy problem of the AI era.

This page looks at how the paradox applies to the green energy transition, the "DeepSeek" effect and the future of data centres.

### Summary:

_"In 1865, a British economist warned that saving coal would only lead to burning more of it. Today, as we celebrate 'efficient' AI models that can run on a fraction of the power, we are building gigawatt-scale data centers at a record pace. We haven't solved the energy problem; we've just made the resource too valuable to stop using."_

### 1. The Core Tension: Efficiency vs. Scale

In the 19th century, William Stanley Jevons noticed that when coal was used more efficiently, it did not save coal. Instead, coal-powered work became so cheap that dozens of new industries took it up, and total use shot up.

In the 21st century, the same thing is happening with **GPU and model efficiency** (the chips and the AI software that runs on them):

- **The Hardware Loop:** Each new generation of chips (for example Nvidia's Blackwell) gives much more "compute per watt". In theory, that should lower energy bills. In practice, it lets developers train even larger models that used to be "too expensive", so total energy demand keeps climbing steeply.
- **The Software Loop:** The Chinese model DeepSeek (early 2025) showed that advanced reasoning could be done with a fraction of the computing power previously needed. Industry leaders such as Satya Nadella said this "efficiency" would probably trigger the Jevons Paradox. By making AI "cheap", it makes sure AI ends up **everywhere**, built into every app and device, which in the end means _more_ data centres.

### 2. The Data Center "Land Grab"

Data centres are the "smokestacks" of the AI revolution, and the paradox creates a particular problem for the green energy transition:

- **Hyper-growth vs. De-carbonization:** The IEA and Goldman Sachs forecast that data centre power demand could reach nearly **100 GW by 2027**. Tech giants such as Microsoft, Google and Amazon are the world's largest buyers of renewable energy, but AI is spreading faster than we can build wind and solar farms.
- **The "Firm Power" Problem:** AI needs power 24/7, while solar and wind come and go with the weather. So the extra demand is pushing a return to "firm" energy sources that run on demand. Nuclear energy is making a big comeback (for example Three Mile Island reopening for Microsoft), and, as a further twist, **natural gas** is being relied on to fill the gap while green infrastructure catches up.

### 3. The Rebound Effect


- **The Direct Rebound:** AI becomes 20% more energy-efficient, so companies simply run 40% more AI queries because it is cheaper.
- **The Indirect Rebound:** A company uses AI to optimise its logistics and saves millions in fuel. It then puts those millions into growing its business, which means more warehouses, more lorries and more total energy use.
- **The Green Transition Irony:** We use energy-hungry AI to _design_ better batteries and smarter grids, which is a "green" use. But if that AI makes green energy 50% cheaper, the Jevons Paradox suggests people will simply find 100% more ways to use that energy, which could cancel out the climate gains.

### 4. Expert Quotes & Context

- **Satya Nadella (Microsoft CEO):** In early 2025 he tweeted "Jevons Paradox strikes again!", arguing that as AI becomes a commodity, its "skyrocketing" use will turn it into a resource we can't get enough of.
- **Jensen Huang (Nvidia CEO):** He often argues that the world is moving from "general purpose computing" to "AI factories". In Jevons' terms, that is like moving from the "Newcomen engine" to the "Watt engine", a leap in efficiency that sets off a global industrial shift.
