> **Editorial summary:** Reuse — as opposed to recycling — is emerging as one of the most concrete ways for French construction to cut its carbon footprint. Ademe estimates a **30–40% cut** is achievable if doors, tiles, sanitary ware, panels and other elements are taken straight from demolition sites and refitted into new projects. This page files the original own-research write-up on the topic, drawing on Ecologie360 (2025) and covering the Blanchemaille project in Roubaix, Engie's new head office, RE2020 policy support, and the practical bottlenecks (insurance, certification, supply/demand matching). ## Summary

Reuse is gaining ground in French construction as a practical way to lower the sector's carbon footprint. Rather than recycling materials through industrial processing, reuse means taking doors, tiles, sanitary ware, lighting or flooring straight from a demolished or renovated building and fitting them into a new project. According to Ademe, this alone could reduce the sector's carbon impact by 30 to 40 percent, a meaningful number given that construction is responsible for close to a third of France's emissions.

> The renovation of Blanchemaille in Roubaix, the former head office of retailer La Redoute, is the clearest example so far. Over three years, the 13,000 square metre site was fitted out with wood doors, flooring, sanitary ware, carpets and lighting recovered mostly on site, along with roof tiles from demolished houses nearby and sanitary ceramics from the Kennedy tower, a 95 metre building torn down in Loos last July. The result: **more than 600,000 euros saved and close to 600 tonnes of CO2 avoided**. Neo-Eco, a firm specialised in circular economy project management, worked across both sites and made the material transfer easier to organise.

Reuse works best when demolition and construction are managed by the same company, or when a clear framework is set from the start. In Châtenay-Malabry, developer Eiffage wrote the deconstruction specifications itself and passed recovered materials on to social economy associations and local residents. In Metz, a former Printemps department store being converted into housing by Bouygues Immobilier's subsidiary Nouveau Siècle had its air vents, lighting, sanitary ware and doors carefully removed by cooperative Les Ecoopérateurs, then resold through a local community shop.

Material manufacturers are also entering the market. Saint-Gobain now offers reused ceiling panels through Eurocoustic and reused modular partitions through Clipper Coramine, which supplied 6,000 square metres of panels and 600 units of glazing for Engie's new head office in La Garenne-Colombes. Manufacturers bring an advantage here: they know their own products and have the equipment to test acoustic, thermal and mechanical performance before a second use.

The environmental case is well established. Reuse avoids the emissions tied to extracting, manufacturing and transporting new materials: up to 500 kilograms of CO2 per tonne of concrete, a 90 percent energy saving per tonne of metal, and 1,000 litres of water per cubic metre of wood. Under the RE2020 regulation, which sets increasingly strict carbon limits per square metre for new buildings, reused materials count as zero carbon, so a project's carbon intensity can fall by up to 30 percent. For companies with large property portfolios, such as major developers and social housing providers, this makes reuse a genuine tool for meeting decarbonisation targets, alongside its value for reputation and community engagement.

Even so, several obstacles remain. Selective removal of materials takes longer, costs more and requires specific training compared with standard demolition. Once removed, materials need cleaning, refurbishing, testing and often certification before they can be insured again, and insurance remains one of the hardest parts of the process. Certification has become somewhat easier over recent years, but most projects are still insured on a case by case basis. Not every material makes economic sense once these extra steps are costed in. Electrical and electronic products tend to remain attractive because they contain valuable materials, which is why Circable, a venture born inside construction group Vinci, has focused specifically on reusing electrical cables.

Reuse began in the office and retail sector, where budgets and timelines leave more room for it, and has been slower to reach housing. Bouygues Immobilier is trying to change that: its Opaline Green Fabrik' building in Pierrefitte sur Seine, with 1,000 square metres of reused bricks on the facade, is presented as proof that reused materials can look good and help shift public perception. Matching supply and demand remains the main practical challenge. Physical platforms such as Cyneo, set up by Bouygues, and digital ones such as BTP Match in the Paris region or Recyclo'Bat in Toulouse, are starting to address this, though industry figures argue that a national digital platform is still missing. Regional initiatives, including events run by the CD2E sustainable building centre in Loos en Gohelle, aim to bring buyers and sellers together and build new ways of managing building projects before shortages of key materials force the issue.
