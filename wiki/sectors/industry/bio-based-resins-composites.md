# Bio-Based Resins: Wood-Derived Composites That Beat Fossil Equivalents

## What is it

Composite resins are a kind of chemistry most people never hear about, yet we come into contact with them every day. **Epoxy** and polyester resins are the "glue" that holds glass or carbon fibres together in wind turbine blades, boat hulls, car body panels, roofing sheets, aircraft parts, sports equipment and reinforcements for roads and bridges. Both are made almost entirely from petroleum, and both are very hard to recycle. Once they have set, they form tightly linked chemical networks that cannot simply be melted down.

In March 2026, a team at the University of Oulu, Finland, reported the first **bio-based resins that are stronger than their fossil equivalents** and can also be chemically broken down and recovered as raw materials. The starting point is furfural, made from woody plant material (lignocellulosic biomass) such as sawdust and straw. The findings were published in *Composites Part B: Engineering*.

## Why it matters

Composite materials are one of the awkward, mostly hidden parts of the fossil economy. Every wind turbine blade built today contains fossil-derived epoxy that will last for decades and becomes a disposal problem at the end of its life. Until now, bio-based alternatives have been **weaker, impossible to recycle, or both**. The Oulu team says its result is the first to improve on both counts at once, and it uses existing chemical industry production lines, so factories do not need new equipment.

If it works at pilot scale, it would affect some of the most valuable parts of the industrial materials market. It also supports the case for **EU strategic autonomy**, meaning Europe relying less on others for key supplies. Europe holds less than 2% of global oil reserves, so replacing petroleum-based resins with raw materials from European forests and farms helps both the climate and Europe's independence.

## How it works

Conventional epoxy resin is built from **DGEBA** (diglycidyl ether of bisphenol A), a molecule made from petroleum. The Oulu team replaces DGEBA with diepoxides made from furfural, which comes from woody plant material. Furfural is a five-carbon aldehyde that has been made from plant sugars for over a century. What is new is turning it into a set epoxy that behaves like DGEBA.

The polyester work follows the same idea: start with the same type of bio-based building-block chemicals and run them through the existing polyester production process.

## The performance results

- The bio-based polyester resin showed **up to 76% higher tensile strength** (resistance to being pulled apart) than a commercial fossil-based polyester
- Glass fibre composites made with the bio-based epoxy were tougher and had significantly higher tensile and flexural (bending) strength than DGEBA composites
- Panels were **weather-tested in a harbour for 90 days**, facing water, UV light, temperature changes and humidity, and held up

## Chemical recyclability

The biggest difference comes at the end of the product's life. The furfural-based diepoxide has a **chemical structure that can be broken down**, so the composites can be taken apart and their components recovered as raw materials. Reporting on the work, Mikko Salonen describes it as the first bio-resin that combines high mechanical performance with chemical recyclability. Put simply, you can get the fibres and the resin back out when the product is finished with, instead of shredding the whole thing or sending it to landfill. That closes a loop the composites industry has left open for decades.

## What is next

For now the work is at the testing and validation stage. The team has filed **three patents** and is looking for industrial partners to move to pilot-scale production. Juha Heiskanen, one of the researchers, argues that once bio-based building-block chemicals are made at scale, they can be processed on existing lines and should not cost much more.

## Key facts

- Bio-based polyester: **76% higher tensile strength** than commercial fossil polyester
- Bio-based epoxy: stronger and tougher than conventional DGEBA composites, and **chemically recyclable**
- Raw material: **furfural from lignocellulosic biomass**, such as sawdust, straw, and leftovers from forestry and farming
- Uses **existing chemical industry production lines**, so no factory refit is needed
- **90 days** of harbour weathering with no breakdown
- Three patents filed; pilot production being sought
- Europe holds **<2% of global oil reserves**, so bio-based resins would help Europe supply more of its own materials
