# Bio-Based Resins: Wood-Derived Composites That Beat Fossil Equivalents

## What is it

Composite resins are a class of chemistry most people never hear about but come into contact with every day. **Epoxy** and **polyester** resins are the "glue" that holds glass or carbon fibres together to make **wind turbine blades, boat hulls, car body panels, roofing sheets, aircraft parts, sporting goods and civil engineering reinforcements**. Both are made almost entirely from petroleum, and both are notoriously difficult to recycle: once cured, they form tightly cross-linked networks that cannot simply be melted down.

In March 2026, a team at the **University of Oulu, Finland**, reported the first bio-based resins that both **outperform their fossil equivalents on mechanical strength** and can be **chemically broken down and recovered as raw materials**. The starting point is **furfural**, produced from lignocellulosic biomass such as sawdust and straw. The findings were published in *Composites Part B: Engineering*.

## Why it matters

Composite materials are one of the awkward, largely invisible strands of the fossil economy. Every wind turbine blade built today locks in decades of fossil-derived epoxy and, at end of life, becomes a disposal problem. The reason has always been that bio-based alternatives were **either weaker or unrecyclable** or both. This Oulu result claims the first combination that improves on both fronts at once, using **existing chemical industry production lines** so no factory retooling is needed.

If it holds up at pilot scale, this affects some of the highest-value slices of the industrial materials market. It also fits an **EU strategic autonomy** argument: Europe holds less than 2% of global oil reserves, so replacing petroleum-based resins with feedstocks from European forests and farms is both a climate and a sovereignty story.

## How it works

Conventional epoxy resin is built from **DGEBA** (diglycidyl ether of bisphenol A), a petroleum-derived molecule. The Oulu team replaces DGEBA with **furfural-derived diepoxides** produced from lignocellulosic biomass. Furfural is a five-carbon aldehyde that has been made from plant sugars for over a century; the new step is engineering it into a cured epoxy that behaves like DGEBA.

The wider polyester chemistry follows the same idea: start with the same class of bio-based platform chemicals and route them through the existing polyester production process.

## The performance results

- The bio-based **polyester** resin showed **up to 76% higher tensile strength** than a commercial fossil-based polyester
- Glass fibre composites made with the bio-based **epoxy** showed **improved toughness and significantly higher tensile and flexural strengths** than DGEBA composites
- Panels were **weather-tested in a harbour for 90 days** against water, UV, temperature and humidity and stood up

## Chemical recyclability

The single biggest difference is on end-of-life. Because the furfural-based diepoxide has a **degradable chemical structure**, the composites can be broken down and their components extracted as raw materials. Reporting on the work, Mikko Salonen frames it as the first bio-resin that combines high mechanical performance with chemical recyclability. In plain terms: **you can get the fibres and the resin back out** at the end of the product's life, rather than shredding the whole thing or landfilling it. This closes a loop that has stayed open in the composite industry for decades.

## What is next

For now the work is at the experimentation and validation stage. The team has filed **three patents** and is looking for industrial partners to move into pilot-scale production. Juha Heiskanen, one of the researchers, argues that once bio-based platform chemicals are produced at scale, they can be processed on existing lines and should not carry a significant price premium.

## Key facts

- Bio-based polyester: **76% higher tensile strength** than commercial fossil polyester
- Bio-based epoxy: **stronger and tougher** than conventional DGEBA composites, and **chemically recyclable**
- Feedstock: **furfural from lignocellulosic biomass** — sawdust, straw, forestry and agricultural sidestreams
- Uses **existing chemical industry production lines** — no factory retool
- **90 days** of harbour weathering with no breakdown
- Three patents filed; pilot production being sought
- Europe holds **<2% of global oil reserves** — bio-based resins as a material self-sufficiency play
