Concrete is the most used material on Earth after water, and making it accounts for around 8% of global greenhouse gas emissions. Most of the problem lies with cement, its main ingredient. Making cement releases CO2 through a chemical reaction, on top of the emissions from the energy it uses. That makes it very hard to decarbonise.

Demand is still growing, driven largely by the growth of cities in Asia, and the sector is currently off track on its climate targets.

Real solutions exist, including replacing clinker with other materials, new low-carbon cement technologies and carbon capture. But progress is slow, profit margins are thin, and none of these is yet used on a large scale.

Timber is a promising alternative for building, but only if the wood is sustainably sourced and properly protected.

Without stronger carbon pricing and clearer standards for what builders and governments buy, the gap between where the industry is and where it needs to be will stay hard to close.
### Key Numbers

- **8%** of global greenhouse gas emissions come from concrete production
- **4 billion tonnes** of cement are produced each year
- **10 billion tonnes** of concrete are used every year
- **660 kg of CO2** are released for every tonne of cement produced
- If the cement industry were a country, it would be the **third largest emitter** in the world, after China and the United States
- Global demand for cement is forecast to **increase by 48%** by 2050
- The cement sector is currently **off track** to meet Paris Agreement targets

### What Is the Difference Between Cement and Concrete?

People often use the two words as if they meant the same thing, but they do not.

> **Cement** is a binding agent made from limestone and clay. **Concrete** is a building material made by mixing cement with water, sand, and rock. Cement is the key ingredient in concrete, and it is also the source of most of its emissions.

Concrete is the most used material on Earth after water. It is hard to imagine modern construction without it.

---

### Why Is Cement So Polluting?

Cement's emissions come partly from the energy needed to make it, and partly from the chemistry itself.

To make cement, limestone is heated to very high temperatures in a kiln. This turns calcium carbonate into calcium oxide and gives off CO2 as a direct by-product. This chemical reaction alone accounts for 3 to 7% of global CO2 emissions, whatever fuel is used to heat the kiln.

> The main component produced in this process is called **clinker**. More than half of cement's CO2 emissions come from clinker production. Today, it takes roughly 660 kg of CO2 to produce one tonne of cement.

This is why cement is so hard to decarbonise. In many sectors the main task is switching to cleaner energy, but cement releases CO2 as part of the production process itself.

---

### The Scale of the Problem

A few facts help put this into perspective:

> - All the plastic produced in the past 60 years amounts to around 8 billion tonnes. The cement industry emits more than that every two years
> - China alone used more cement between 2011 and 2013 than the United States did in the entire 20th century
> - China currently uses around half of all the concrete produced globally
> - Production is still growing. The largest share of future demand is expected to come from India and other developing countries in Asia, driven by rapid urbanisation.

The impact carries on after the concrete leaves the factory. Concrete buildings soak up heat and give it off long after sunset, adding to the **urban heat island effect** in cities. This can make towns and cities several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside, and it makes worse the extreme weather that buildings are meant to protect us from.

There are serious indirect effects too. Global demand for sand to make concrete has caused severe environmental damage and, in some regions, violent conflict. Dust from cement works and building sites makes air quality worse and contributes to breathing diseases.

### What Are the Targets?

The Global Cement and Concrete Association (GCCA) has set these targets:

- **20% reduction in CO2 per tonne of cement by 2030**
- **25% reduction in CO2 per cubic metre of concrete by 2030**
- **Full decarbonisation by 2050**

These are ambitious goals. According to the World Resources Institute's 2023 tracker, the sector is currently well off track to meet them.

Several deep-rooted barriers stand in the way. A small number of large producers dominate the industry, and they are wary of new materials or processes. Margins are very thin, which limits investment in change. Where carbon pricing exists, it is not yet high enough in most markets to close the cost gap between conventional and low-carbon cement. And engineers, architects and clients are understandably cautious about using new building materials on large projects.

### How Can Cement Be Decarbonised?

There is no single solution. Progress will most likely come from combining several approaches.

**Clinker substitution** is the most established route. Replacing some of the clinker in a cement mix with other materials reduces the CO2 released. These substitutes include fly ash (a by-product of burning coal), ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBS), volcanic ash, ground limestone and certain clays. The LC3 process, for example, uses heated (calcined) clay and limestone and can cut CO2 emissions by up to 40% without losing performance.

**Alternative raw materials** offer another path. Seratech, a UK company, has developed a process that uses olivine, a common mineral, to partly replace clinker. Replacing 35% of the regular cement with silica made this way could produce carbon-neutral cement. Replacing 40% or more could make it carbon negative.

**Carbon capture and storage (CCS)** is seen by many in the industry as essential for full decarbonisation, especially for the process emissions that cannot be designed out. In Norway, Heidelberg Materials is building a CCS facility at its Brevik cement plant, aiming to capture 90 to 95% of the plant's emissions. The captured CO2 will be shipped and stored under the Norwegian seabed as part of the Longship project, a cross-border infrastructure scheme backed by the Norwegian government.

CCS is expensive, uses a lot of energy, and has a mixed track record in other sectors. For cement, though, it is currently the most advanced technology for the share of emissions that swapping materials alone cannot remove.

**Low-carbon cement start-ups** are also making progress. Dublin-based Ecocem is developing a technology called ACT that aims to cut cement's carbon intensity by up to 70% using widely available low-carbon materials, while working with existing cement plants. In the United States, companies such as Brimstone, CarbonBuilt and Fortera are bringing to market methods that either remove process emissions or capture CO2 during production and lock it into minerals.

### The Role of Alternative Materials

Changing what goes into cement is only one lever. **Timber** is one of the most discussed alternatives to concrete in construction. Wood stores carbon for the lifetime of the building, and replacing concrete with responsibly sourced timber can cut the emissions built into a building (its embodied emissions) significantly.

> One estimate suggests that substituting one cubic metre of concrete with treated wood saves around 1.1 tonnes of CO2 in avoided emissions, on top of the 0.9 tonnes already locked into the wood itself. A 10% increase in the share of European homes built with timber structural components could reduce CO2 equivalent to roughly 25% of the reductions set out under the Kyoto Protocol.

This only works if the wood is sustainably sourced and well protected, so the stored carbon is not released back into the air. Circular economy approaches, such as designing buildings to be taken apart and reusing modular parts, can also extend the life of materials and put off the emissions that come with demolition and rebuilding.

---

### Where Does This Leave Us?

Cement and concrete are central to how the world builds, and demand is still growing. The industry has set meaningful targets, and real technologies are in development. But progress is slow, investment is limited, and the sector is not currently on a path that fits climate goals.

A higher carbon price would help close the cost gap between conventional and low-carbon cement. Clearer purchasing standards, particularly for public construction, could speed up the take-up of alternatives. And continued investment in CCS and new materials will be needed for the share of emissions that substitution alone cannot cut.
