It sits alongside the [Environmental Impact of football world cups](Environmental Impact of football world cups.md), [Environmental Impact of the Paris Olympics](Environmental Impact of the Paris Olympics.md) and [Formula 1 Sustainability 2026](formula-1-sustainability-2026.md), and complements the [Wet-Bulb](../climate-science/DRAFT - Wet-Bulb explained.md) explainer on the physiology side.

## 1. A problem that runs in both directions

Sport and climate change affect each other in two distinct ways. On one hand, a warming planet is changing how and where sport can be played, and it is putting athletes and fans at growing health risk. On the other hand, sport itself, especially large events, adds to emissions through travel, construction and sponsorship. Jessica Murfree, a researcher at the University of North Carolina who studies the link between sport and climate, discussed both sides of this problem in a recent episode of the Zero podcast from Bloomberg.

## 2. The health risk to athletes

Elite athletes already push their bodies to the limit, so even small increases in heat can have an outsized effect. According to Murfree, heat-related illness in athletes is rarely caused by physical exertion alone. It usually results from a combination of the body's own heat production and the surrounding temperature. This combination can lead to a range of conditions, from mild heat exhaustion to heat stroke, which carries a real risk of death, particularly for people who put their bodies under strain, including athletes, farmers and construction workers.

Humidity makes the problem worse. The body normally cools itself through the evaporation of sweat, but when the air is already saturated with moisture, that sweat cannot evaporate properly, so it simply sits on the skin and warms up instead of cooling the body down. Combined with direct sunlight, this means the body spends more energy trying to regulate its own temperature rather than performing.

## 3. Which sports are most exposed

There is no strict ranking of which sports suffer most, but exposure tends to follow the traditional playing season. Sports that are typically played in warmer months, combined with frequent matches and heavy travel schedules, face higher exposure to heat-related risk. Baseball was mentioned as a clear example in the United States, alongside football, soccer and track and field. Cricket was also highlighted as a sport at particular risk, since it is played outdoors on large, uncovered pitches, often in regions that already experience high heat and humidity.

## 4. How sport is already adapting

Several changes are already visible. Some are structural, such as the hydration breaks added to matches at this year's World Cup, or the way the Tokyo Olympics moved its marathon further north to manage heat. Others are more gradual: rule changes to speed up play, shorter formats in cricket, and outdoor winter sports increasingly moving indoors, such as ice hockey, which historically began on frozen outdoor ponds.

Equipment and clothing are shifting too, with lighter padding and less coverage in athletic wear, partly for performance and partly to manage heat exposure. Playing surfaces are also affected. Heat and dryness change how grass and turf behave, which in turn changes ball bounce, player grip, and injury risk, a concern already raised in relation to Wimbledon and to stadiums built for American football being adapted for this year's World Cup.

## 5. Athletes as advocates

Beyond adaptation, some athletes are using their public profile to raise awareness of climate issues. Murfree pointed to a wider trend of growing athlete activism over the past five to ten years, helped by social media, though she noted that this remains concentrated among relatively few athletes and teams. She gave the example of a group of female football players who signed a protest against fossil fuel sponsorship in professional football. Australian rugby player and later senator David Pocock was cited as another example, having taken direct action against a coal mining project before moving into politics.

Murfree noted that speaking out carries real risk for athletes, given the potential for political backlash and fan alienation, and that athletes have historically been expected to stay within the boundaries of competition rather than raise social or environmental issues.

## 6. The emissions cost of major events

Large tournaments carry a heavy carbon cost. This year's World Cup, held across three countries with a record 48 teams, is expected to emit around 7.8 million tonnes of CO2, most of it linked to fan travel. Murfree was careful to note that this responsibility should not fall on fans alone, since the format, schedule and number of teams are decisions made at institutional level, not by supporters attending matches.

The 2022 World Cup in Qatar is cited as the second highest emitting tournament, for different reasons: rather than fan travel, its emissions came mainly from the construction of new stadiums and infrastructure built specifically for the event.

## 7. Rethinking how events are organised

One option raised is to rely more on a rotation of a small number of locations that already have the infrastructure in place, rather than building new venues for each edition, following an approach the International Olympic Committee has increasingly used. Other options include less frequent tournaments, smaller formats, or simply accepting that events do not need to keep growing in size each time. Murfree also pointed out that the economic benefits often promised to host communities do not always match what is actually delivered locally.

## 8. Sponsorship and fossil fuels

Fossil fuel sponsorship remains deeply embedded in major sporting events, from football to cycling to cricket. Murfree described the web of ownership between fossil fuel companies, financial institutions and media rights holders as very difficult to untangle, since many of the same companies and individuals are connected across all three. Beverage sponsors are one example: many of the bottled drinks sold at major events rely on plastic packaging, which is itself tied to the fossil fuel industry.

She was clear that there is no immediate, clean break available. Change is more likely to come gradually, through less frequent events, fewer commercial slots, and a slow shift away from sponsors that are tightly linked to fossil fuels.

## 9. Could the sporting calendar itself change

Qatar already demonstrated that a summer tournament can be moved to autumn to avoid extreme heat. Murfree suggested this kind of flexibility should be considered more widely, including for the Olympic Games, pointing out that sport has already been rescheduled or cancelled in the past for public health reasons. She also noted that the idea of a "summer sport" or "winter sport" is culturally specific. What counts as a summer sport in the northern hemisphere does not apply in the southern hemisphere, and as seasons shift with climate change, these associations may need to change too.

## Bottom line

Heatwaves are already changing how sport is played, from added hydration breaks to shifting event dates and locations. The health risks to athletes are real and, in some cases, life-threatening, particularly where heat combines with humidity. At the same time, sport itself contributes to the problem through travel, construction and sponsorship tied to fossil fuels. There is no single fix, but smaller, less frequent events, reuse of existing infrastructure, and a gradual shift away from fossil fuel sponsorship were all raised as realistic ways forward, alongside a growing, if still limited, willingness among athletes to speak up.
