# Myth: meat substitutes are too expensive

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

For chicken, mince and sausages the plant versions already cost about the same as meat in UK supermarkets, though bacon and burgers are still pricier. And lab-grown meat has got 50,000× cheaper since 2013, so the gap is closing.

## Why it sounds right

For some products it's true. Plant-based bacon does cost more than pork bacon in UK supermarkets, and plant burgers can cost noticeably more than beef burgers. Anyone who has bought both has seen it. The step too far is going from "some plant products cost more" to "plant substitutes are unaffordable", because the picture varies a lot from product to product.

## What is actually true

Comparing **UK supermarket prices per kg**:

- Chicken and plant-based chicken overlap heavily, and some plant versions are cheaper
- Plant and meat mince overlap in price
- Plant and meat sausages overlap too
- Burgers overlap a little, but plant burgers can cost noticeably more
- Plant-based bacon tends to cost more than pork bacon
- Fish is one of the more expensive meats either way

So plant-based substitutes aren't always more expensive, but on average they still cost more than the meat they replace, mainly because of bacon and burgers.

Lab-grown (cultivated) meat is on a steep downward price curve. In 2013 it cost about $2.4 million per kg. Today it's roughly $10-100 per kg, about 50,000× cheaper in around 10 years. The cost keeps falling along a curve similar to solar, wind and batteries. It still has to fall further to compete with chicken, and a lot further to compete with cheap beef.

There are two ways to close the gap: make substitutes cheaper, or make meat reflect its true cost. Making substitutes cheaper is the more realistic of the two.

## If they push back

**"Just tax meat to level the price."**

That is politically very hard. UK surveys show only about 1 in 5 people support a meat tax. The 2021 UK National Food Strategy consultation found it was the least popular of all its ideas, and it split the panel members. A meat tax could also hurt the roughly **3 billion people worldwide who can't currently afford a full healthy diet**.

**"Nothing will change unless meat gets more expensive."**

There's another route. Moving existing farm subsidies away from meat and dairy and towards plant proteins would shift prices in a similar way to a meat tax. It tends to be easier to accept, because it isn't a new tax, only public money spent differently.

**"Lab-grown meat is a fantasy for the rich."**

At $10-100/kg it's still expensive, but its cost has fallen faster than any food technology in memory. It looks like solar's path: early buyers pay high prices, then larger-scale production brings costs down steeply. The bigger obstacle now is **regulation** rather than cost. Singapore and the US have approved cultivated meat for people, the UK allows it only in pet food, and Italy and some US states have banned it outright.

## Go deeper

**The learning curve does the work.** Meat substitutes have followed a familiar pattern: expensive at first, then cheaper as production grows. Plant burgers went from restaurant-only in 2016 to supermarket staples by 2020. Lab-grown meat is on the same path, 8-10 years behind. Each generation gets cheaper because making more of something teaches manufacturers how to make it for less.

**Regulation is the real bottleneck for cultivated meat.** Only Singapore and the US currently allow lab-grown meat as food for people. The UK allows it only for pets. Italy has banned it, and so have several US states. Without approval the industry can't grow, and without growth prices won't fall far enough to compete. This is a policy fight more than a technology one.

**Precision fermentation.** Microorganisms are already used to make specific proteins (such as insulin), fats, vitamins and flavours cheaply. Applied to food, this could make protein at very low cost, possibly cheaper than plant-based products. Companies such as Perfect Day (dairy proteins), Formo (cheese) and Nature's Fynd are already selling products. This is the technology most likely to bring substitute prices below meat within a decade.

**What you can do.** Buy meat substitutes now. Every kilo bought tells the market there is demand, brings in more competition and pushes prices down further. It's the same way solar got cheap: early buyers helped pay for the learning. One person's shopping is a small nudge, but it does move the whole industry's costs.
