# Myth: meat substitutes are too expensive

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

For chicken and mince the price gap is already small — plant versions overlap with meat versions in UK supermarkets. Bacon and burgers still cost more. Lab-grown meat has dropped 50,000× in cost since 2013 and is still falling fast, but it's not yet cheap enough to compete with chicken, let alone beef. Two ways to close the gap: make substitutes cheaper, or make meat reflect its true cost. Cheaper substitutes is the more realistic path.

## Why it sounds right

Because for some products it's true. Plant-based bacon really does cost more than pork bacon in UK supermarkets. Plant-based burgers can cost noticeably more than beef burgers. Anyone who has bought both knows this. The mistake is generalising "some plant products are pricier" into "plant substitutes are unaffordable" — the picture varies a lot by product.

## What is actually true

Looking at **UK supermarket prices per kg**:

- **Chicken and plant-based chicken**: overlap heavily; some plant versions are actually cheaper
- **Mince**: plant and meat versions overlap in price
- **Sausages**: plant and meat versions overlap
- **Burgers**: some overlap, but plant burgers can cost noticeably more
- **Bacon**: plant-based bacon tends to be more expensive than pork bacon
- **Fish**: one of the more expensive meats regardless

Overall: plant-based substitutes are not always more expensive, but on average still tend to cost more than the meat equivalent, especially for bacon and burgers.

**Lab-grown (cultivated) meat cost trajectory:**
- **2013**: about **$2.4 million per kg**
- **Today**: roughly **$10-100 per kg** — about **50,000× cheaper** in ~10 years
- Cost keeps falling on a curve similar to solar, wind and batteries
- Still needs to fall further to compete with chicken; a lot further to compete with cheap beef

## If they push back

**"Just tax meat to level the price."**

Politically extremely hard. UK surveys show **only about 1 in 5 people** support a meat tax. The 2021 UK National Food Strategy consultation found a meat tax was the **least popular idea** among all its suggestions — and it caused strong disagreement among panel members. Meat tax also risks hurting the roughly **3 billion people worldwide who cannot currently afford a full healthy diet**.

**"Nothing will change unless meat gets more expensive."**

Two paths, not one. Making substitutes cheaper is the more politically viable route. Reallocating **existing farm subsidies away from meat and dairy** toward plant proteins would have a similar price effect to a meat tax, but tends to be more politically acceptable — it's not a new tax, it's just spending public money differently.

**"Lab-grown meat is a fantasy for the rich."**

At $10-100/kg it's still expensive, but the cost has fallen faster than any food technology in memory. The trajectory looks like solar's — early adopters pay premium prices, then production scaling drops costs steeply. The **regulatory bottleneck** is now more binding than the cost bottleneck. Singapore and the US have approved cultivated meat for humans; the UK allows it only for pet food; Italy and some US states have banned it outright.

## Go deeper

**The learning curve is doing the work.** Meat substitute costs have followed a familiar pattern: expensive early, then falling as production scales. Plant burgers went from restaurant-only in 2016 to supermarket staples in 2020. Lab-grown meat is on the same trajectory 8-10 years behind. Each generation of the technology gets cheaper because manufacturing scale drives learning-curve improvements.

**Regulatory approval is the real bottleneck for cultivated meat.** Right now only Singapore and the US allow lab-grown meat as human food. The UK allows it only for pets. Italy has banned it. Several US states have banned it. Without regulatory approval, the industry can't scale, and without scale, prices won't fall to competitive levels. This is a policy fight, not a technology one.

**Precision fermentation.** Microorganisms are already used to produce specific proteins (like insulin), fats, vitamins and flavours cheaply. Applied to food, this could make protein at very low cost — potentially cheaper than plant-based products. Companies like Perfect Day (dairy proteins), Formo (cheese) and Nature's Fynd are already commercial. This is the technology most likely to drop food-substitute prices below meat within a decade.

**What individuals can do.** Buy meat substitutes now. Every kilo of demand signals the market, encourages more competition, and drives further price drops. That is the same mechanism that made solar cheap: consumers buying early. The individual purchasing decision isn't just personal — it's a small nudge on the whole industry's cost curve.
