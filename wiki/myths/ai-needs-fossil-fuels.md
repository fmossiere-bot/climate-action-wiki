# Myth: AI will need so much energy we have to burn more fossil fuels to keep up

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

AI will use more electricity, but much less than the news stories suggest: data centres are about 1 to 3% of world electricity, and even gloomy forecasts only take that to around 6%. Tech firms are building gas plants because they want power right now, not because clean power can't do the job if it's planned for.

## Why it sounds right

The numbers being thrown around sound enormous, and the tech companies themselves act as if the worry is real. They are signing gas deals, restarting nuclear plants and buying up grid connections. Every week there's a story about a giant data centre that needs a whole reactor. If your reference point is Meta buying 10 gas plants to power a single Louisiana data centre, "AI is a fossil fuel problem" looks like a plain description of what's happening.

## What is actually true

The real picture is smaller and easier to manage than the news coverage suggests.

- Data centres use **around 1 to 3% of world electricity** today, AI included.
- The often-quoted line that "AI search uses 10× the energy of a Google search" came from Alphabet's chairman in 2023. Tech companies have not published real numbers to check it since.
- The IEA expects world electricity demand to grow by about 6,000 TWh by 2030. Data centres account for only about 3% of that growth.
- Industry, buildings, electric vehicles, air conditioning and heating, in that order, each add more to the 2023-2030 increase than data centres do.
- Even if AI demand doubles or triples, data centres reach about 6% of global electricity.
- Location matters more than the global total. In Ireland, data centres use 17% of national electricity, and in more than five US states they use at least 10%. That is a real problem for local grids and planning, rather than for global emissions.
- Personal AI use is tiny: 100 ChatGPT questions use about as much energy as streaming a 20-minute video, or running an electric heater for one minute.
- We have been here before. In the late 1990s, people predicted data centres would eat over half of US electricity. Between 2010 and 2018, global computing grew 550% while data centre energy use grew only 6%, thanks to steady efficiency gains known as Koomey's Law.

## If they push back

**"But look at all the new gas plants being built for data centres."**

That part is a real problem. Tech companies want power immediately, and gas is the quickest thing to plug into today's grid. That is a policy choice rather than a physical need: solar with storage, hydro, geothermal and existing nuclear can all serve the same demand if they get the same lead time.

**"AI could triple electricity demand."**

Even the most pessimistic public forecasts take data centres to about 6% of global electricity, which is a lot but not a runaway. And "could" is carrying a heavy load in that sentence: over 40 years of computing, efficiency has kept beating the panic scenarios.

**"Individuals should stop using AI to reduce their footprint."**

That isn't where the leverage is. Next to driving, flying, heating a home or what you eat, personal AI use barely registers. The pressure that changes things is on **tech companies** to source clean power and publish real numbers.

## Go deeper

**Where the "dash for gas" comes from.** Tech companies are racing each other to build AI infrastructure. That creates a timing problem. New gas turbines can be up and running in about 2 years, a new nuclear plant takes 10+, and new offshore wind takes 5-8. So gas is the fastest route to steady power right now, even where a slower clean option would be cheaper over 20 years. The issue is less that AI needs gas and more that AI executives won't wait for clean power to catch up.

Recent examples include Meta contracting 10 gas plants for its Hyperion data centre in Louisiana, and Microsoft signing a deal with Chevron for gas at a West Texas site. Google has been linked to similar partnerships. Meta has since **withdrawn from RE100**, a group of companies committed to clean electricity, because it can no longer meet its round-the-clock (24/7) clean energy criteria.

**The nuclear angle.** Big Tech is investing in nuclear too: Amazon, Microsoft and Google have all placed bets. Building new reactors is slow, so the quickest route is reopening existing plants, which is what Microsoft is doing at Three Mile Island. This may be the most useful thing the AI money is funding, but on its own it won't come fast enough.

**AI as a climate tool.** Hannah Ritchie also lists ways AI could help: finding hidden deposits of the minerals clean energy needs, balancing electricity grids that carry more variable renewables, and forecasting weather more accurately to help plan renewables and adaptation. Whether these gains outweigh the extra emissions from running AI is an open question. The answer depends on how AI is powered.
