# Myth: AI will need so much energy we have to burn more fossil fuels to keep up

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

AI will use more electricity. The problem is real, but not the size the headlines make it. Data centres are around 1 to 3% of world electricity today, and even under pessimistic AI scenarios that maybe doubles by 2030. The IEA expects industry, buildings, EVs, air conditioning and heating to each add more to global demand than data centres do. The real issue is that tech companies are dashing for gas because they want power right now — not because clean power cannot cover it if we plan properly.

## Why it sounds right

Because the raw numbers being thrown around sound enormous, and because tech companies themselves are behaving as if the concern is real — striking gas deals, restarting nuclear plants, buying up interconnects. Every week brings a headline about a hyperscale project needing a whole nuclear reactor. If your reference point is Meta buying 10 gas plants to power a single Louisiana data centre, "AI is a fossil fuel problem" looks like a factual observation.

## What is actually true

The picture is much smaller and more manageable than the headlines suggest:

- **Data centres use around 1 to 3% of world electricity today**, even including AI
- The often-quoted "AI search uses 10× the energy of a Google search" line came from **Alphabet's chairman in 2023**; tech companies have not published real numbers to verify it since
- **IEA forecast: world electricity demand grows by ~6,000 TWh by 2030**. Data centres account for only **~3% of that growth**
- Larger drivers of the 2023-2030 electricity increase are, in order: **industry, buildings, electric vehicles, air conditioning and heating**
- Even under pessimistic scenarios where AI demand doubles or triples, data centres reach **~6% of global electricity**, not a "civilisation-ending" number
- **Location matters more than the global total.** In Ireland, data centres are 17% of national electricity; in more than five US states, at least 10%. That is a real siting and grid problem, not a global-emissions problem
- Personal AI use is trivial: 100 ChatGPT questions ≈ **streaming a 20-minute video** or **running an electric heater for one minute**
- **History rhymes**: in the late 1990s people predicted data centres would eat over half of US electricity. Between 2010 and 2018, global compute grew 550% while data centre energy use only grew 6% — thanks to steady efficiency gains ("Koomey's Law")

## If they push back

**"But look at all the new gas plants being built for data centres."**

That is the real problem. It is not that AI needs fossil fuels, it is that tech companies want power **immediately** and gas is the fastest thing to plug into an existing grid. Which is a policy choice, not a physical necessity. Solar + storage, hydro, geothermal and existing nuclear can all serve the same demand if given the same lead time.

**"AI could triple electricity demand."**

Even in the most pessimistic public forecasts, that pushes data centres to ~6% of global electricity, not a runaway. And "could" is doing a lot of work in that sentence — efficiency has consistently beaten the panic scenarios for 40 years of computing history.

**"Individuals should stop using AI to reduce their footprint."**

Not really where the leverage is. Personal AI use is somewhere between negligible and rounding error compared to driving, flying, heating a home, or diet. The pressure that changes anything is on **tech companies to source clean power and publish real numbers**, not on individuals to skip a chatbot query.

## Go deeper

**Where the "dash for gas" comes from.** Tech companies are racing each other to build AI infrastructure faster than competitors. That has created a specific urgency mismatch: new gas turbines can be commissioned in about 2 years; a new nuclear plant takes 10+; new offshore wind takes 5-8. So the fastest path to firm power right now is gas — even where a slower clean path is cheaper over 20 years. This is not "AI needs gas." It is **AI executives will not wait for clean power to catch up**.

Recent examples: **Meta contracting 10 gas plants for its Hyperion data centre in Louisiana**; **Microsoft signing a deal with Chevron** for gas at a West Texas site; **Google** linked to similar partnerships. Notably, Meta has now **withdrawn from RE100** because it can no longer meet 24/7 clean energy criteria.

**The nuclear angle.** Big Tech is investing in nuclear too — Amazon, Microsoft and Google have all placed bets. New nuclear is slow, so the fastest route is **reopening existing plants**: Microsoft is doing exactly that at Three Mile Island. This is arguably the most productive use of the AI capital surge, but it doesn't help fast enough on its own.

**AI as a climate tool.** The Ritchie extract also lists specific ways AI could help: **finding hidden mineral deposits** needed for clean energy, **balancing electricity grids** with more variable renewables, and **forecasting weather** more accurately to support renewable planning and adaptation. Whether these gains outweigh the extra emissions from running AI is an open question — the honest answer is that it depends on how AI is powered.
