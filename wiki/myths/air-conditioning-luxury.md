# Myth: air conditioning is a luxury the climate can't afford

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

In a warming world, cooling is a necessity: it saves lives, lets people sleep and work, and helps children learn. The thing to fix is efficiency, because switching to the efficient units we already have would cut cooling electricity nearly in half, and heating still causes four times more emissions than cooling anyway.

## Why it sounds right

Air conditioning does use a lot of electricity, and it does strain the grid on the hottest days. Its emissions are real. It's also easy to see it as a rich-country habit, since the places with the most AC per person are wealthy ones like the US. If you picture the US electricity draw on a July afternoon and imagine India reaching the same level, "AC is a luxury we can't afford" sounds sensible.

## What is actually true

There are around **2 billion AC units** in the world today, and that is expected to reach 6 billion by 2050, roughly a tripling. AC already accounts for 3-4% of world greenhouse gas emissions and about 8% of world electricity. Most of the growth comes from rising incomes in low- and middle-income countries, where people who could not afford cooling finally can.

Heating still causes about 4× more global emissions than cooling. So the idea that cooling is driving the climate crisis gets the ratio the wrong way round.

Efficient units cut demand nearly in half:
- With today's typical inefficient units, global cooling electricity demand reaches 6,200 TWh by 2050, up from 2,000 TWh in 2020.
- With efficient units that already exist, demand only reaches 3,400 TWh.
- The difference is about the size of the entire EU's electricity use.

Inefficient units dominate because of cost. Poor households buying their first AC often can't find the extra $100 for the efficient model, and that gap locks them into years of higher bills. A family buying a 50% more efficient unit could save around $2,000 over the following years.

Grid strain is real, but efficiency makes it manageable:
- **US**: cooling is already about 30% of peak electricity demand on hot days. With efficient units, that could fall to just over 20% by 2050.
- **India**: cooling is about 10% of peak today. With inefficient units it could rise to about 45% by 2050, a huge strain on the grid. With efficient units, it would be only about 20%.

Solar helps too. Cooling demand peaks when the sun is shining, so a grid with lots of solar can meet it without a big rise in emissions.

## If they push back

**"AC is causing the climate to warm even more."**

It adds some, yes, but heating emits 4× more, so if emissions are the worry, start with heating. If grid strain is the worry, the answer is efficient units rather than banning AC.

**"But people in Asia getting AC will double emissions."**

Only if they buy inefficient units and the electricity comes from coal. Efficient units on solar-heavy grids cut cooling emissions to a small fraction of the simple projection, which makes efficient AC one of the cheapest emissions cuts on offer.

**"Rich people don't need it, they should just adapt."**

Rich people already have it. The people who most need it, for basic health and to be able to work, are lower-income households in tropical countries where heat is now dangerous. Calling cooling a luxury means telling them to sweat it out, and that isn't a workable climate policy.

## Go deeper

**Cooling as a necessity.** In the UK we call it "fuel poverty" when households can't afford to heat their homes, and we rightly worry about it. The same logic applies to cooling. Households that can't afford AC in a warming world are also in fuel poverty, and that group grows as temperatures rise. Access to cooling is a health equity issue.

**The humidity problem.** Up to **half** of an AC unit's energy goes into removing water vapour from the air rather than lowering the temperature. In humid air the body can't cool itself by sweating, so cooling people properly means drying the air as well. Many units are poorly designed for humid climates and waste energy as a result. Designs tuned for humidity are a big efficiency gain, especially for South Asia.

**The efficient-unit gap.** Take the Indian AC market. A 3-star efficiency unit costs about $350, and a 5-star costs about $100 more. That is a **20-35% price jump**, which most low-income households can't pay. Efficiency labels help, but the underlying problem is that efficient models cost more up front. Subsidies, financing schemes and bulk buying (like the EU's proposed CPO cooling equivalent) can close this gap.

**Solar and cooling go together.** The hottest hours of the day are also the sunniest, so solar output lines up with cooling demand better than with almost any other use. A grid that pairs efficient AC with solar, on rooftops or in large solar farms, can meet much of the cooling load with close to zero new emissions.
