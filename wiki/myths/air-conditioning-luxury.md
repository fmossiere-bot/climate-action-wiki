# Myth: air conditioning is a luxury the climate can't afford

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

Cooling saves lives, helps kids learn, lets people sleep and work. As the world warms, it's a necessity, not a luxury. The number of AC units will roughly triple by 2050 — that's mostly people escaping heat, which is a good thing. What we need to worry about is efficiency: switching everyone to efficient units instead of cheap inefficient ones would cut cooling electricity demand nearly in half. Heating still causes four times more emissions than cooling anyway.

## Why it sounds right

Because AC does use a lot of electricity, and it does contribute to grid strain on the hottest days. The direct emissions are real. And it's easy to frame it as a "rich person's problem" when the countries with the most AC per capita are wealthy ones like the US. If you look at the electricity draw of the US on a July afternoon and worry that India getting to the same level would break the planet, "AC is a luxury we can't afford" sounds sensible.

## What is actually true

- Around **2 billion AC units** in the world today; expected to reach **6 billion by 2050** — a tripling
- AC is already **3-4% of world greenhouse gas emissions** and uses about **8% of world electricity**
- Growth is driven by rising incomes in low- and middle-income countries — people who cannot currently afford cooling finally can
- **Heating still causes about 4× more global emissions than cooling** — the "cooling is the climate crisis" framing has the emissions ratio backwards

**Efficient units cut demand nearly in half:**
- Business as usual (today's typical inefficient units): global cooling electricity demand reaches **6,200 TWh by 2050**, up from 2,000 TWh in 2020
- Switch to efficient units that already exist: demand only reaches **3,400 TWh**
- The difference is about the size of the **entire EU's electricity use**

**Why inefficient units dominate:**
- Cost. Poor households buying their first AC often can't afford the extra $100 for the efficient model. That price gap dooms them to years of higher electricity bills — a family buying a 50% more efficient unit could save around **$2,000** over the following years.

**Grid strain (real, but manageable with efficiency):**
- **US**: cooling is already ~30% of peak electricity demand on hot days. With efficient units, that could fall to just over 20% by 2050
- **India**: cooling is ~10% of peak today; with inefficient units, that could rise to ~45% by 2050 — a massive grid strain. With efficient units, only ~20%.

**Solar helps.** Cooling peaks when the sun shines. Solar generation aligns almost perfectly with AC demand, so a grid with lots of solar can meet cooling demand without a huge rise in emissions.

## If they push back

**"AC is causing the climate to warm even more."**

Some, yes — but heating still emits 4× more. If your worry is emissions, look at heating first. If your worry is grid strain, the fix is efficient units, not banning AC.

**"But people in Asia getting AC will double emissions."**

Only if they buy inefficient units, and only if the electricity is coal. **Efficient units + solar-heavy grids** cut cooling emissions to a small fraction of the naïve projection. The transition to efficient AC is one of the cheapest emissions wins available.

**"Rich people don't need it, they should just adapt."**

Rich people already have it. The people who most urgently need it — for basic health and productivity — are lower-income households in tropical countries where heat is now dangerous. Framing cooling as unnecessary luxury effectively means telling those households to sweat it out. That is not a viable climate policy.

## Go deeper

**Reframing cooling as a necessity, not a luxury.** In the UK we call it "fuel poverty" when households can't afford to heat their homes, and we're rightly concerned. The same logic applies to cooling: households that can't afford AC in a warming world are also in fuel poverty. As the world warms, that becomes a bigger and bigger group. Cooling access is a health equity issue.

**The humidity problem.** Up to **half** of an AC unit's energy goes not to lowering temperature but to removing water vapour from the air. In humid conditions the body can't cool itself through sweat evaporation, so effective cooling means dehumidifying, not just cooling. Many AC units are poorly designed for humid climates, wasting energy trying to cool without properly dehumidifying. Better humidity-tuned designs are a major efficiency lever, especially for South Asia.

**The efficient-unit gap.** The typical Indian AC market example: a 3-star efficiency unit costs ~$350; a 5-star costs ~$100 more. A **20-35% price jump** most low-income households can't pay. Efficiency labels help, but the underlying problem is affordability of the efficient models. Subsidies, financing schemes and bulk procurement (like the EU's proposed CPO cooling equivalent) can close this gap.

**Solar aligns with cooling almost perfectly.** The hottest hours of the day are also the sunniest. Solar generation lines up with cooling demand better than with almost any other use. A grid that pairs efficient AC with distributed solar — either rooftop or utility-scale — can meet a large share of cooling load with new emissions close to zero.
