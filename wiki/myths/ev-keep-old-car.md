# Myth: Keeping my old car running is greener than buying a new EV

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

For a fridge or a phone, keeping the old one wins. For a car it does not, because most of the damage is in the fuel, not the factory.

## Why it sounds right

It sounds right because you avoid the emissions of building a whole new car, and use what you already have is usually sound environmental advice.

## What is actually true

It holds for most products, but not for cars, because a car keeps emitting every day you drive it. A 14 year old petrol car produces around 45 tonnes of CO2 over its remaining life against 14 tonnes for a new Tesla Model Y, a cut of about 68%. Even accounting for the emissions of building the new car, replacing an old petrol car with an EV breaks even after roughly 20,000 to 32,000 miles, about four years of average UK driving.

## If they push back

**"So I should scrap a perfectly good car?"**

Not scrap. It gets resold and used. The question is what you buy when the old one is genuinely done, and then the EV wins clearly.

**"Surely that's only true for an old banger, not a car I just bought?"**

2026 US modelling (Campbell & Geyer, UC Santa Cruz / UC Santa Barbara) tested exactly that and found the same result holds "in the vast majority of cases, and even for a brand new gas vehicle" — the driving emissions saved by switching outweigh the extra emissions of building the new EV. The main exceptions are a coal-heavy electricity grid, or driving under about 7,000km a year.

## Also heard as

- "old bangers are the green choice"
- "the greenest car is the one you already own"
- "manufacturing a new car is worse than running an old one"
- "it can't be worth scrapping a car that's only a year or two old"

## Go deeper

2026 research from Elliott Campbell (UC Santa Cruz) and Roland Geyer (UC Santa Barbara) modelled scrapping a petrol car at different points in its life — from 1-2 years old through to end-of-life — accounting for manufacturing emissions, grid mix, efficiency and mileage. Their conclusion: there is essentially no scenario, short of a coal-heavy grid or very low annual mileage, where it makes sense to keep driving a petrol car to the end of its life instead of switching. The researchers frame this as support for subsidised scrappage schemes, not a call for individuals to scrap brand-new cars on the spot. One caveat raised by other academics: scrapped petrol cars often re-enter the second-hand market, and the knock-on effects of that (e.g. cheaper second-hand petrol cars pulling people away from public transport) are not yet well studied.
