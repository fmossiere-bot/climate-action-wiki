# Myth: Keeping my old car running is greener than buying a new EV

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

For a fridge or a phone, keeping the old one usually wins. For a car it doesn't, because most of the damage comes from the fuel, not the factory.

## Why it sounds right

You avoid the emissions of building a whole new car, and "use what you already have" is usually sound environmental advice. It's a sensible instinct.

## What is actually true

That instinct holds for most products, but cars are different because they keep emitting every day you drive them. A 14 year old petrol car produces around **45 tonnes of CO2** over its remaining life, against 14 tonnes for a new Tesla Model Y, a cut of about 68%. Even counting the emissions of building the new car, swapping an old petrol car for an EV breaks even after roughly 20,000 to 32,000 miles, about four years of average UK driving.

## If they push back

**"So I should scrap a perfectly good car?"**

Not scrap it. It gets resold and someone else drives it. The real question is what you buy when your old car is truly done, and at that point the EV wins clearly.

**"Surely that's only true for an old banger, not a car I just bought?"**

US modelling from 2026 (Campbell & Geyer, UC Santa Cruz / UC Santa Barbara) tested exactly that. It found the same result holds "in the vast majority of cases, and even for a brand new gas vehicle": the driving emissions saved by switching outweigh the extra emissions of building the new EV. The main exceptions are a coal-heavy electricity grid, or driving under about 7,000km a year.

## Also heard as

- "old bangers are the green choice"
- "the greenest car is the one you already own"
- "manufacturing a new car is worse than running an old one"
- "it can't be worth scrapping a car that's only a year or two old"

## Go deeper

In 2026, Elliott Campbell (UC Santa Cruz) and Roland Geyer (UC Santa Barbara) modelled scrapping a petrol car at different points in its life, from 1-2 years old through to end-of-life. They accounted for manufacturing emissions, grid mix, efficiency and mileage. They concluded there is almost no scenario, short of a coal-heavy grid or very low annual mileage, where it makes sense to keep driving a petrol car to the end of its life instead of switching.

The researchers see this as support for subsidised scrappage schemes. They are not asking individuals to scrap brand-new cars on the spot. Other academics raise one caveat: scrapped petrol cars often go back onto the second-hand market, and the knock-on effects of that (for example, cheaper second-hand petrol cars pulling people away from public transport) are not yet well studied.
