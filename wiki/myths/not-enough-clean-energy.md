# Myth: there isn't enough clean energy to replace fossil fuels

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

We don't have to match today's fossil fuel use one-for-one, because burning fuel wastes most of its energy as heat, so one unit of solar or wind can replace two to three units of coal. If we electrify cars, heating and industry, total world energy demand actually falls by about 40%, even as electricity use goes up.

## Why it sounds right

At first glance the numbers look enormous. The world uses hundreds of exajoules of energy every year, and if all of that has to come from solar panels and wind turbines, it sounds impossible on any human timescale. The trouble is that this measures the target in "primary energy": the total energy that goes in, including everything lost as heat. That measure was designed for a world that burns fuel, not one that runs on electricity.

## What is actually true

Burning fuel is very wasteful, and the alternatives are far more efficient:

- A coal power plant turns about one third of its energy into electricity, and the rest is lost as heat up the chimney. Gas power wastes about half. Solar and wind waste almost nothing.
- So **1 unit of solar or wind can replace 2 to 3 units of coal**. A city needing 100 TWh of electricity would burn 300 TWh of coal to get it, or 200 TWh of gas, or need just 100 TWh of solar and wind.
- Petrol cars are only about 20% efficient: 80 cents of every €1 of petrol becomes waste heat. Electric cars are about 80% efficient, so driving an EV uses only a quarter to a third of the energy of a petrol car.
- If the world electrified everything that can be electrified (cars, heating, industry, short flights, ferries), total world energy demand would fall by around 40%, from about 416 EJ today to about 247 EJ (Ritchie 2024), even as electricity demand rises.
- Renewables are already doing most of the new work. Between 2010 and 2022 they supplied 57% of the growth in global electricity, and in 2022 alone they supplied 92% of new demand.

## If they push back

**"But electricity demand is going up, that proves it isn't working."**

Rising electricity demand is exactly what you'd expect if the transition is working. Cars, heat pumps and industry are moving off gas and petrol and onto the grid. It's **total** energy demand that falls. Electricity is the visible part of a much bigger efficiency gain that mostly goes unseen.

**"OK, but poor countries will need much more energy as they develop."**

Yes, and that's right and fair. Rich countries will use less and poorer countries will use more, but the world total still falls, because moving away from burning fuel saves so much energy. The two don't conflict.

**"You're leaving out storage, solar and wind are intermittent."**

Not quite. The first 70 to 80% of a power system can be covered by solar and wind plus a modest amount of storage. The last 20 to 30% is harder in regions with little sun and wind, and needs other tools: nuclear, hydro, geothermal, batteries, and gas as a shrinking backup. That's a real engineering problem, but it's very different from there not being enough clean energy at all.

## Go deeper

**Why the primary energy trap is so common.** International energy statistics were built for a world that burns coal, oil and gas. They report the total heat content of the fuel, not the useful energy that reaches the wheels or the room. So a country switching from petrol cars to EVs looks as if it's using less energy on paper, which it is in the way that matters, yet headlines about falling energy demand often present that as a failure. For this reason Ritchie and others (Bloomberg NEF, RMI, and the IEA in some reports) now try to publish "useful energy" figures alongside primary energy.

**Where the transition is now.** Global sales of new petrol and diesel cars peaked in 2017. In 2022, renewables supplied 92% of new global electricity demand. Analysts expect coal, oil and gas to each reach their global peak within about five years of that. This is what the transition looks like in progress: curves gradually crossing rather than a switch being flipped.

**One caveat.** Closing existing nuclear plants early, as Germany and Japan did, generally sets the transition back. New renewables then replace nuclear instead of fossil fuels, so total emissions don't fall as much. Keeping existing low-carbon plants running while building new renewables is a much faster path.
