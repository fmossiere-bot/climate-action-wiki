# Myth: there isn't enough clean energy to replace fossil fuels

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

We don't need to build clean energy to match our current fossil fuel use, one-for-one. That's the wrong target. Because burning fuel wastes most of the energy it makes, one unit of solar or wind can replace two to three units of coal. If we electrify what we currently burn — cars, heating, industry — total world energy demand actually falls by about 40%, even as electricity demand goes up.

## Why it sounds right

Because the numbers, at first glance, look enormous. The world uses hundreds of exajoules of energy every year. If someone tells you all of that has to be replaced by solar panels and wind turbines, it sounds impossible in any human timescale. The mistake is measuring the target in "primary energy" — the total energy input including everything that gets wasted as heat. That measure is designed for a world that burns fuel, not one that runs on electricity.

## What is actually true

Burning fuel is astonishingly wasteful, and the alternative is much more efficient:

- A **coal power plant** turns about **one third** of its energy into electricity; the rest is heat lost up the chimney. **Gas power** wastes about half. Solar and wind waste almost nothing.
- Therefore **1 unit of solar or wind can replace 2 to 3 units of coal**. A city needing 100 TWh of electricity would burn 300 TWh of coal to get it, or 200 TWh of gas, or just 100 TWh of solar and wind.
- **Petrol cars are only about 20% efficient** — 80 cents of every €1 of petrol becomes waste heat. **Electric cars are about 80% efficient**. Driving an EV uses **only a quarter to a third of the energy** of a petrol car.
- If the world electrified everything that can be electrified (cars, heating, industry, short flights, ferries), **total world energy demand would fall by around 40%**, from about **416 EJ today to about 247 EJ** (Ritchie 2024) — even as electricity demand rises.
- Renewables are already doing most of the new work: between **2010 and 2022, renewables supplied 57% of the growth** in global electricity, and in **2022 alone they supplied 92%** of new demand.

## If they push back

**"But electricity demand is going up, that proves it isn't working."**

Rising electricity demand is exactly what you would expect if the transition is working. Cars, heat pumps and industry are moving off gas and petrol and onto the grid. **Total** energy demand is what falls. Electricity is the visible tip of a much bigger and mostly hidden efficiency gain.

**"OK, but poor countries will need much more energy as they develop."**

Yes, and that is right and fair. Rich countries will use less; poorer countries will use more; the world total still falls, because the switch away from burning is such a large efficiency win. The two things are not in conflict.

**"You're leaving out storage — solar and wind are intermittent."**

Not entirely. The **first 70 to 80% of a power system** can be covered by solar and wind plus modest storage. The **last 20 to 30%** is harder in regions with poor sun and wind, and needs other tools — nuclear, hydro, geothermal, batteries, gas as a decreasing backup. That is a real engineering problem, but it is very different from "there isn't enough clean energy at all."

## Go deeper

**Why the primary energy trap is so common.** International energy statistics were built for a world that burns coal, oil and gas. They report the total heat content of the fuel, not the useful energy delivered to the wheel or the room. So a country switching from petrol cars to EVs looks like it is using less energy on paper — which it is, in the sense that matters — but headlines that lead with "energy demand fell" often frame that as a failure. Ritchie and others (Bloomberg NEF, RMI, IEA in some reports) now try to publish "useful energy" figures alongside primary energy for this reason.

**Where the transition is now.** Sales of new petrol and diesel cars already peaked in **2017** globally. In **2022, renewables supplied 92% of new global electricity demand**. Analysts expect coal, oil and gas to each hit their global peak within about five years of that. That is what the transition looks like in flight — not a switch flipped, but curves crossing.

**One important caveat.** Closing existing nuclear plants early (as Germany and Japan did) generally leaves the transition worse off — renewables then just replace nuclear rather than fossil fuels, so total emissions do not fall as much. Keeping existing low-carbon capacity online while building new renewables is a much faster path.
