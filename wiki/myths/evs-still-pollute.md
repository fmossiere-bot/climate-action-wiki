# Myth: electric cars pollute just as much as petrol cars

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

EVs still cause some pollution — from tyre wear, brake wear and road dust — and being heavier, they're slightly worse on those than petrol cars. But they completely eliminate exhaust pollution, which is by far the biggest and worst source. Overall, they are much better for air quality, not the same. The fix for the rest is making cars lighter and smaller.

## Why it sounds right

Because "zero emissions" is a claim EV marketing often makes, and it's technically wrong. Cars pollute in four ways: exhaust, brake wear, tyre wear and road dust. EVs only get rid of one of them. Anyone pointing out that tyre and road particles still exist is telling the truth. The mistake is treating "not zero" as "same as petrol."

## What is actually true

The four sources of air pollution from any car:

1. **Tailpipe emissions** — nitrogen oxides (NOx) and PM2.5. Both terrible for respiratory health, both eliminated by EVs.
2. **Brake wear** — friction between pads and discs. EVs use **regenerative braking**, so brakes engage far less often. Much reduced, not eliminated.
3. **Tyre wear** — friction with the road. About the same or slightly worse in EVs, because they're heavier.
4. **Road dust** — kicked up by moving vehicles, also affected by weight. Slightly worse in EVs.

Overall verdict: EVs cause slightly higher tyre and road dust pollution, but this is heavily outweighed by removing exhaust emissions and cutting brake wear. Per source-by-source comparison, EVs come out cleaner overall.

The remaining problem is real, though — **PM2.5 from non-exhaust sources** still causes real health harm, especially in dense urban areas.

## If they push back

**"So EVs still cause air pollution."**

Yes, some. But the pollution they eliminate — NOx and exhaust PM2.5 — is directly linked to millions of deaths per year worldwide. The pollution they slightly worsen (tyre and road dust) is a smaller problem. Trading one for the other is a big win.

**"They're heavier because of the battery, so tyre wear is worse."**

True. This is one of the strongest arguments for [smaller EVs](../sectors/transport/smaller-evs-better.md) — a smaller EV with a smaller battery is lighter, cheaper, safer for pedestrians and produces less non-exhaust pollution. Both petrol and electric cars have grown heavier over the past decade, with the "SUV effect" pushing weight up across the board. France has already introduced a weight tax; that logic should apply to EVs too, not just to petrol SUVs.

**"So EVs aren't really clean."**

The right framing is "much cleaner than petrol on the biggest source, slightly worse on the smaller sources." Not "clean" in the absolute sense. That's why walking, cycling and public transport still beat any car.

## Go deeper

**Tailpipe pollution is a public-health killer.** NOx and PM2.5 from petrol and diesel engines are linked to respiratory disease, cardiovascular disease and premature death. Global estimates put fossil fuel air pollution deaths at 4-8 million per year. Removing tailpipe emissions from the vehicle fleet is one of the biggest air-quality wins available.

**The weight problem cuts across fuels.** Modern cars — both petrol and electric — are heavier than a generation ago. SUVs and pickups have driven "car bloat" up across the industry. Both electric and petrol SUVs make non-exhaust pollution worse. The [smaller EVs](../sectors/transport/smaller-evs-better.md) case applies directly: cutting vehicle weight is the biggest lever for reducing tyre and road dust.

**Regenerative braking helps.** Because EVs recover energy through the motor when slowing down, they use their friction brakes far less than petrol cars. This dramatically reduces brake dust — one of the worst non-exhaust PM sources. Some new designs are exploring **brake drums** that capture particles at the source, easier to fit on EVs because their brakes run cooler.

**The real fix is fewer cars.** Cutting exhaust emissions is a huge health win. But the biggest air-quality win is still reducing the number of car journeys — through public transport, cycling infrastructure and denser cities. That's the top of the ladder, regardless of what fuel the cars use.
