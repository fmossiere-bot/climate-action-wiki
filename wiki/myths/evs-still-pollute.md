# Myth: electric cars pollute just as much as petrol cars

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

EVs still cause some pollution from tyres, brakes and road dust, and because they're heavier they can be a bit worse on tyres and dust. But they get rid of exhaust fumes completely, which is the biggest and most harmful source, so overall they're much better for the air we breathe.

## Why it sounds right

"Zero emissions" is a claim EV marketing often makes, and strictly speaking it's wrong. Cars pollute in four ways: exhaust, brake wear, tyre wear and road dust. EVs get rid of only one of them. Anyone pointing out that tyre and road particles still exist is telling the truth. The mistake is treating "not zero" as "the same as petrol."

## What is actually true

Every car pollutes the air in four ways:

- Exhaust fumes: nitrogen oxides (NOx) and PM2.5 (fine particles small enough to get deep into the lungs). Both are very harmful to breathing, and EVs remove both.
- Brake wear: from friction between pads and discs. EVs use **regenerative braking**, so the brakes are used far less often. Much reduced, though not eliminated.
- Tyre wear: from friction with the road. About the same, or slightly worse, in EVs because they're heavier.
- Road dust: kicked up by moving vehicles, and also affected by weight. Slightly worse in EVs.

So EVs cause a little more tyre and road dust pollution, but that is far outweighed by removing exhaust fumes and cutting brake wear. Taking each source in turn, EVs come out cleaner overall.

The remaining problem is real, though. **PM2.5 from non-exhaust sources** still harms health, especially in crowded cities.

## If they push back

**"So EVs still cause air pollution."**

Yes, some. But the pollution they remove, NOx and PM2.5 from exhausts, is directly linked to millions of deaths a year worldwide. The pollution they make slightly worse (tyre and road dust) is a smaller problem. Trading one for the other is a big win.

**"They're heavier because of the battery, so tyre wear is worse."**

True. It's one of the strongest arguments for [smaller EVs](../sectors/transport/smaller-evs-better.md). A smaller EV with a smaller battery is lighter, cheaper, safer for pedestrians and produces less pollution from tyres and road dust. Petrol and electric cars have both got heavier over the past decade, with the "SUV effect" pushing weight up across the board. France has already brought in a weight tax, and that logic should apply to EVs as well as petrol SUVs.

**"So EVs aren't really clean."**

A fairer way to put it: much cleaner than petrol on the biggest source, slightly worse on the smaller ones. They're not "clean" in an absolute sense. That's why walking, cycling and public transport still beat any car.

## Go deeper

Exhaust pollution kills. NOx and PM2.5 from petrol and diesel engines are linked to lung disease, heart disease and early death. Global estimates put deaths from fossil fuel air pollution at **4-8 million per year**. Removing exhaust fumes from the roads is one of the biggest air quality gains available.

The weight problem applies to every kind of car. Modern cars, petrol and electric alike, are heavier than a generation ago. SUVs and pickups have driven "car bloat" across the industry, and electric and petrol SUVs both make non-exhaust pollution worse. The [smaller EVs](../sectors/transport/smaller-evs-better.md) case applies directly: making cars lighter is the biggest lever for cutting tyre and road dust.

Regenerative braking helps. Because EVs recover energy through the motor when slowing down, they use their friction brakes far less than petrol cars. That greatly reduces brake dust, one of the worst non-exhaust sources of particles. Some new designs are trying **brake drums** that capture particles at the source, which are easier to fit on EVs because their brakes run cooler.

The real fix is fewer cars. Getting rid of exhaust fumes is a huge health gain. But the biggest gain for air quality still comes from fewer car journeys, through public transport, safe cycling routes and denser cities. That holds whatever fuel the cars use.
