# Myth: it's too late, we're heading for 5 or 6 degrees, so nothing matters

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

The 5 or 6 degree scenarios aren't where we're heading any more: current policies put us at roughly 2.5 to 3 degrees by 2100, and closer to 1.8 if the net zero pledges are delivered. That's still a dangerous world, but "too late" is not the same as "nothing matters", and every tenth of a degree we shave off is a real, measurable win.

## Why it sounds right

Because part of it is true. The **1.5°C target is now out of reach**, and even 2°C is unlikely on current form. Anyone still claiming 1.5 is achievable without a miracle isn't being straight with you. Years of headlines about worst-case scenarios have also stuck in people's minds. If your reference point is Mark Lynas's *Six Degrees* from the mid-2000s, the picture really did look apocalyptic. The despair rests on real numbers, and waving it away as "just doomism" misses the point.

## What is actually true

Recent projections narrow the realistic range. The latest is the **UNEP Emissions Gap Report 2026**, published in October 2026:

- Current policies (nothing new, nothing better): about 2.8°C by 2100.
- Every current national pledge delivered: about 2.1°C. That is worse than 2024's projection of 1.9°C, "driven mainly by the US's exit from the UN climate process".
- The "lowest possible overshoot" path: warming peaks at 1.7-1.8°C, with a 50% chance of falling back to 1.5°C by 2100. That needs emissions to fall 18% by 2030, 45% by 2035 and 69% by 2040, while current pledges only deliver 5-8% by 2030.

So the realistic range is roughly 1.8 to 2.8°C, not 5 or 6. The extreme scenario often quoted from older modelling, labelled **RCP8.5**, is now considered implausible. None of this makes 2.1 or 2.8°C safe. Either would still mean losing most coral reefs, worse heatwaves and serious pressure on food supplies, especially in poorer countries. But it is a very different world from the "civilisation collapse" picture, and every fraction of a degree we prevent is a measurable win. There is no single global switch that flips at 1.5 or 2°C.

## If they push back

**"1.5°C is dead, so we've already failed."**

Missing an exact number isn't the same as failing at the whole task. 1.7°C is meaningfully better than 1.9°C, which is meaningfully better than 2.1°C. The goal moves from "hit a specific number" to "cut emissions as fast as possible, whatever number we land near."

**"We'll hit tipping points and everything collapses anyway."**

Tipping points are real, and they matter for particular systems such as the Amazon, the AMOC (the Atlantic current system) and permafrost. But they are not one global switch. If anything they make the case for cutting emissions stronger, because every degree we prevent lowers the odds of setting one off.

**"So why bother, if we're still heading for 2.5°C?"**

Because 2.5°C is what happens if we drift, and 1.8°C is what happens if we push. The gap between those two worlds decides whether coral reefs survive, whether food systems cope, and whether hundreds of millions of people can stay in their homes.

**"1.5°C is officially over now, doesn't that prove it's too late?"**

The UN Emissions Gap Report 2026 does say 1.5°C can now only be reached through "overshoot": going past 1.5°C for a while, then using huge amounts of mechanical and natural cooling (direct air capture, planting forests and so on) to bring temperatures back down later. **Bob Watson, former IPCC chair, calls that scenario "cuckoo land"**, because the cooling technologies it needs don't yet exist at scale. But the UN Secretary-General's view still holds: "Overshoot is not surrender. We can still keep it as small and as short as possible." The question has moved from "can we hit 1.5°C" to "how small and short can we keep the overshoot". That is still a debate about how much damage to avoid, and trying is still the point. See the [UN overshoot 2.8°C source](../sources/un-overshoot-2.8c-newscientist-2026.md).

## Go deeper

**The RCP8.5 trap.** RCP8.5 is the label older IPCC modelling gave to an extreme, high-emissions scenario. It still turns up in headlines because it makes for dramatic stories, but researchers now generally treat it as an unlikely upper limit rather than the "business as usual" path. If a claim about future warming rests on RCP8.5, treat it with caution.

**Where the big levers are.** Ritchie is blunt that individual choices alone won't get the world where it needs to be. Most of the work has to happen across whole systems: building low-carbon electricity fast (solar, wind, nuclear, geothermal, with quicker planning permission), speeding up batteries, electrifying cars, home heating and industry, cutting global meat and dairy consumption, restoring forests, and keeping research money flowing into the sectors that are hardest to clean up (cement, steel, aviation, direct air capture).

**What you can do, without the guilt spiral.** The biggest personal items are transport (drive less, choose an EV over petrol), flying (often the largest single slice of a personal footprint) and home energy (insulation, and an electric heat pump instead of a gas boiler). Diet matters too, especially eating less beef and lamb. Solar panels are worth it if you can afford them and own your roof, and switching to a renewable energy supplier helps signal demand. Ritchie warns against spending emotional energy on small things (plastic bags, standby mode, recycling), because it can trigger moral licensing: feeling you have "done your bit" with something low-impact, and using that as a pass on the things that matter more. Her example is someone who feels good about bringing a reusable bag, then fills it with beef and dairy.

**Good tipping points too.** Tim Lenton (*Positive Tipping Points*, 2024) makes a stronger version of this argument. The bad climate tipping points (AMOC, Greenland, Amazon, permafrost) are our biggest risk, but the same self-reinforcing feedback can also drive fast good change. Norway went from 0.25% to 84% EV sales in 14 years, UK coal fell from 40% to 2% of electricity in 7 years, and wind and solar double worldwide every 3 years. His colleague Simon Sharpe argues we need to move "Five Times Faster" (cutting emissions 7% a year) to stay below 2°C, and ten times faster to keep 1.5°C alive. Lenton suggests swapping the question "how much damage can we avoid" for a more hopeful one: "which good tipping points can we trigger fastest".
