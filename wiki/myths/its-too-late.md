# Myth: it's too late, we're heading for 5 or 6 degrees, so nothing matters

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

The 5 or 6 degree scenarios are not where we are heading any more. Current policies put us at roughly 2.5 to 3 degrees by 2100, and if the net zero pledges are actually delivered, closer to 1.8. That is still a serious, dangerous world, but "too late" is not the same as "nothing matters." Every tenth of a degree we can shave off is a real, measurable win.

## Why it sounds right

Because part of it is true. The **1.5°C target is now out of reach**. Even 2°C is unlikely on current form. Anyone still claiming 1.5 is achievable without a miracle is being dishonest, and years of headlines about worst-case scenarios have burned into public memory. If your reference point is Mark Lynas's *Six Degrees* from the mid-2000s, the picture really did look apocalyptic. The despair is grounded in real numbers, and dismissing it as "just doomism" misses the point.

## What is actually true

Recent projections narrow the realistic range considerably:

- **Current policies (nothing new, nothing better)**: about **2.5 to 3°C** by 2100 (UNEP Emissions Gap Report 2024).
- **Countries hit their 2030 targets but do nothing more**: about **2.4°C**.
- **Every mid-century net zero pledge is actually delivered**: about **1.8°C**.

So the realistic range is roughly **1.8 to 3°C**, not 5 or 6. The extreme scenario often cited in older modelling, labelled **RCP8.5**, is now considered implausible. That does not make 2.5°C safe. It still means most coral reefs lost, worse heatwaves, and serious pressure on food systems, especially in poorer countries. But it is a very different world from the "civilisation collapse" framing, and every fraction of a degree we prevent is a measurable win. There is no single global switch that flips at 1.5 or 2°C.

## If they push back

**"1.5°C is dead, so we've already failed."**

Failing to hit an exact number is not the same as failing on the whole task. 1.7°C is meaningfully better than 1.9°C, which is meaningfully better than 2.1°C. The goal shifts from "hit a specific number" to "cut emissions as fast as possible, whatever number we land near."

**"We'll hit tipping points and everything collapses anyway."**

Tipping points are real, and they matter for specific systems like the Amazon, the AMOC, and permafrost. They are not one global switch. Passing them makes the case for cutting emissions harder, not weaker, because every degree we prevent lowers the odds of triggering one.

**"So why bother, if we're still heading for 2.5°C?"**

Because 2.5°C is what happens if we drift. 1.8°C is what happens if we actually push. The gap between those two worlds is the difference between coral reefs surviving, food systems coping, and hundreds of millions of people staying in their homes.

## Go deeper

**The RCP8.5 trap.** RCP8.5 is a specific labelling from older IPCC modelling for an extreme, high-emissions scenario. It still turns up in headlines because it makes for dramatic stories, but researchers now generally treat it as an implausible upper bound rather than the "business as usual" path. If a claim about future warming rests on RCP8.5, that is a red flag.

**Where the levers actually are.** Ritchie is blunt that individual choices alone will not get the world where it needs to be. The heavy lifting has to be at the systems level: rolling out low-carbon electricity fast (solar, wind, nuclear, geothermal, with better permitting), speeding up batteries, electrifying cars and home heating and industrial processes, cutting global meat and dairy consumption, restoring forests, and keeping R&D flowing into hard-to-decarbonise sectors (cement, steel, aviation, direct air capture).

**The individual angle, without the guilt spiral.** For personal action, the big-ticket items are transport (drive less, EV over petrol), flying (often the single biggest slice of a personal footprint), and home energy (insulation, electric heat pump instead of a gas boiler). Diet matters, especially cutting beef and lamb. Solar panels are worth it if you can afford them and own your roof. Switching to a renewable energy supplier helps signal demand. What she explicitly warns against is spending emotional energy on the small stuff (plastic bags, standby mode, recycling) because it triggers **moral licensing**: feeling like you have "done your bit" by handling a low-impact thing, and using it as a pass on the high-impact things. Her example: someone feels virtuous about bringing a reusable bag, then fills it with beef and dairy.
