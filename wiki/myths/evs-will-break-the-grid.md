# Myth: electric cars will break the electricity grid

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

The scary maths only works if every EV charges at exactly the same moment, which never happens: even if every UK car went electric, National Grid estimates peak demand would rise about 25 to 29%, which is manageable. With smart charging, EVs can even help the grid, charging when power is cheap and clean and feeding some back when demand spikes.

## Why it sounds right

If you multiply the numbers naïvely, they look frightening. If every UK driver plugged in at 6:30pm and drew maximum power, peak demand from cars alone would be about **230 GW**, nearly four times the UK's current 60 GW peak. Newspapers love this comparison. It just isn't how charging works.

## What is actually true

Real demand from EVs is much smaller, because most cars sit parked most of the time and can charge whenever suits the grid:

- There are **33 million cars** in the UK, and the average driver does ~20 miles a day.
- Most drivers wouldn't need to charge every day. Once a week is often enough.
- National Grid estimates that if all UK cars went electric, peak demand would rise by **25 to 29%**.
- If all UK cars, vans and lorries went electric, the peak would rise about 40%.
- Total yearly generation would go from ~300 TWh to about 370 TWh (cars only) or 410 TWh (with vans and lorries).
- The UK was already generating **400 TWh in 2005**, more than would be needed today even if every car were electric.

## If they push back

**"But everyone will charge at 6pm when they get home."**

Some will, and that would be a problem, which is why smart charging and off-peak tariffs exist. Pricing that changes with the time of day shifts most charging to overnight (or midday for people with solar panels). Norway already manages this well, spreading charging across morning, evening and overnight.

**"The grid can't handle a 30% peak rise."**

Grid capacity grows over time as power stations and power lines are added. The UK's peak was **already higher in 2005** than it would be today with every car electric. It fell after 2005 because industry and household appliances became more efficient. EVs will push it back up gradually, in step with grid expansion.

**"What about extreme weather events, like Texas in winter?"**

The Texas grid failed because of a lack of investment, and EVs played no part. But it does point to an opportunity: **vehicle-to-grid (V2G)** turns cars into a backup power source for the grid in exactly that kind of event. Germany and the Netherlands are already piloting V2G.

## Go deeper

Smart charging does most of the work. A smart system staggers charging times across drivers, which eases the peak. Even without fancy equipment, a smart meter and an off-peak tariff nudge most people to charge overnight, when the grid has spare capacity. That alone flattens the "everyone plugs in at 6pm" scenario.

Vehicle-to-grid (V2G): modern EV batteries hold **60-80 kWh**, more than a typical home battery. If your car is plugged in and half full, it can send power back to the grid when demand and prices spike (say, 6pm on a cold evening) and charge again overnight. A UK-wide fleet of EVs taking part in V2G would work like one huge battery spread across the country.

Cutting demand still helps. Even a well-managed fleet of EVs adds demand. Walking, cycling, public transport and simply driving less all reduce the extra load on the grid. Smaller EVs help too, because they use less electricity per mile.

The real bottleneck is local connections. What often holds back EV rollout is the capacity of the **local distribution network** (the transformer at the end of your street) rather than how much power the country can generate. That's an engineering problem, and time and money can solve it.
