# Myth: electric cars will break the electricity grid

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

The scary maths only works if you assume every EV charges at the exact same moment, which nobody does. Even if every UK car went electric, the National Grid estimates peak demand would rise about 25 to 29%. That's manageable. With smart charging, EVs can actually help the grid — charging when power is cheap and clean, and even feeding it back when demand spikes.

## Why it sounds right

Because the numbers, if you multiply them naïvely, are terrifying. If every UK driver plugged in at 6:30pm and drew maximum power, peak demand from cars alone would be about **230 GW** — nearly four times the UK's current 60 GW peak. Headlines love this comparison. It just isn't how charging actually works.

## What is actually true

Real electricity demand from EVs is much smaller because most cars sit parked most of the time and can charge whenever suits the grid:

- **33 million cars** in the UK; average driver does **~20 miles a day**
- Most drivers wouldn't need to charge every day — **once a week is often enough**
- National Grid estimate: if **all UK cars went electric**, peak demand would rise by **25 to 29%**
- All UK **cars, vans and lorries** electric: peak would rise about **40%**
- Total annual generation would go from ~300 TWh to about 370 TWh (cars only) or 410 TWh (with vans/lorries)
- **The UK was already generating 400 TWh in 2005** — more than would be needed even with every car electric today

## If they push back

**"But everyone will charge at 6pm when they get home."**

Some will, and that would be a problem — which is why smart charging and off-peak tariffs exist. Time-of-use pricing shifts most charging to overnight (or midday for solar owners). Norway is already managing this well, spreading charging across morning, evening and overnight.

**"The grid can't handle a 30% peak rise."**

Grid capacity grows over time as generation and transmission expand. The UK's peak was **already higher in 2005** than it will be with every car electric today. What made it fall since 2005 was industrial efficiency and appliance efficiency. EVs will lift it back, gradually, in line with grid build-out.

**"What about extreme weather events, like Texas in winter?"**

The Texas grid failed because it was under-invested, not because of EVs. But it points to a real opportunity: **vehicle-to-grid (V2G)** turns cars into a backup source for the grid, exactly for events like that. Germany and the Netherlands are already piloting V2G.

## Go deeper

**Smart charging is the whole answer.** A smart system schedules charging at different times for different drivers, easing peak load. Even without fancy hardware, a smart meter and off-peak tariff nudges most people to charge overnight, when the grid has spare capacity. This alone flattens the "everyone plugs in at 6pm" scenario.

**Vehicle-to-grid (V2G).** Modern EV batteries are 60-80 kWh — bigger than a typical home battery. If your car is plugged in and half-full, it can push power back to the grid when demand and prices spike (say, 6pm on a cold evening) and charge again overnight. A UK-wide EV fleet participating in V2G would be, essentially, a massive distributed battery.

**Cutting demand still helps.** Even the best-managed EV fleet still adds demand. Walking, cycling, public transport and just driving less all reduce the electrification burden on the grid. Smaller EVs help too — they use less electricity per mile.

**The infrastructure question isn't cars, it's connections.** The real grid bottleneck for EV rollout is often **local distribution network capacity** (the transformer at your street corner), not national generation capacity. That is a solvable engineering problem with time and money.
