# Myth: we can just keep burning fossil fuels and capture the CO2

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

Carbon capture and storage is real and it does have a role, but it is not a way out of burning less fossil fuel. Today all the CCS plants in the world capture around one thousandth of global emissions. Even in the most optimistic 2030 case it is about 2%. We still have to cut fossil fuels fast, and use CCS only where nothing cheaper works, like cement and steel.

## Why it sounds right

Because CCS is a real technology and it does work when it works. It sounds like exactly the kind of engineering fix a wealthy society should be able to build: keep the existing power plants and factories, bolt on a capture unit at the top of the chimney, pump the CO2 underground, done. It also comes from serious voices — oil companies, IPCC reports, government net-zero plans — so people assume it must be doing more than it is.

## What is actually true

CCS today captures a very small share of global emissions and its track record is poor:

- **CCS is running at 45 sites worldwide**, capturing about **50 million tonnes of CO2 per year** — around **0.1% of global emissions** (Hannah Ritchie, *Not the End of the World*)
- **Around 50 more plants could be online by 2030**, taking the total to about **0.4% of emissions**
- Even in a very optimistic scenario where CCS captures **1 billion tonnes a year by 2030** (a number that appears in many net-zero plans), that is still only **2% of world emissions**
- Track record is bad: by 2020, **149 CCS projects were meant to be running; more than 100 were cancelled**
- In the US, **around 80% of CCS projects have failed**, mostly due to cost and technology readiness

Where CCS could genuinely help is a narrow list: **the last 20-30% of electricity** in regions with poor sun and wind, and hard-to-decarbonise industries like **cement, steel and fertiliser (~15% of world emissions)**. For transport and most electricity, there is no useful role — solar, wind and batteries do the job cheaper.

Cost matters too. Oxford researchers modelled two paths: one where CCS handles half of all emission cuts, and one where it handles a tenth. The high-CCS path costs **over $1 trillion more per year**, tens of trillions by 2050.

## If they push back

**"So we should invest heavily in CCS then?"**

Some investment is worthwhile so the technology can improve, but it should get much less funding than solar, wind, batteries and electrification. Using CCS heavily is far more expensive than using it only where nothing else works.

**"Isn't it in every credible net-zero plan?"**

Yes, but it is treated as a **small residual solution** for the hard cases (cement, steel, fertiliser), not a substitute for cutting fossil fuel use. When oil companies present CCS as their central strategy, that is a different claim from what climate scientists are saying.

**"Fossil fuel companies can afford it — why not just make them pay?"**

They can and they should. In 2022 they made **$4 trillion in profit**. At roughly $100 to capture one tonne, that would have paid to capture 40 billion tonnes, more than a year of global emissions. The problem is not the money, it is the political will to make them do it. And even then, capture would not solve the other side effects of fossil fuel extraction — air pollution, methane leaks, spills, land use.

## Go deeper

There is a specific pattern to watch for. **CCS gets used as a reason to delay cutting emissions today** — a fossil fuel company says "we will keep drilling because we will capture the CO2 eventually." That is the version of CCS the myth is really about. The version climate scientists advocate for is much narrower: a residual solution for the small share of emissions we cannot cut any other way, funded largely by the industry that created the problem.

Direct air capture (DAC) is a related but distinct technology — pulling CO2 out of ambient air rather than from a chimney. It is even more energy-intensive and expensive per tonne, and even further from scale. It has a similar role: keep researching, do not use it as an excuse.

**Research is moving on the cost problem.** In April 2026 a team at **Chiba University** published a new class of solid carbon adsorbents ("viciazites") that release captured CO2 at temperatures below 60°C — well below the 100°C+ needed for conventional aqueous amine scrubbing. That means the material could potentially be regenerated with industrial waste heat instead of active heating, cutting operating costs. This is a lab result, not deployment, and it doesn't shift the verdict on this myth: even a cheaper CCS is still deployed at 45 sites capturing 0.1% of emissions. But it does support the narrower case for CCS in hard-to-abate sectors like cement and steel where alternatives are limited.
