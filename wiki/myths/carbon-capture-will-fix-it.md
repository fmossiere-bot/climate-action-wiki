# Myth: we can just keep burning fossil fuels and capture the CO2

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

Carbon capture is real and it has a role, but it can't let us carry on burning fossil fuels. All the capture plants in the world today catch about a thousandth of global emissions, and even the most hopeful plans for 2030 only reach about 2%. We still need to cut fossil fuels fast and keep carbon capture for the few places where nothing cheaper works, like cement and steel.

## Why it sounds right

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) does work in the right conditions, and the idea is appealing. Keep the power stations and factories we already have, fit a unit to the chimney, pump the CO2 underground, and carry on. It also comes up in serious places: oil company plans, IPCC reports, government net-zero strategies. So it's natural to assume it is doing more than it actually is.

## What is actually true

CCS captures a very small share of global emissions, and its track record is poor.

- CCS runs at **45 sites** worldwide, capturing about 50 million tonnes of CO2 a year. That is around 0.1% of global emissions (Hannah Ritchie, *Not the End of the World*).
- Around 50 more plants could be running by 2030, taking the total to about 0.4% of emissions.
- Even a very optimistic scenario, capturing 1 billion tonnes a year by 2030 (a figure that appears in many net-zero plans), would cover only 2% of world emissions.
- By 2020, 149 CCS projects were supposed to be running. More than 100 were cancelled.
- In the US, around 80% of CCS projects have failed, mostly because of cost or immature technology.

Where CCS can help is a short list: the last 20 to 30% of electricity in regions with little sun and wind, and industries that are hard to clean up, such as **cement, steel and fertiliser**, which together make up about 15% of world emissions. For transport and most electricity it has no real role, because solar, wind and batteries do the job more cheaply.

Cost matters too. Oxford researchers compared two paths: one where CCS delivers half of all emission cuts, and one where it delivers a tenth. The CCS-heavy path costs over $1 trillion more per year, adding up to tens of trillions by 2050.

## If they push back

**"So we should invest heavily in CCS then?"**

Some investment makes sense so the technology keeps improving, but far less than goes to solar, wind, batteries and electrification. Relying on CCS everywhere costs much more than using it only where nothing else works.

**"Isn't it in every credible net-zero plan?"**

Yes, as a small backstop for the hard cases (cement, steel, fertiliser), not as a replacement for cutting fossil fuel use. When an oil company makes CCS its main strategy, that is a different claim from the one climate scientists are making.

**"Fossil fuel companies can afford it, so why not make them pay?"**

They can, and they should. In 2022 they made about $4 trillion in profit. At roughly $100 a tonne, that could have paid to capture 40 billion tonnes, more than a year of global emissions. Money isn't the obstacle, political will is. And even then, capture would do nothing about the other harms of extracting fossil fuels: air pollution, methane leaks, spills and land use.

## Go deeper

The version of this myth to watch for is CCS used as a reason to delay. A fossil fuel company says it will keep drilling because it will capture the CO2 one day. Climate scientists mean something much narrower: a backstop for the small share of emissions we can't cut any other way, paid for largely by the industry that caused the problem.

Direct air capture (DAC) is related but different. It pulls CO2 out of the open air rather than from a chimney, which makes it even more energy-hungry and expensive per tonne, and even further from working at scale. The same logic applies: keep researching it, but don't treat it as an excuse.

Research is chipping away at the cost. In April 2026 a team at Chiba University described new solid materials, called viciazites, that release captured CO2 below 60°C, well under the 100°C or more that today's liquid amine systems need. That could let plants reuse waste heat instead of burning extra fuel, which would cut running costs. It is a lab result, not a working plant, and it doesn't change the verdict here: CCS still runs at 45 sites and catches about 0.1% of emissions. It does strengthen the case for using CCS in cement and steel, where there are few alternatives. See the [viciazites source summary](../sources/viciazites-cheaper-ccs-chiba-2026.md).
