# Myth: nuclear power takes too long to build to matter for the climate

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

Western nuclear plants often take over a decade because we stopped building them for 30 years and had to relearn how, but countries that build regularly, like China, South Korea and India, do it in five to eight. Nuclear won't outpace solar and wind, but "too slow" describes recent Western projects, not what's possible.

## Why it sounds right

The Western examples really are bad. Construction of **Hinkley Point C** in the UK started in 2017, and it now looks unlikely to produce power before 2031: at least 14 years for the first unit. Flamanville in France started in 2007 and only opened in 2024, more than a decade late. Both are poor advertisements for nuclear just when climate policy needs speed. Looking at those two projects and concluding nuclear is too slow is a reasonable reaction.

## What is actually true

Those Western projects are outliers. Data on over 600 nuclear plants built since 1950 shows:

- Around **80% took less than a decade** to build
- 1 in 5 took less than 5 years
- The global average construction time is 6 to 8 years

China and South Korea are building at about 5-7 years per plant today. India is building too, more slowly. Nearly all US and EU plants are over 30 years old, because those countries largely stopped building. Restarting from cold makes everything slow.

Ritchie points to four reasons the West is slow. Almost every Western plant is a one-off design, so there is no learning curve. Regulations change partway through construction, and each design change costs a great deal of time and money. The skilled workforce has gone, because the UK and France stopped building and the expertise retired. And big, complex construction projects tend to overrun in general, with nuclear at the harder end.

## If they push back

**"Solar and wind are faster to build, why bother with nuclear?"**

They are faster, and most new capacity will be renewable. But grids in northern countries with weak winter sun still need steady low-carbon backup, and nuclear is one of the few options. It works alongside solar and wind rather than against them.

**"The Hinkley Point disaster proves the whole industry is broken."**

Hinkley is a bad advertisement, but it isn't typical. It's a first-of-its-kind design in a country that hadn't built for 20 years. The lesson is to build the same design many times in a row, keep the rules stable, and keep the workforce employed between projects.

**"We don't have time to wait 8 years."**

An 8-year build starting today finishes in the mid-2030s, which is still useful. Renewables can handle near-term growth while nuclear plants under construction come online to replace retiring fossil fuel plants.

## Go deeper

### Where speed comes from

The countries building fast (China, South Korea, India) keep a skilled workforce continuously employed and **repeat the same reactor design**. It's basic industrial learning. Countries that stopped and restarted, like the UK, France and the US, have to relearn it from scratch at very high cost.

### Small modular reactors

One proposed fix for the big-project problem is to go smaller. **Small modular reactors (SMRs)** are designed to be built in factories and shipped to site, which cuts the biggest source of overruns: complex on-site construction. Google has ordered several SMRs to power data centres by 2030. The technology is still early, and it isn't yet clear whether it will deliver.

### Standardise, then repeat

This isn't new advice. South Korea does it now, and France did it in the 1970s and 80s, when the Messmer Plan built about 56 reactors on essentially two standard designs. The template exists.

### The fair comparison

A plant that starts today and connects in 2032 would deliver steady power sooner than most gas plants planned today, once permits and grid connection queues are counted. Nuclear feels slow because we compare it with renewables. Compared with other sources of steady power, its build time is not unusual.
