# Myth: nuclear power takes too long to build to matter for the climate

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

Nuclear plants in the West often take over a decade because we haven't built any for 30 years and had to relearn how. Countries that keep building regularly — China, South Korea, India — do it in five to eight. Nuclear won't outrun solar and wind, but calling it "too slow" reflects the recent Western experience, not what's physically possible.

## Why it sounds right

Because the Western examples are shockingly bad. **Hinkley Point C** in the UK started construction in 2017; it now looks unlikely to produce power before 2031 — at least **14 years** for the first unit. **Flamanville** in France started in 2007 and only opened in 2024 — more than a **decade late**. Both are terrible advertisements for nuclear power at exactly the moment climate policy needs speed. Anyone looking at those two projects and concluding "nuclear is too slow" is being reasonable.

## What is actually true

The Western examples are outliers, not the global norm. Looking at data on over 600 nuclear plants built since 1950:

- Around **80% took less than a decade** to build
- **1 in 5 took less than 5 years**
- Global average construction time: **6 to 8 years**

Where is the speed today?

- **China and South Korea** are currently building at about **5-7 years per plant**
- **India** is building too, more slowly
- Nearly all US and EU plants are **>30 years old** — those countries essentially stopped building. When you restart from cold, everything is slow.

Why the West is slow (Ritchie's diagnosis):
1. **Plants aren't repeated.** Almost every Western plant is a bespoke design. No learning curve.
2. **Regulations shift mid-build.** Design changes partway through cost enormous amounts of time and money.
3. **Skilled workforce gone.** The UK and France stopped building; the expertise left with the retirements.
4. **Big complex construction projects generally overrun.** Nuclear is on the harder end of that class.

## If they push back

**"Solar and wind are faster to build, why bother with nuclear?"**

They are, and most new capacity will be renewable. But grids in high-latitude countries with poor winter sun still need firm low-carbon backup, and nuclear is one of the few options. It's a partner to solar and wind, not a competitor.

**"The Hinkley Point disaster proves the whole industry is broken."**

Hinkley is a bad advertisement, but it isn't representative. It's a first-of-its-kind design in a country that hadn't built for 20 years. The lesson is not "nuclear is impossible" — it's "build the same design many times in a row, keep regulations stable, and keep the workforce employed between projects."

**"We don't have time to wait 8 years."**

An 8-year build starting today finishes in the mid-2030s. That is still useful. Better yet: renewables handle the near-term growth while nuclear plants under construction come online to backfill the retiring fossil fleet.

## Go deeper

**Where speed comes from.** The countries building fast — China, South Korea, India — do it by keeping a **skilled workforce continuously employed** and **repeating the same reactor design**. This is basic industrial learning-curve logic. The countries that stopped and restarted (UK, France, US) have to relearn it from scratch, at very high cost.

**Small modular reactors (SMRs).** One proposed fix for the big-complex-project problem is going smaller. SMRs are designed to be **manufactured in factories and shipped to site**, cutting the biggest source of overrun (on-site construction complexity). Google has ordered several SMRs to power data centres by 2030. It's still an early technology; whether it delivers on the promise is not yet clear.

**Standardise, then repeat.** This isn't really new advice — it's what South Korea does now and what France did in the 1970s and 80s (the Messmer Plan built ~56 reactors on essentially two standard designs). The template exists.

**The real time comparison.** A plant that starts today and connects in 2032 is faster to firm power than most gas plants planned today, once permitting and grid connection queues are counted. Nuclear feels slow because we compare it to renewables. Compared to other firm-power alternatives, its build time is not the outlier.
