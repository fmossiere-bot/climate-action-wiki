# Myth: we'll never be able to make low-carbon steel

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

We already know how to make green steel: use hydrogen instead of coal, or run the process on electricity, and the first big plants are being built now in Sweden, Spain and the US. It costs about 25% more per tonne, but that's roughly $150 on a car and $400 on a house.

## Why it sounds right

About two-thirds of the world's steel is made with coal (strictly, with "coke", which is made from coal). That has been true for more than a century, and anyone who works in steel knows coal is at the centre of it. If you frame the problem as "steel needs coal", cleaning it up sounds impossible. But coal isn't there because steel physically requires it. It's there because it strips the oxygen out of iron ore, and hydrogen can do the same job without producing CO2. The hard part is scale and cost, not the technology.

## What is actually true

Steel causes **7% of global greenhouse gas emissions**. About ⅔ of the world's steel is made with coke from coal, and the remaining ⅓ is recycled scrap melted in electric arc furnaces. Each tonne of new steel burns about 0.8 tonnes of coal, which accounts for most of the emissions.

There are two proven ways to remove the coal. The first is hydrogen: it strips oxygen from iron ore just as coal does, but the by-product is water (H2O) rather than CO2. It needs clean hydrogen to work. The second is direct electrification, using electricity instead of burning coal. A third option, fitting carbon capture to existing plants, also works but costs more.

The scale is large but manageable:

- 1 tonne of green steel needs about 4 MWh of electricity
- Making the world's 1.3 billion tonnes of steel this way would need 5,200 TWh, about ⅕ of the world's electricity generation today
- Total investment needed worldwide is about €1.3 trillion, or €50 billion/year over 25 years (H2 Green Steel figures)

The extra cost is small once the steel is in a finished product:

- Green steel: about 25% more per tonne
- A $25,000 car: about $150 more (0.5-1%)
- A $250,000 house: about $400 more (0.2%)
- An offshore wind turbine: 0.5-2% more

Several companies are already building it. H2 Green Steel has a plant under construction in Sweden, with government support. ArcelorMittal is converting a Spanish plant to hydrogen, also with government support. Boston Metal, backed by Microsoft, uses direct electrification and expects its first plant in 2026. Most European steel companies have committed to cutting emissions by 30-50% or more by 2030.

## If they push back

**"Green steel is a lab curiosity."**

Not any more. H2 Green Steel's Boden plant is under construction, Boston Metal's first plant is expected in 2026, and ArcelorMittal has committed billions to hydrogen conversion. These are commercial-scale projects, not lab demonstrations.

**"It needs too much electricity."**

5,200 TWh is about a fifth of current world generation, which is a lot. But a 25% rise in global electricity demand over 25 years fits the growth the grid is already planning for EVs and heat pumps. It's part of the transition rather than something blocking it.

**"25% more expensive means nobody buys it."**

The effect on finished products is tiny. A car maker paying 25% more for its steel raises car prices by less than 1%, and buyers won't notice. What counts is car makers asking for green steel, because their own Scope 3 targets (emissions in their supply chain) require it.

## Go deeper

**Where the coal goes.** In a traditional blast furnace, iron ore is mixed with coke (baked coal). At high temperature, the carbon in the coke pulls oxygen out of the ore, leaving iron plus CO2. The coal isn't acting as fuel here. It's a chemical "reducing agent", so replacing it means finding something else that removes oxygen. Hydrogen does exactly that: iron ore + hydrogen → iron + water vapour, with no CO2 from the chemistry itself.

**Why scrap recycling isn't enough.** About ⅓ of the world's steel is already made from scrap in electric arc furnaces, with low emissions if the electricity is clean. Recycling more scrap makes sense, but there isn't enough scrap in the world to make all steel this way. New steel from iron ore is still needed, especially in developing countries, and green steel is how that share gets cleaned up.

**Location will matter.** Green steel plants need cheap clean electricity. Today's steelworks are often near coal mines or in countries with expensive power. The next generation is being built where renewables are cheap: Sweden's hydro and wind, Spain's solar, and perhaps later Morocco or Australia. The map of the steel industry is about to change, following where clean electricity is available rather than where steel is used.

**Buyers are starting to pull.** Car makers, wind turbine manufacturers and construction firms all have Scope 3 targets, and their supply chains contain a lot of steel. Volvo, Mercedes and BMW have all committed to buying significant volumes of green steel. That demand is what makes the economics work: plants like H2 Green Steel signed 5-year supply contracts before they even opened.
