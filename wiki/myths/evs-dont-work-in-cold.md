# Myth: electric cars don't work in cold weather

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

EVs do lose some range in the cold, typically 10 to 20%, and charging is a bit slower. But for everyday driving, well under 100 miles, it barely matters, and the coldest countries, like Norway, Sweden and Finland, have the highest rates of EV ownership.

## Why it sounds right

Because the range drop is real. Compare summer and winter figures for the same car and the winter one is lower. Anyone who has driven an EV through a proper winter can tell you so. This myth doesn't come from nowhere, and the fair answer is "yes, a bit."

## What is actually true

The most detailed study comes from the **Norwegian Automobile Federation (NAF)**, which tested 50 different EV models in Norway. Each drove the same route in summer and winter, at temperatures of 0°C to -10°C:

- **Average range drop: 19%.** A car with an advertised range of 450 km managed about 364 km in winter.
- **Tesla Model Y (best performer): 11% loss** (280 → 226 km)
- **Skoda Enyaq iV80 (worst performer): 32% loss** (360 → 245 km)
- Similar studies elsewhere find drops of 10 to 20%, so the pattern holds up.
- **Fast charging is also slower**: 37 minutes on average in winter against 33 in summer, about 10% slower. Some cars (BMW iX) saw charging time jump by a third.

For most drivers this rarely matters. In the US, only about **5% of journeys** are longer than 50 km. Even the EV with the biggest range drop still comfortably handles a 100 km round trip in the cold.

The real-world proof: **Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Finland and Iceland**, five of the coldest countries in the world, have some of the highest rates of EV ownership anywhere.

## If they push back

**"So EVs lose 20% of their range. That's a lot."**

For an EV with 400 km of range, a 20% drop still leaves 320 km. Very few people drive that far on a normal day. Where it does matter is long winter road trips, and even those are manageable with one extra charging stop.

**"Nobody wants to be stuck at a charger in the freezing cold."**

That's a fair concern, and there are fixes. Many new EVs have **heat pumps**, which warm the cabin and battery more efficiently. Pre-heating the battery before you reach the charger also brings charging time back closer to summer levels.

**"What about very extreme cold, like -30°C?"**

Range drops further, and public charging gets patchy. The Norwegian tests don't cover Arctic extremes. For driving at -30°C, a hybrid or petrol backup might make more sense, but that's a tiny share of the world's driving.

## Go deeper

Why cold hurts batteries: the chemical reactions inside a battery slow down when it's cold, so it works less efficiently. This isn't a design flaw manufacturers are hiding. It's physics, and it affects every battery in every country. It can be managed with heat pumps (which most new EVs now have) and battery pre-heating (which most new EVs also offer).

What car labels should show: advertised range is usually a figure for warm weather and ideal conditions. Manufacturers should publish a **"cold range"** figure alongside the summer one, so buyers know what to expect. That's a question for regulators, since nothing technical stops manufacturers publishing it today.

Cold slows charging too. If you know you'll fast-charge in winter, pre-heat the battery for 20-30 minutes before you arrive. Most modern EVs can do this automatically through the navigation system as you approach a charger.
