# Myth: electric cars don't work in cold weather

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

EVs do lose range in the cold — typically 10 to 20% — and charging is a bit slower. That is real. But for daily driving (well under 100 miles), it barely matters. And the coldest countries in the world, Norway, Sweden, Finland, all have the highest EV adoption rates. Cold weather is a limitation to plan around, not a reason not to switch.

## Why it sounds right

Because the range drop is real. If you compare summer and winter numbers for the same car, the winter figure is genuinely lower. Anyone with an EV who has driven it through a proper winter can confirm this. So this myth doesn't come from nowhere — the honest answer is "yes, a bit."

## What is actually true

The most detailed study on this comes from the **Norwegian Automobile Federation (NAF)**, which tested **50 different EV models** in Norway. Same route summer and winter, temperatures 0°C to -10°C:

- **Average range drop: 19%.** A car with 450 km advertised range managed about **364 km** in winter.
- **Tesla Model Y (best performer): 11% loss** (280 → 226 km)
- **Skoda Enyaq iV80 (worst performer): 32% loss** (360 → 245 km)
- Similar studies elsewhere find **10 to 20% drops**, so the pattern holds up
- **Fast charging is also slower**: 37 minutes average in winter vs 33 in summer — about 10% slower. Some cars (BMW iX) saw charging time jump by a third.

For most drivers this rarely matters. In the **US, only about 5% of journeys are longer than 50 km**. Even the worst-affected EV with the biggest range drop still comfortably handles a 100 km round trip in the cold.

The real-world confirmation: **Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Finland and Iceland** — five of the coldest countries in the world — have some of the highest EV adoption rates on Earth.

## If they push back

**"So EVs lose 20% of their range. That's a lot."**

For a 400 km EV, a 20% drop is still 320 km. Very few people drive that far on a normal day. The situations where it actually matters are long winter road trips, and even those are manageable with one extra charge stop.

**"Nobody wants to be stuck at a charger in the freezing cold."**

Real concern. Fixes exist: many new EVs have **heat pumps** to warm cabin and battery more efficiently, and **battery pre-heating** before you arrive at the charger cuts charging time back closer to summer levels.

**"What about very extreme cold, like -30°C?"**

Range drops further and public infrastructure gets patchy. The Norwegian tests don't cover Arctic extremes. For -30°C driving, a hybrid or petrol backup might genuinely make more sense — but that is a tiny share of global driving.

## Go deeper

**Why cold hurts batteries.** Cold batteries are chemically less efficient — the reactions inside them slow down. This is not a design flaw manufacturers are covering up; it is a physics constraint that affects every battery in every country. The good news is that it can be managed with heat pumps (which most new EVs now have) and battery pre-heating (which most new EVs also offer).

**What car labels should really show.** Advertised range is usually a warm-weather, ideal-condition number. Manufacturers should publish honest **"cold range"** figures alongside the summer number so buyers know what to expect. This is a regulatory question, not a technical one — nothing stops manufacturers publishing it today.

**Cold slows charging too.** If you know you'll fast-charge in winter, pre-heat the battery for 20-30 minutes before you arrive. Most modern EVs let you schedule this from the navigation system automatically as you approach a charger.
