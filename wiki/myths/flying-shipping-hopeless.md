# Myth: there's no hope for low-carbon flying and shipping

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

Flying and shipping are the hardest to clean up, but hard isn't impossible: Norway already runs 60+ electric ferries and Maersk is building 18 methanol-powered container ships. They'll probably be the last sectors to get there, not the ones that never do.

## Why it sounds right

The physics really is unforgiving. A container ship crossing the Pacific carries 80,000 tonnes of cargo for weeks. A jumbo jet lifts hundreds of tonnes to 40,000 feet and burns huge amounts of energy every second to do it. Batteries can't come close to that. Anyone who has read up on replacing jet fuel knows the shortlist is short, and none of the options is easy. So "hopeless" feels grounded, until you look at what is being tested.

## What is actually true

Each sector is a fairly small slice of the problem, and both have real options. Some already work for short trips; others are being tested for long ones.

How big the problem is:

- Aviation: **2-3% of global emissions**
- Shipping: 2-3% of global emissions
- Aviation's emissions have doubled since 1990
- Aviation adds extra warming from contrails, because water vapour high in the sky warms the planet more than it does at ground level
- About 40% of everything shipped around the world is fossil fuels. As we move off fossil fuels, demand for shipping drops by itself.

Short trips, already working:

- Norway has **60+ electric ferries** running, and short flights are being electrified too
- Today's batteries manage about 100 km, which is enough for ferries and short-hop flights but not long-haul

Long trips, options being tested:

- Biofuels, made from waste fats or vegetable oils. The catch: making all aviation fuel from vegetable oil would need 250-300 million tonnes a year. That is more than double the world's current vegetable oil production, so it would compete with food.
- Hydrogen, which is clean where it is used. Powering all of today's aviation with it would need about 4,000 TWh of extra electricity, or 15-20% of the world's electricity today.
- Synthetic fuels made from hydrogen. They face similar electricity limits, but existing aircraft can use them without changes.
- Direct air capture to offset the emissions. If capturing CO2 from the air gets down to about $100 a tonne, flight fuel costs would rise by about 50%. That is cheaper than hydrogen at today's prices.

Commercial progress:

- Maersk is building **18 methanol-powered container ships**, expected to be running by the end of 2025
- "Green" methanol costs $320-720 a tonne today. It needs to fall to about $250 to compete with fossil fuel.

## If they push back

**"Battery-electric long-haul flights are impossible."**

That's right: batteries don't hold enough energy for their weight, and probably won't for decades. But long-haul isn't the only kind of flight. Hydrogen or synthetic fuels can cover it, and batteries work for short-haul, which is a real slice of aviation.

**"Biofuels compete with food."**

Yes, that is a real limit. Making all aviation fuel from vegetable oil would use more oil than the world grows for food, and fuel from waste fats has a much lower ceiling. So biofuels will probably cover part of aviation, not all of it. That's fine, because no single fuel was ever going to do everything.

**"Hydrogen aviation needs a fifth of world electricity."**

For all of today's aviation, yes, and that is a real problem of scale. In practice hydrogen flying would grow as the grid grows: the more clean electricity comes online, the more hydrogen makes sense. It doesn't have to solve aviation on day one.

**"Shipping and aviation avoid climate rules."**

True, and frustrating. No country is responsible for international flights or shipping, so both industries have been able to sidestep climate talks for decades. This is a problem of rules and oversight more than technology. The fix is to bring them formally under UN climate accountability.

## Go deeper

**A small group flies most.** In many rich countries, about 1% of people take about 50% of all flights. A frequent flyer tax targets that small group without hitting the family that flies once a year to see relatives. It is also easier to get through politically than a general tax on flights, and it is the kind of fairness most climate policy lacks.

**Private jets.** Per passenger-kilometre, a private jet emits roughly 5-14× more than a commercial flight, sometimes more. The people who fly private are also the people best able to pay for research into clean fuels. Taxing private flying heavily would do a lot for the climate at little political cost.

**Contrails.** The warming from jet contrails may roughly equal the warming from aviation's CO2. Small changes to flight paths, avoiding the humid air where contrails form, could cut aviation's climate impact a good deal without changing the fuel. NATS (UK air traffic control) and others are researching this.

**Shipping has a fuel that works today.** Unlike aviation, shipping has green methanol, which can be used with few changes. Maersk's 18 methanol ships come online in 2025 and the cost gap is closing. For this reason, shipping may clean up faster than aviation.

**What you can do.** Fly less where you can: swap short business trips for video calls and take the train around Europe. Some flying will still happen. For the flights you need, pay a real offset price rather than buying cheap offsets that do little. And push governments to bring aviation and shipping under UN climate accountability.
