# Myth: there's no hope for low-carbon flying and shipping

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

Aviation and shipping are the hardest sectors to decarbonise, but "hard" isn't "impossible." Norway already runs 60+ electric ferries. Maersk is building 18 methanol-powered container ships. Short flights and ferries can go electric today. For long-haul, we don't yet have a clear winner between hydrogen, biofuels and synthetic fuels — but each has real options being tested. These will probably be the last sectors to fully decarbonise, not sectors that never do.

## Why it sounds right

Because the physics is unusually unforgiving. A container ship crossing the Pacific carries 80,000 tonnes of cargo for weeks. A jumbo jet lifts hundreds of tonnes to 40,000 feet using enormous amounts of energy per second. Nothing about battery-electric replicates that. Anyone who's read a technical paper on jet fuel replacement knows the shortlist is very small, and none of the options is easy. So "hopeless" feels grounded — until you look at what's actually being tested.

## Why it isn't as hopeless as it sounds

**Scale, honestly:**
- Aviation: **2-3% of global emissions**
- Shipping: **2-3% of global emissions**
- Aviation's emissions have **doubled since 1990**
- Aviation adds extra warming from **contrails** (water vapour high in the sky is a stronger warming agent than at ground level)
- **About 40% of everything shipped globally is fossil fuels**. Move off fossil fuels and shipping demand drops significantly on its own.

**Where progress is happening:**

**Short trips (already working):**
- **Norway has 60+ electric ferries** operating; short flights are being electrified too
- Battery limit today: about **100 km** — enough for ferries and short-hop flights, not long-haul

**Long trips (options being tested):**
- **Biofuels** — made from waste fats or vegetable oils. Problem: making all aviation biofuel from vegetable oil would need **250-300 million tonnes/year** — more than double current global vegetable oil production, so it would fight with food supply
- **Hydrogen** — clean at the point of use, but powering all today's aviation with hydrogen would need **~4,000 TWh** extra electricity — **15-20% of world electricity today**
- **Synthetic fuels made from hydrogen** — similar electricity constraints, but drop-in compatible with existing aircraft
- **Direct air capture offsets** — if DAC gets to ~$100/tonne, flight fuel costs rise about 50%, actually **cheaper** than hydrogen at today's prices

**Concrete commercial progress:**
- **Maersk** is building **18 methanol-powered container ships**, expected operational by end of 2025
- **"Green" methanol**: $320-720 per tonne today; needs to fall to ~$250 to compete with fossil fuel

## If they push back

**"Battery-electric long-haul flights are impossible."**

Correct — the energy density isn't there and probably won't be for decades. But long-haul isn't the only category, and hydrogen or synthetic fuels can serve it. Battery-electric works for short-haul, which is a real slice of aviation.

**"Biofuels compete with food."**

Yes, this is a real constraint. Vegetable-oil biofuel for all aviation would use more oil than the world grows for food. Waste-fat biofuel has a much lower ceiling. So biofuels probably handle a share of aviation, not all of it. That's fine — no single fuel is going to solve everything.

**"Hydrogen aviation needs a fifth of world electricity."**

For **all** current aviation, yes. That's a real scale problem. In practice, hydrogen aviation would grow alongside grid expansion — as more clean electricity comes online, more hydrogen becomes viable. It doesn't need to solve aviation on day one.

**"Shipping and aviation avoid climate rules."**

True and infuriating. **No country is responsible for international flights or shipping**, so both industries have been able to duck at climate talks for decades. This is a governance problem, not a technology problem. The fix is putting them formally under UN climate accountability.

## Go deeper

**The frequent-flyer concentration.** In many rich countries, **~1% of people take ~50% of all flights**. A **frequent flyer tax** targets the small group causing most emissions, without hitting the family flying once a year to see relatives. Politically much easier than a general flight tax. This is exactly the equity design missing from most climate policy.

**Private jets are a specific outrage worth naming.** A private jet emits per passenger-kilometre roughly 5-14× more than a commercial flight, sometimes more. The people who fly private are also the people most able to pay for clean fuel R&D. Aggressive taxation of private aviation is one of the highest-leverage climate policies available and one of the least politically costly.

**Contrails matter more than most emissions accounting says.** The warming effect of jet contrails may roughly equal the warming from aviation CO2 itself. Small changes in flight paths (avoiding humid regions where contrails form) could cut aviation's climate impact substantially without changing fuel. This is being actively researched by NATS (UK air traffic control) and others.

**Shipping's advantage: methanol works today.** Unlike aviation, shipping has a working near-drop-in fuel: green methanol. Maersk's 18 methanol ships come online in 2025. Cost gap is closing. Shipping may actually decarbonise faster than aviation for this reason.

**What individuals can do.** Fly less where you can — replace short business trips with video calls, take trains for European trips. Accept some flying is going to happen. Pay a real offset price on essential flights, not the cheap greenwash offsets. Push governments to bring aviation and shipping under UN climate accountability.
