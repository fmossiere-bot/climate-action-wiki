# Myth: poor countries need fossil fuels to develop

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

Poor countries need energy to develop, and today solar and wind are the cheapest new power in most of the world, so that energy doesn't have to come from fossil fuels. The real obstacle is money: they pay two to three times more than rich countries to borrow for the same clean project.

## Why it sounds right

For 100 years it was true. Every country that industrialised did it on coal, oil and gas, because there was no serious alternative. In the 20th century, energy and fossil fuels became so tangled up that people still treat them as the same thing. There's also a real fairness point inside the claim. Rich countries burned fossil fuels for centuries to get where they are, then told poorer countries not to. That hypocrisy is real, and people are right to be wary when it comes wrapped in climate messaging.

## What is actually true

Fossil fuels were the fuel of development, but it's **energy in general** that lifts people out of poverty, and the cheapest new energy is now clean.

The emissions at stake are small. The 48 countries of sub-Saharan Africa (excluding South Africa) hold 14% of the world's population but produce only 0.6% of global CO2 emissions. Even if their electricity tripled and every kWh came from natural gas, world emissions would rise by only about 0.6%, while transforming people's lives.

Indoor air pollution from cooking with wood and charcoal kills over 2 million people a year. A short-term switch to kerosene or gas would save lives without much harm to the climate, and clean cooking gas often goes hand in hand with electrification in the longer run.

Poorer countries are well placed for clean energy. They have plenty of sun and wind, a large share of the minerals needed for batteries, and no ageing fossil fuel plants to write off, so they can build modern systems from scratch.

It's already happening:

- Namibia, Brazil, Chile and Morocco get a higher share of their electricity from solar and wind than the EU or the US does.
- Pakistan has a rooftop solar boom, as families make their own power to cope with an unreliable grid.
- South Africa shows the same pattern after repeated failures of its coal-based grid.
- Kigali (Rwanda) banned new petrol motorbikes in 2025 to cut urban air pollution.

The bottleneck is **capital**, not technology:

- Loans for a solar plant in South Africa, India or Mexico cost about twice as much (about 12%) as in rich countries (about 5%). In low-income countries rates are higher still.
- Rich countries promised $100 billion a year in climate finance to poorer countries by 2020. They missed that target by about $20 billion and only reached it years late, with some of the money simply old aid budgets relabelled as green finance.
- The G7 has blocked funding for any fossil fuel project in Africa, while its own members responded to the war in Ukraine by firing up coal plants and building new LNG terminals.

## If they push back

**"But some fossil fuels will still be needed."**

Fair. Some fossil fuel use during the transition, especially gas for cooking and industry, is expected and defensible. Nobody needs to ban all fossil fuels in poor countries. The point is that they aren't locked into fossil fuels when a cheaper, cleaner path is open, as long as the finance is there.

**"Rich countries have no right to lecture."**

Agreed, and that isn't the argument. The argument is that **the finance system** is what's holding poor countries back from clean energy. Rich countries can fix that by delivering the climate finance they pledged, offering fair borrowing rates and no longer blocking regional development banks. Blocking fossil fuels while offering nothing in their place is the worst of both worlds.

**"China is undercutting everyone with cheap solar and EVs."**

Yes, and for poorer countries that's mostly good news, because it brings down the cost of building clean energy. Rich countries can either help spread affordable Chinese clean tech or build a competing industry. Complaining about low prices doesn't help the countries that most need cheap solar.

## Go deeper

### Who is making the argument

Some of the loudest voices for "let Africa burn coal" are Western fossil fuel lobbyists rather than African governments, so it's worth checking who's speaking. African energy debates are usually more nuanced. People want energy access fast, and they're often frustrated both with the rich-world "no coal" position and with the rich world's failure to pay for alternatives. Both frustrations deserve a hearing.

### The double standards

Ritchie gives several examples. During the Russia-Ukraine war, fertiliser prices shot up. The EU delayed a promised programme to help Africa make its own fertiliser, on **environmental grounds**, while EU farmers were using 5 to 10 times more fertiliser per hectare than African farmers. The G7 blocked fossil fuel finance for Africa while some G7 members restarted their own coal plants. Negotiators from poorer countries raise this credibility gap at COPs.

### What helps

- Deliver on climate finance pledges with new money, not relabelled aid
- Offer fair borrowing rates, so clean energy is affordable to build where the sun and wind are strongest
- Let Chinese clean tech compete on price, or build a competing industry, but stop using trade rules against the transition
- For people in rich countries: buying clean tech (solar, EVs, heat pumps) brings costs down for everyone, because the more is built, the cheaper it gets
