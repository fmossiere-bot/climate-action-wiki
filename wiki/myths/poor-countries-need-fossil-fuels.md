# Myth: poor countries need fossil fuels to develop

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

Poor countries need **energy** to develop, not fossil fuels specifically. Because those two got tangled up during the 20th century, people assume they are the same thing. They are not any more. Solar and wind are now the cheapest new power in most of the world, and poor countries have the sun, the wind, and the mineral resources to build modern grids without repeating the coal era. The catch is finance: they pay two to three times more to borrow for the same clean project than rich countries do.

## Why it sounds right

Because for 100 years it was true. Every country that industrialised did so on coal, oil and gas. Nobody had a serious alternative. There is also a real fairness argument buried in the claim: rich countries burned fossil fuels for centuries to get where they are, then turned around and told poorer countries not to. That hypocrisy is real, and people are right to be suspicious when it comes wrapped in climate messaging.

## What is actually true

The premise, that fossil fuels are the necessary fuel for development, is no longer accurate. Fossil fuels **were** the fuel; **energy in general** is what lifts people out of poverty. Two related facts change the picture:

- **The 48 countries in sub-Saharan Africa (excluding South Africa) hold 14% of the world's population but produce only 0.6% of global CO2 emissions.** Even if their electricity tripled and every kWh came from natural gas, world emissions would rise by only about **0.6%** — while transforming lives.
- **Indoor air pollution kills over 2 million people a year** (cooking with wood and charcoal). A short-term shift to kerosene or gas would save lives without materially harming the climate, and clean cooking gas is often paired with electrification long-term.

The clean-energy path is now competitive on cost, and poor countries are structurally well placed for it. They have:

- Loads of sun and wind
- A large share of the minerals needed for batteries
- **No stranded fossil fuel plants** to write off, so they can build modern systems from scratch

The evidence that it is already happening:

- **Namibia, Brazil, Chile and Morocco** already get a higher share of electricity from solar and wind than the EU or the US does
- **Pakistan** has a boom in **rooftop solar** as families make their own power in response to grid instability
- **South Africa** shows the same pattern after its coal grid kept failing
- **Kigali (Rwanda)** banned new petrol motorbikes in 2025 to cut urban air pollution

The bottleneck is not technology, it is **capital**:

- Loans for a solar plant in **South Africa, India or Mexico cost about twice as much (~12%)** as in rich countries (~5%). In low-income countries the rates are higher still.
- Rich countries promised **$100 billion a year in climate finance** to poorer countries by 2020. That target was **missed by about $20 billion** and only reached years late, with some money simply being old aid budgets **relabelled as green finance**.
- The **G7 has blocked funding for any fossil fuel project in Africa** — while their own energy security responses to Ukraine involved firing up coal plants and building new LNG terminals.

## If they push back

**"But some fossil fuels will still be needed."**

Fair. Some transitional fossil use, especially gas for cooking and industry, is expected and defensible. The issue is not banning all fossil fuel use in poor countries. The issue is not pretending they are locked into fossil fuel dependence when the cheaper and cleaner path is available if the finance is there.

**"Rich countries have no right to lecture."**

Correct, and that is not the argument. The argument is: **the finance system, not the atmosphere, is what is holding poor countries back from clean energy**. Rich countries can either fix that (deliver the pledged climate finance, offer fair borrowing rates, stop blocking regional development banks) or stop blocking clean energy while doing nothing. The current combination is the worst option.

**"China is undercutting everyone with cheap solar and EVs."**

Yes — and for poorer countries that is largely good news, because it drives down deployment cost. Rich-country policy should either **help spread affordable Chinese clean tech worldwide** or build a competing industry. Just complaining about the low prices does not help anyone in the countries that most need cheap solar.

## Go deeper

**Why the framing is often bad-faith.** Some of the loudest voices for "let Africa burn coal" are Western fossil fuel lobbyists rather than African governments. It is worth checking who is making the argument. Serious African energy debates are usually more nuanced — they want energy access fast, and they are frequently frustrated with both the rich-world "no coal" position and the rich-world failure to deliver alternative finance. Both sides of that frustration matter.

**The specific hypocrisy list.** Ritchie names some of the sharpest examples. During the Russia-Ukraine war, fertiliser prices shot up. The EU delayed a promised programme to help Africa produce its own fertiliser on **environmental grounds**, while EU farmers were using 5 to 10 times more fertiliser per hectare than African farmers. The G7 blocked fossil-fuel finance for Africa while some G7 members restarted their own coal plants. That is the credibility gap poor-country negotiators point to at COPs.

**What actually helps.**

- **Deliver on climate finance pledges**, with new money — not relabelled aid
- **Fair borrowing rates**, so clean energy is affordable to build where the sun and wind are strongest
- **Let Chinese clean tech compete on price**, or build a competing industry — but stop weaponising trade rules against the transition
- Rich individuals: buying clean tech (solar, EVs, heat pumps) drives the cost curve down for everyone else globally, because deployment volume drives learning curves
