# Myth: renewables aren't replacing fossil fuels, they're just being added on top

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

The world isn't in one place at once. Some countries are already replacing fossil fuels with renewables and their fossil fuel use is falling — the UK, Denmark, Finland. China and most of the world are in an in-between stage: renewables are filling new demand that would otherwise have gone to fossil fuels, so fossil fuel use is growing more slowly than it would have. Almost nowhere is renewables truly "just added on top." Coal, oil and gas are each expected to peak globally within about five years.

## Why it sounds right

Because if you look at global charts of energy use, both fossil fuels and renewables are still rising at the same time. It looks like renewables are just being added, not replacing anything. And in the poorest, fastest-growing economies that is broadly what is happening today: their total energy demand is rising, so both curves go up. When someone frames this as "the energy transition is a myth," the chart looks like their evidence.

## What is actually true

The global picture hides three very different situations:

1. **Energy addition** — renewables added on top, fossil fuels grow as normal. **There are no clear real-world examples** of this at country level today.
2. **Energy displacement** — renewables fill demand that would otherwise have gone to fossil fuels. Fossil fuels still grow, but slower. **China** is the classic case.
3. **Energy transition** — renewables replace fossil fuels, so fossil fuel use actually falls. **Denmark, UK, Finland** are clear examples.

More than **half of the world's countries have already passed "peak fossil fuels" in their electricity sector**, even while total electricity demand kept rising.

- **UK**: fossil fuel electricity down by **almost half** since it peaked in 2008
- **Denmark**: down by **almost 80% since 2000**
- **Finland**: down by **more than two-thirds since 2000**
- Globally in **2022, renewables supplied 92% of new electricity demand**; fossil fuels the remaining 8%
- Between **2010 and 2022, renewables supplied 57%** of the growth in global electricity

Analysts now expect **coal, oil and gas each to hit their global peak within the next five years**. Sales of **new petrol and diesel cars already peaked in 2017**.

## If they push back

**"But global fossil fuel use is still rising."**

Yes, and that is the honest bit of the claim. It hides three different stories, and one of them (real transition) is clearly winning at country level. The global total is the sum of countries at different stages — mostly at stage 2 (displacement) today, transitioning into stage 3.

**"Renewables can only replace some of it."**

For most of the electricity system, they can do the heavy lifting. For the last 20-30% and for hard-to-abate industry, other tools are needed (nuclear, hydro, geothermal, storage, some gas as declining backup). But that is very different from "renewables aren't replacing fossil fuels." They are, and the countries doing this are the leading indicators.

**"You're cherry-picking Denmark."**

Denmark is at the leading edge, but the pattern is very general in Europe. UK, Germany, Portugal, Spain, Ireland, Italy have all seen large falls in fossil fuel electricity. Outside Europe, the US has been slower but its coal fleet is now clearly in retreat. The transition looks different in different places because it started at different times and with different endowments, not because it is not happening.

## Go deeper

**Why the confusion is easy to fall into.** The global chart is what most people see. It shows both curves rising because it aggregates all countries at once — mature grids where fossil fuels are falling, growing grids where renewables are absorbing new demand, and a very small number of countries where fossil fuel use is still growing outright. The three-way split (addition / displacement / transition) is the fix.

**The countries closest to real transition are worth knowing.** Denmark, UK, Finland, Germany, Portugal and Spain have all cut fossil fuel electricity substantially since their local peaks. Their trick has been: keep existing low-carbon capacity online (nuclear, hydro where they have it), build renewables fast, phase out coal aggressively, and use gas as a shrinking backup. Where Germany closed nuclear plants early, its transition slowed noticeably — a lesson repeated in Japan.

**What has to happen next.** Two transitions have to run in parallel. The first, cleaning up electricity, is well under way. The second, **electrifying everything else** (cars, heating, industry), is much earlier. The UK's own target (fully low-carbon electricity by 2030) implies building offshore wind and solar about **three times faster** than the pace of the last 20 years — technically possible, but demanding.
