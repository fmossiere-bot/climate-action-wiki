# Myth: renewables aren't replacing fossil fuels, they're just being added on top

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

In countries like the UK, Denmark and Finland, renewables are already replacing fossil fuels, and in China and much of the world they're covering new demand that would otherwise have gone to coal and gas. Almost nowhere are renewables simply added on top, and coal, oil and gas are each expected to peak worldwide within about five years.

## Why it sounds right

Look at a global chart of energy use and both fossil fuels and renewables are still rising. It really does look as if renewables are being added rather than replacing anything. In the poorest, fastest-growing economies, that is roughly what is happening today: total energy demand is rising, so both lines go up. When someone says "the energy transition is a myth", that chart looks like their evidence.

## What is actually true

The global total hides three quite different situations:

- **Energy addition** (stage 1): renewables are added on top and fossil fuels grow as normal. There are no clear real-world examples of this at country level today.
- **Energy displacement** (stage 2): renewables cover demand that would otherwise have gone to fossil fuels. Fossil fuels still grow, but more slowly. China is the classic case.
- **Energy transition** (stage 3): renewables replace fossil fuels, so fossil fuel use falls. Denmark, the UK and Finland are clear examples.

More than half of the world's countries have already passed **"peak fossil fuels" in electricity**, even while their total electricity demand kept rising.

- UK: fossil fuel electricity down by almost half since its peak in 2008.
- Denmark: down by almost 80% since 2000.
- Finland: down by more than two-thirds since 2000.
- Worldwide in 2022, renewables supplied 92% of new electricity demand, and fossil fuels the remaining 8%.
- Between 2010 and 2022, renewables supplied 57% of the growth in global electricity.

Analysts now expect coal, oil and gas each to reach their global peak within the next five years. Sales of new petrol and diesel cars already peaked in 2017.

## If they push back

**"But global fossil fuel use is still rising."**

Yes, and that is the true part of the claim. But the global total adds together countries at different stages. Most are at stage 2 (displacement) today and moving into stage 3 (transition), and at country level, transition is clearly gaining ground.

**"Renewables can only replace some of it."**

For most of the electricity system, they can do the heavy lifting. The last 20-30% of the grid and hard-to-clean industry need other tools: nuclear, hydro, geothermal, storage, and some gas as a shrinking backup. That is very different from saying renewables aren't replacing fossil fuels. They are, and the countries doing it show where others are heading.

**"You're cherry-picking Denmark."**

Denmark is ahead of the pack, but the pattern is common across Europe. The UK, Germany, Portugal, Spain, Ireland and Italy have all seen large falls in electricity from fossil fuels. Outside Europe, the US has moved more slowly, but its coal plants are now clearly in retreat. The transition looks different from place to place because countries started at different times with different resources, not because it isn't happening.

## Go deeper

**Why the confusion is easy to fall into.** The global chart is the one most people see. Both lines rise because it lumps every country together: mature grids where fossil fuels are falling, growing grids where renewables are absorbing new demand, and a very small number of countries where fossil fuel use is still growing outright. Splitting the picture into addition, displacement and transition clears it up.

**The countries closest to a full transition.** Denmark, the UK, Finland, Germany, Portugal and Spain have all cut electricity from fossil fuels a great deal since their own peaks. Their approach has been similar: keep existing low-carbon power running (nuclear, and hydro where they have it), build renewables fast, phase out coal firmly, and use gas as a shrinking backup. Where Germany closed nuclear plants early, its transition slowed noticeably, and Japan learned the same lesson.

**What has to happen next.** Two transitions need to run side by side. The first, cleaning up electricity, is well under way. The second, electrifying everything else (cars, heating, industry), is at a much earlier stage. The UK's own target of fully low-carbon electricity by 2030 means building offshore wind and solar about three times faster than over the last 20 years. That is technically possible, but demanding.
