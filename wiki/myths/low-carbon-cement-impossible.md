# Myth: we'll never be able to make low-carbon cement

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

Cement is 7% of global emissions and it's the second-most-used material on Earth after water. It's genuinely hard to decarbonise, but there are three real routes and all three work: use less cement in each mix, capture the CO2 at the plant, or use a different rock entirely. The first plant using basalt instead of limestone is being built. Cement made with capture costs more, but adds tiny amounts to the final price of a house.

## Why it sounds right

Because the chemistry is uniquely awkward. Cement is made by heating limestone (CaCO3) at very high temperature. Doing that releases CO2 directly from the rock itself — before you even count the fuel used to heat the kiln. There's no way to make cement from limestone without releasing that CO2. If you fix your gaze on that single fact, "cement is impossible to decarbonise" sounds obvious.

## What is actually true

**Why cement matters:**
- **7% of global greenhouse gas emissions** — more than shipping and aviation combined
- For every **1 tonne of concrete**, roughly **1 tonne of CO2** goes into the atmosphere

**Three genuine routes to cut it:**

**1. Use less cement in concrete.** Cement is one ingredient in concrete (mixed with water, sand, aggregate). Europe's mix is currently ~78% cement. Many countries build safely with much less. Cutting European mixes to 50% cement by 2050 could reduce industry CO2 by **40%**.

**2. Carbon capture and storage (CCS).** Capture the CO2 as it leaves the kiln, pipe it underground. This works — **Heidelberg Cement** is building the first commercial-scale plant in Norway. It costs more:
- Normal cement: **~$125 per tonne**
- CCS-cement: **$100-200 per tonne extra** (75-140% more per Gates' book)
- In places with cheap energy: extra cost could be as low as **$40 per tonne**
- The US **Inflation Reduction Act** already provides an **$85/tonne tax break** for captured and stored CO2

Even though CCS-cement is expensive per tonne, cement is a small share of what a house costs, so the finished-product impact is **very small**.

**3. Use a different rock entirely.** Instead of limestone (which contains carbon), use **calcium silicate rocks like basalt** (which don't). No CO2 released during heating. Useful magnesium by-products can actually **absorb CO2 from the air**. **Brimstone** has proven the concept and is building a test plant. Basalt is one of the most common rocks on Earth.

## If they push back

**"CCS-cement is way too expensive."**

Per tonne of cement, yes. Per house, the extra cost is tiny — cement is a small fraction of a house's price. And in places with cheap clean energy, the CCS premium can be as low as **$40/tonne**. Government support (like the US IRA's $85/tonne tax credit) closes most of the gap.

**"Basalt cement is a lab experiment."**

Brimstone has proven the concept and is building a test plant. That's the same stage lab-grown meat was at 10 years ago. Ten years ago most people thought CCS was the only real option. The list has grown.

**"You can't rebuild every cement plant."**

You don't have to. Use less cement in every mix (route 1) — that alone cuts 40% at zero technology change, only building regulation change. Then retrofit CCS to plants that keep producing (route 2). New capacity in basalt regions can use route 3.

## Go deeper

**Where the CO2 in cement actually comes from.** About half of cement's emissions come from **calcination** — the chemical reaction that converts limestone (CaCO3) into calcium oxide (CaO) and releases CO2. This half isn't from burning fuel, so switching the kiln to renewable electricity doesn't remove it. The other half comes from the fuel used to heat the kiln — that half can be decarbonised by electrification or hydrogen. The calcination CO2 is the hard bit, and it's what the three routes all target.

**Building rules are a bigger lever than they look.** Many countries mandate high cement content in concrete for perceived strength reasons. Countries that already build with less cement have equally durable structures. Updating building codes to allow lower-cement mixes is a policy change with no technology risk — it can happen this year and cut a big share of emissions immediately.

**Basalt cement is more than a cost play.** The Brimstone-style process produces magnesium compounds as a by-product. Those compounds can be used in concrete themselves, and some of them **absorb CO2 from air**. In principle, a basalt-cement building could be carbon-negative over its lifetime. That is a genuinely different proposition from "cement, but less bad."

**The finished-product effect.** Even at CCS-cement's higher per-tonne cost, the added cost of a finished house is small because cement is a small share of overall costs. Consumer-facing prices barely move. That is why "decarbonising cement is unaffordable" doesn't survive the maths — the affordability question is about who pays the extra per-tonne cost between producers, governments, and construction customers, not whether the sector as a whole can bear it.
