# Myth: we'll never be able to make low-carbon cement

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

Cement is hard to clean up, but it's far from impossible. There are three routes that work: put less cement in each mix, capture the CO2 at the plant, or make it from a different rock, and the extra cost barely shows on the price of a house.

## Why it sounds right

The chemistry really is awkward. Cement is 7% of global emissions, and it's the second-most-used material on Earth after water. It's made by heating limestone (CaCO3) to a very high temperature, and that releases CO2 straight from the rock itself, before you even count the fuel used to heat the kiln. There's no way to make cement from limestone without releasing that CO2. If you focus on that one fact, "cement is impossible to decarbonise" sounds obvious.

## What is actually true

Cement accounts for **7% of global greenhouse gas emissions**, more than shipping and aviation combined. For every 1 tonne of concrete, roughly 1 tonne of CO2 goes into the atmosphere. But there are three routes to cut it.

Route 1 is to use less cement in concrete. Cement is one ingredient in concrete, alongside water, sand and aggregate. Europe's mix is currently about 78% cement, and many countries build safely with much less. Cutting European mixes to 50% cement by 2050 could reduce the industry's CO2 by 40%.

Route 2 is carbon capture and storage (CCS): catch the CO2 as it leaves the kiln and pipe it underground. This works, and Heidelberg Cement is building the first commercial-scale plant in Norway. It does cost more:

- Normal cement: about $125 per tonne
- CCS cement: $100-200 per tonne extra (75-140% more, according to Gates' book)
- In places with cheap energy, the extra cost could be as low as $40 per tonne
- The US Inflation Reduction Act already gives an $85/tonne tax break for CO2 that is captured and stored

CCS cement is expensive per tonne, but cement is a small share of what a house costs, so the effect on the finished building is very small.

Route 3 is to use a different rock. Limestone contains carbon. **Calcium silicate rocks such as basalt** don't, so no CO2 is released when they're heated. The process also gives useful magnesium by-products that can absorb CO2 from the air. Brimstone has proven the concept and is building a test plant, and basalt is one of the most common rocks on Earth.

## If they push back

**"CCS-cement is way too expensive."**

Per tonne of cement, yes. Per house, the extra cost is tiny, because cement is a small fraction of a house's price. Where clean energy is cheap the premium can be as low as $40/tonne, and government support, such as the US IRA's $85/tonne tax credit, closes most of the gap.

**"Basalt cement is a lab experiment."**

Brimstone has proven the concept and is building a test plant. That's the stage lab-grown meat was at 10 years ago. Ten years ago, most people thought CCS was the only real option for cement. The list has grown since then.

**"You can't rebuild every cement plant."**

You don't have to. Using less cement in every mix (route 1) cuts 40% on its own, with no new technology, only a change to building rules. Plants that keep running can be fitted with CCS (route 2), and new plants in basalt regions can use route 3.

## Go deeper

**Where the CO2 in cement comes from.** About half of cement's emissions come from calcination, the chemical reaction that turns limestone (CaCO3) into calcium oxide (CaO) and releases CO2. This half has nothing to do with burning fuel, so running the kiln on renewable electricity doesn't remove it. The other half comes from the fuel that heats the kiln, and that can be cut with electrification or hydrogen. The calcination CO2 is the hard part, and it's what all three routes target.

**Building rules matter more than they look.** Many countries require a high cement content in concrete because they assume it makes it stronger. Countries that already build with less cement have structures that last just as well. Updating building codes to allow lower-cement mixes is a policy change with no technology risk. It could happen this year and cut a large share of emissions straight away.

**Basalt cement is about more than cost.** The Brimstone-style process produces magnesium compounds as a by-product. These can go into the concrete itself, and some of them absorb CO2 from the air. In principle, a building made with basalt cement could remove more carbon than it caused over its lifetime. That is a different proposition from making cement slightly less bad.

**The effect on the finished product.** Even at CCS cement's higher cost per tonne, a finished house costs only a little more, because cement is a small share of the total. Prices for buyers barely move. That's why the claim that decarbonising cement is unaffordable doesn't survive the maths. The real question is how the extra cost per tonne is shared between producers, governments and construction customers, not whether the sector can bear it.
