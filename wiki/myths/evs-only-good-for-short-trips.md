# Myth: electric cars are only good for short trips

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

That was true in 2010, when the average EV did 80 miles on a charge, but most new EVs now do 200+ miles, plenty do 400+, and the best do over 500. The average UK driver covers 20 miles a day and the average American 40, so range is sorted for daily driving, and the only real gap left is reliable charging on the odd long trip.

## Why it sounds right

The myth is about a decade behind the technology. Early EVs really were only good for short trips: the first Nissan Leaf and the BMW ActiveE managed less than 100 miles per charge. That was the best on offer around 2010-2012. People whose picture of EVs dates from then, or who haven't looked closely since, are remembering a very different market accurately.

## What is actually true

Range has grown enormously in a decade:

- **2010 average EV range: ~80 miles** on a single charge
- **2021 average: ~220 miles**, nearly triple
- **US average: ~450 miles** (bigger cars are popular there)
- Plenty of mainstream models now offer 280+ miles
- At the top of the market, the longest-range EV in 2011 (the BMW ActiveE) managed 94 miles. By 2022 the record was **520 miles** (Lucid Air Dream R), and it will keep rising.

What people drive each day:

- **Average UK driver: 20 miles/day** (even less in a two-car household)
- **Average US driver: ~40 miles/day**

Both fit easily within the range of even the cheapest new EVs.

On long trips, driving on a motorway at the speed limit, you'd cover about 280 miles in 4 hours. Drivers are advised to take a **15-minute break every few hours** anyway, which lines up with when the EV needs a top-up.

Battery wear is often exaggerated. Studies across tens of thousands of vehicles find batteries lose about **2% of their capacity per year**, much slower than most people assume.

## If they push back

**"But I do a 500-mile road trip once a year."**

You have two options: buy a longer-range EV (they exist), or make one 20-minute charging stop halfway, which you'd take as a break anyway. If it's truly a one-off, some drivers hire a petrol car for that trip. Over 15 years of owning a car, one long trip a year isn't a good reason to choose a worse car for every other day.

**"What if I can't find a charger?"**

That's a real concern, but it's a problem with the **charging network**, and the car itself is fine. If your petrol car ran dry because there were no petrol stations, you'd blame the fuel network, not the car. The fix is more reliable public chargers on the routes people drive.

**"Batteries degrade over time so range gets worse."**

By about 2% a year, on average. Over 10 years that takes 20% off the starting range, so a 300-mile EV becomes a 240-mile EV. That's still well beyond what almost anyone drives in a day.

## Also heard as

- "You can't take an EV on a road trip"
- "Range anxiety is a real thing"
- "EVs are city cars, not proper cars"
- "You'd never make it to Scotland in one"
- "EVs are only good for the school run"
- "The battery dies too fast"

## Go deeper

Range anxiety is mostly charger anxiety. Surveys of EV drivers keep finding that what people call "range anxiety" is really "charger anxiety": worry about whether a charger will be there, working and free when needed. The car itself is rarely the problem, so making charging reliable deals with most of it.

Not everyone needs 500 miles of range. There's a tendency to design EVs for the rare case (a long road trip) rather than the everyday one (a short commute). That makes cars heavier, pricier and more demanding of minerals than they need to be. The [Smaller EVs Are Better](../sectors/transport/smaller-evs-better.md) argument applies here: **250 miles** of range is enough for almost everyone, and building beyond that has costs.

The record will keep moving. The amount of energy batteries can store for their weight has improved by roughly 5-8% per year for a decade, so ranges will keep growing.
