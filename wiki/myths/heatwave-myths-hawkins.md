# Heatwave myths

## What is it

This page collects the most common sceptical questions about heatwaves, with short answers you can use. It follows a July 2026 Guardian article by Jonathan Watts, who put each myth to **Prof Ed Hawkins at the University of Reading**, the climate scientist who created the well-known "climate stripes" graphic.

The same message runs through every answer. The warming is caused by burning fossil fuels, the physics has been known since the 1850s, and every heatwave today is hotter than it would have been without human emissions.

## Why it matters

Heatwaves are the climate impact most people in Europe experience directly. That makes them the place where denial and distraction land hardest.

## The myths and the rebuttals

### 1. Isn't climate change caused by something other than humans?

**Say this:** No. Global warming is caused by human activities, mainly burning fossil fuels. Every heatwave and every summer is hotter than it would have been without those greenhouse gas emissions. That is the simple message from climate scientists around the world.

### 2. "This is a complex subject with real uncertainty. Isn't there room for doubt?"

**Say this:** The physics is simple and has been known since the 1850s. The warming effect of CO2 was first calculated in the 1890s. In 1938 Guy Callendar, an English steam engineer, became the first to show the world had already warmed by about 0.3°C, and he linked it to burning coal. That was more than 80 years ago. Today the evidence is overwhelming. Scientists have no doubt, even if some people still refuse to believe it.

### 3. "Aren't this year's heatwaves caused by El Niño or natural cycles?"

**Say this:** We've always had extreme weather and we always will. The difference is that a warmer world makes the consequences worse. The current El Niño has only just started and won't peak until Christmas 2026, so it is not the main driver of the summer heatwaves we are seeing now.

### 4. "What about the theory that the Earth is moving closer to the sun?"

**Say this:** Earth's orbit does affect the climate, but over tens of thousands of years. When steam engines were invented in the 18th century, the orbit was actually nudging the Earth onto a slightly cooler path. The rapid warming we are seeing instead comes from burning fossil fuels since the Industrial Revolution, not from changes in the orbit.

### 5. "I remember the 1976 UK heatwave. How is this summer any different?"

**Say this:** 1976 was a hot spot over northern Europe, and nearby regions had a cool summer. The peak of **June 2026 was about 2°C hotter than June 1976** in the UK, and it broke records across a much wider area of Europe. Southern Europe and other regions have been breaking records too. In 1976, two weeks above 32°C killed about 250 extra people a day in the UK. A similar spell in today's hotter world would be even more dangerous.

### 6. "Isn't it impossible to attribute any single extreme event to climate change?"

**Say this:** Climate change doesn't create the weather patterns behind extreme events, those have always happened. But when they do happen, climate change makes them worse: hotter heatwaves, heavier rain. Scientists can measure how much worse with **attribution studies**. There are many methods now, and they all show the same overall picture: heatwaves are getting hotter because of human activities.

### 7. "Aren't the records too short to show clear trends?"

**Say this:** Temperature records go back to the early 1800s, and ice cores go back hundreds of thousands of years. The last time the world was as hot as it is now was about **125,000 years ago**. CO2 levels today are higher than anything in those ice-core records, and the rise comes from burning fossil fuels and cutting down forests.

### 8. "Can't we just enjoy heatwaves, beaches and ice-cream?"

**Say this:** Lots of people do. Many others can't: people with heart disease or diabetes, older people, children at school who can't keep cool, patients in hospital, people working in overheated workplaces. For them a heatwave is miserable or dangerous. At very high temperatures even fit people are at risk. The media should show a more balanced picture.

### 9. "Other countries manage with mid-30s°C. Why should the UK get so bothered?"

**Say this:** **UK homes and offices were built for the cooler climate of the past**, not the present and certainly not the future. They were designed to stay warm in winter, not cool in summer. Unlike Mediterranean countries, the UK has little air conditioning and no shutters on its windows. British society has to adapt or live with the consequences.

### 10. "Aren't heat warnings just scaring people? Government overreach?"

**Say this:** No, they save lives. The 2003 European heatwave killed an estimated **40,000-70,000 people** because the continent wasn't prepared. Warning systems are much better now: drink water, keep the curtains shut during the day, don't go out in the hottest hours. Temperatures have risen since then, but excess deaths have fallen.

### 11. "Isn't there an upside: CO2 is plant food, so better harvests?"

**Say this:** No. We can only grow what the weather lets us grow. In ideal conditions some plants do grow better with more CO2, but in the real world **harvests are suffering** from hot, dry spells and changing rainfall.

### 12. "Humans are puny compared with nature. Isn't it too late?"

**Say this:** It is never too late. Because human actions are causing this, human actions can also fix it. We can keep burning fossil fuels and watch heatwaves get hotter, or we can reach **global net zero**, which stabilises the climate and stops things getting worse. That is the choice we face, for our children and grandchildren.

## What the myths have in common

The physics is older than most people think. The theory dates from the 1850s, the first calculation from the 1890s, and the first observed warming from 1938. When someone describes climate science as "new and uncertain", those dates are an answer in themselves.

Hot weather has always happened, but climate change shifts the odds, and attribution studies now measure by how much. When someone says "but we've had hot summers before", the reply that works is "the stakes are now higher". Answering "no we haven't" would be wrong.

Adapting is a real policy task, not an excuse to do nothing. The point about UK homes built for a cooler past and the point about warning systems after 2003 are both about adaptation. Cutting emissions (net zero) and adapting (warning systems, retrofitting buildings, shutters) go together.
