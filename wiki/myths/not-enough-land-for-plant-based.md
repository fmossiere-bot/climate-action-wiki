# Myth: there isn't enough land for everyone to go plant-based

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

The opposite. Going more plant-based needs less land, not more, because most farmland today grows food for animals, not for people. Three-quarters of the world's farmland supports livestock. A fully vegan world would need about **1 billion hectares** of cropland instead of the current 4 billion — a 75% drop. Even a moderate shift makes a bigger difference than a few people going fully vegan.

## Why it sounds right

Because at first glance, replacing meat with lots of vegetables sounds like you'd need lots of extra veg fields. There's also a common intuition that grazing land is "wasted" if it's not producing meat — so switching away from meat would somehow strand it. The mistake is missing that most of the cropland today is already growing food for animals, not people.

## What is actually true

**Where farmland actually goes:**
- **Grazing land + cropland used for animal feed = 75% of all farmland on Earth**
- Only **half of the world's cereal crops** go directly to human food
- **75% of the world's soybeans are fed to animals**, not humans
- **Tofu, tempeh, soy milk use only 7% of world soy** (~20% including soybean oil)

**How wasteful animals are as calorie converters:**
- Feed a **cow** 100 calories → get **3 calories** back as meat or milk
- Feed a **pig** 100 calories → get 9 back
- Feed a **chicken** 100 calories → get 13 back

Animals give us about **37% of our calories** but eat most of the crops.

**What a plant-shift means for land** (from the modelling Ritchie cites):
- **Current diet**: about **4 billion hectares** of farmland
- **Fully plant-based diet**: about **1 billion hectares**
- That's a **75% cut**

The reason grazing land doesn't fully help even if released: **two-thirds of the world's grazing land can't grow crops anyway** — the soil, terrain or climate isn't suitable. So it isn't waste, but it also can't easily be turned into cropland.

## If they push back

**"But what about the land the animals graze on?"**

Two-thirds of it can't grow crops anyway. That's fine — grazing is a legitimate use of unarable land. The issue is the **arable land** that today grows soy and maize to feed animals, when it could grow food for humans directly. That share is the big saving.

**"Everyone going vegan is unrealistic."**

Correct, and Ritchie is explicit about this. She doesn't argue for 100% vegan. Even a moderate cut in meat and dairy across the whole world would matter more than a small group of people going fully vegan. That's the target, not a plant-only future.

**"You'd need vast new fields of vegetables."**

Some, yes — more fruit and vegetables would be needed. But the drop in animal-feed cropland is much larger, so the net effect is much less total farmland used. This is the [EAT-Lancet](../sectors/agriculture-food/eat-lancet-planetary-diet-2025.md) modelling number: total agricultural land falls by about 3.4 million km² — an area the size of India — under a full dietary shift.

## Go deeper

**The calorie conversion loss is the whole story.** When you feed 100 calories of edible crops to a cow, you get 3 back as meat. That is a 97% loss. You'd have to grow 33 kg of feed for every 1 kg of beef you eat. Multiply that across 8 billion people and you have most of the world's farmland going through a very inefficient converter. Cutting the loop by eating the crops directly is a huge land saving.

**Alternative proteins as land savers.** Precision fermentation (microorganisms making protein, fat and flavour) and lab-grown meat can produce a lot of nutrition with very little land. **Savor Foods** is working on lab-made fats to displace palm oil and soybean oil. These technologies won't scale overnight, but they open a path where global calorie needs are met on a much smaller land footprint than either current diets or a naive vegan-only future.

**Improving crop yields matters too.** Higher yields on existing farmland means more food from less land. Better seeds, irrigation, fertiliser, and pest control — plus climate-resilient varieties — let farmers grow more without expanding. Combined with dietary shifts, this can free up hundreds of millions of hectares for nature.

**The "natural is better" bias.** Many people intuitively feel that lab-made food is worse than farmed food. Ritchie admits she feels this too sometimes. But it's a bias, not a fact. Lab-made proteins can be nutritionally identical to (or better than) their animal equivalents, with a fraction of the land, water and emissions footprint. Overcoming that cultural bias is one of the biggest barriers to a food system that fits on the planet.
