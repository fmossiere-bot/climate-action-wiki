# Myth: there isn't enough land for everyone to go plant-based

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

It's actually the other way round: eating more plants needs less land, because most farmland today grows food for animals, not people. A fully vegan world would need about 1 billion hectares of farmland instead of today's 4 billion, a 75% drop, and even a moderate shift by everyone would save a lot.

## Why it sounds right

It makes intuitive sense. If you swap meat for vegetables, surely you need lots of extra vegetable fields? There's also a feeling that grazing land would be wasted if it stopped producing meat. Both thoughts are reasonable. What they miss is that most cropland is already growing food, just food for animals rather than for us.

## What is actually true

About three-quarters of the world's farmland goes to livestock: grazing land plus the cropland that grows animal feed. That comes to **75% of all farmland on Earth**.

Where the crops go:
- Only half of the world's cereal crops go directly to human food.
- 75% of the world's soybeans are fed to animals, not people.
- Tofu, tempeh and soy milk use only 7% of world soy (about 20% if you include soybean oil).

Animals are poor at turning feed into food:
- Feed a cow 100 calories and you get 3 calories back as meat or milk.
- Feed a pig 100 calories and you get 9 back.
- Feed a chicken 100 calories and you get 13 back.

Animals give us about 37% of our calories but eat most of the crops.

The modelling Ritchie cites puts today's diet at about 4 billion hectares of farmland. A fully plant-based diet would need about **1 billion hectares**. That's a 75% cut.

Grazing land is a separate story. Two-thirds of the world's grazing land can't grow crops anyway, because the soil, terrain or climate isn't suitable. So it isn't wasted, but it also can't easily become cropland.

## If they push back

**"But what about the land the animals graze on?"**

Two-thirds of it can't grow crops anyway, and grazing is a sensible use of that land. The real saving is the arable land that grows soy and maize for animals, which could grow food for people directly.

**"Everyone going vegan is unrealistic."**

Agreed, and Ritchie says so herself. She doesn't argue for 100% vegan. A moderate cut in meat and dairy across the whole world would matter more than a small group of people going fully vegan.

**"You'd need vast new fields of vegetables."**

Some, yes: we'd need more fruit and vegetables. But the drop in animal-feed cropland is much larger, so total farmland falls. Under a full dietary shift, the [EAT-Lancet](../sectors/agriculture-food/eat-lancet-planetary-diet-2025.md) modelling shows total agricultural land falling by about 3.4 million km², an area the size of India.

## Go deeper

### Calorie conversion

Most of the land story comes down to calorie conversion. Feed 100 calories of edible crops to a cow and you get 3 back as meat, a **97% loss**. You'd need to grow 33 kg of feed for every 1 kg of beef. Across 8 billion people, that means most of the world's farmland runs through a very inefficient converter. Eating the crops directly saves a great deal of land.

### Alternative proteins

Precision fermentation (microorganisms making protein, fat and flavour) and lab-grown meat can produce a lot of nutrition on very little land. Savor Foods, for example, is working on lab-made fats to replace palm oil and soybean oil. These technologies won't scale overnight. Still, they open a path where the world's calorie needs fit on a much smaller area than either today's diets or a simple vegan-only future.

### Crop yields

Higher yields on existing farmland mean more food from less land. Better seeds, irrigation, fertiliser and pest control, along with climate-resilient varieties, let farmers grow more without expanding. Combined with changes in diet, this could free up hundreds of millions of hectares for nature.

### The "natural is better" bias

Many people feel that lab-made food must be worse than farmed food. Ritchie admits she sometimes feels this too. But it's a bias, not a fact. Lab-made proteins can match or beat their animal equivalents on nutrition, with a fraction of the land, water and emissions. Getting past that cultural reaction is one of the biggest hurdles to a food system that fits on the planet.
