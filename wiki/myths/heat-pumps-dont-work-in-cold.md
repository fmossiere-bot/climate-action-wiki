# Myth: heat pumps don't work in cold climates

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

They lose a little efficiency in the cold, but they still work: Norway, Sweden and Finland, three of the coldest countries in Europe, have the most heat pumps per household. Even in a UK winter they get about 2.7 units of heat from each unit of electricity, while a gas boiler gets 0.7 to 0.8 from each unit of gas.

## Why it sounds right

It is true that heat pumps lose some efficiency below zero. Every manufacturer publishes performance curves that show it. If you read the spec sheet and stop there, it's natural to conclude "yeah, they must struggle". The mistake is treating a small drop in efficiency as "doesn't work" rather than "still much better than the alternative".

## What is actually true

Heat pump efficiency is measured as the **COP (Coefficient of Performance)**: how many units of heat you get for each unit of electricity. A COP of 3 means 1 kWh of electricity gives you 3 kWh of heat.

- Between 5°C and –10°C, heat pumps average a COP of about 2.7, which is still very efficient
- A field study of 550 heat pumps across Switzerland, Germany, UK, Canada and US found COPs ranging from 3.3 in Canada to 2.5 in China
- How that compares:
  - Gas boiler: 0.7–0.8 (70–80% efficient, because 20–30% of the heat goes up the flue)
  - Electric radiator: ~1.0
  - Heat pump in a UK winter: ~2.7, better than both

People who have them agree. Nesta surveyed 2,500 UK heat pump users:

- **3 out of 4 people** were happier, or just as happy, with their heat pump compared with their old heating
- Satisfaction was similar in old Victorian houses and modern homes, so the "doesn't work in old leaky homes" version of the myth doesn't hold up either

The places that use them most:

- Norway, Sweden, Finland: the most heat pumps per household in Europe
- Alaska: common even where winters drop below –20°C

## If they push back

**"But they use loads more electricity when it's cold."**

Yes, that part is true. As the outside temperature falls, so does the COP. On a cold snap in a UK winter a heat pump might run at closer to COP 2.0 or a little below. It still uses less energy than a gas boiler, though it isn't always cheaper to run (see the [cost myth](heat-pumps-too-expensive.md)).

**"My old Victorian house won't work with one."**

The Nesta survey found similar satisfaction in old and new homes. Good insulation helps any heating system, but a heat pump doesn't need perfect insulation. With radiators of the right size and a competent installer, an old house works fine.

**"But badly installed ones don't work well."**

This is the real bottleneck. A badly installed heat pump performs poorly. Fitting one is a skilled job that combines engineering, plumbing, pipe-fitting and refrigeration. The UK has roughly **3,000 trained heat pump engineers** and needs closer to 30,000 by 2030. Training is what holds things back, not the technology.

## Go deeper

**Why the myth persists.** Many of the complaints trace back to badly installed heat pumps: radiators that are too small, poor setting up, the wrong control settings. In the Nesta survey, the small minority of unhappy users usually had installation problems, not problems with the technology. So "they don't work in the cold" often means "the one my neighbour got installed wasn't set up right". That is a training problem, not a physics problem.

**You don't need to insulate first.** A common view is that you can't get a heat pump until you've done a full retrofit. That isn't the case. Insulation helps every heating system and is worth doing in time, but nobody should have to wait years to fit a heat pump, and the Nesta data backs this up.

**What makes the difference:**

- Insulating homes over time, which helps whatever the heating
- Training enough installers: the UK needs a 5–10× increase in the next five years
- Standard, reliable ways of installing them, since uneven quality is the main real complaint
