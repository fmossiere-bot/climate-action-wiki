# Myth: nuclear power is too expensive

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

Nuclear is expensive in the US and UK, no argument there, but South Korea builds plants for about a fifth of the US price, and China and India are close behind. The high cost comes from stop-start building, shifting rules and one-off designs, so countries that build steadily build cheaply.

## Why it sounds right

The Western numbers are eye-watering. **Vogtle 3 and 4** in the US are expected to cost at least $6,000 per kilowatt, a sixfold jump from 1960s US plants at about $1,000/kW. Hinkley Point C in the UK is one of the most expensive infrastructure projects in British history. Over the same period, solar and wind costs have dropped 80-90%. Put a Western nuclear figure next to a solar farm bid and it's easy to conclude nuclear can't compete.

## What is actually true

Cost per megawatt built since 2000 (Britain Remade / Dumitriu & Hopkinson data, £m per MW):

| Country | £m per MW |
|---|---|
| South Korea | £2.24 |
| India | £2.26 |
| China | £2.37 |
| Japan | £2.67 |
| France | £4.43 |
| Finland | £5.97 |
| UK | £9.42 |
| USA | £10.23 |

The US and UK are **4-5 times more expensive** than South Korea. Wages in the UK and South Korea are similar, so labour costs can't explain a fivefold gap.

The real causes lie in how projects are run. They run late, and delays cost money: labour makes up about 80% of typical cost overruns on delayed nuclear projects. Regulations keep changing, and a design change mid-build ripples through the whole project. And plants aren't built repeatedly. The US has about 50 reactors, nearly all decades old, and the US Department of Energy estimates you'd need to build at least 20 identical plants to find the real steady-state cost. That hasn't happened.

## If they push back

**"Solar and wind are much cheaper. Why bother?"**

They are cheaper, and most new capacity will be renewable. But grids in northern countries need steady low-carbon backup. The first 70-80% of a grid from solar and wind is cheap, while the last 20-30% is expensive, especially through winters with little sun and wind. For that last slice, nuclear plus storage might work out cheaper than solar and wind with huge amounts of long-duration storage.

**"Once built, isn't it also expensive to run?"**

No, running costs are low. Fuel is a small share of nuclear costs, and a plant produces power for 40-60 years once built, whereas fossil fuel plants keep buying fuel for their whole life. The expensive part is construction.

**"The West has proven it can't do this cheaply."**

For now, yes, but that comes from decades of not building. South Korea and China have shown you can build in the £2-3m/MW range. The fixes are consistency, standard designs and stable regulation.

## Go deeper

### Where the cost went

US plants that started construction in the 1960s cost around $1,000/kW. Vogtle today is about $6,000/kW after adjusting for inflation, roughly 6× higher. Almost none of that gap comes from changes in the technology. It's the cost of building rarely, changing designs and revising rules mid-project. The physics of splitting uranium hasn't got any harder.

### Ritchie's recommended fixes

- Standardise the design: pick one reactor design that works and repeat it.
- Keep regulation stable. Stable rather than weaker: safety matters, but changing the rules during construction wrecks budgets without making plants safer.
- Keep the workforce. Skilled nuclear engineers take years to train, and losing them between projects means rebuilding the workforce from scratch each time.

### First-of-a-kind costs

The West treats almost every new nuclear plant as a **first-of-a-kind project**, and first-of-a-kind is expensive in any industry. The route to cheap nuclear is making sure the fifth, tenth and twentieth plant of a given design actually gets built.

### Paying for it

Nuclear needs a lot of money upfront and pays back slowly. Financial markets prefer shorter payback, which works against nuclear. The usual fixes are **Regulated Asset Base (RAB)** models and government-backed loans. The UK has adopted RAB for Sizewell C.
