# Myth: nuclear power is too expensive

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

Nuclear is expensive in the US and UK — no argument. But South Korea builds nuclear plants for about a fifth of the US price, and China and India are close. The cost is not inherent to nuclear; it comes from stop-start construction, changing regulations and bespoke designs. Where countries build consistently, they build cheaply.

## Why it sounds right

Because the Western numbers are eye-watering. **US Vogtle 3 and 4** is expected to cost at least **$6,000 per kilowatt** — a sixfold jump from 1960s US plants at ~$1,000/kW. **Hinkley Point C** in the UK is one of the most expensive infrastructure projects in British history. Meanwhile, solar and wind have dropped 80-90% over the same period. Anyone comparing headline Western nuclear numbers against a solar farm bid concludes that nuclear can't compete.

## What is actually true

Cost per megawatt built since 2000 (Britain Remade / Dumitriu & Hopkinson data, £m per MW):

| Country | £m per MW |
|---|---|
| South Korea | £2.24 |
| India | £2.26 |
| China | £2.37 |
| Japan | £2.67 |
| France | £4.43 |
| Finland | £5.97 |
| UK | £9.42 |
| USA | £10.23 |

**The US and UK are 4-5 times more expensive than South Korea.** Wages in the UK and South Korea are similar, so labour cost differences don't explain a fivefold gap. What does explain it:

1. **Projects run late; delays cost money.** Labour is ~80% of typical cost overruns on delayed nuclear projects.
2. **Regulations keep changing.** Design changes mid-build cascade through the whole project.
3. **Plants aren't built repeatedly.** The US has ~50 reactors, nearly all decades old. The US DOE estimates you'd need to build **at least 20 identical plants** to work out the real steady-state cost. That hasn't happened.

## If they push back

**"Solar and wind are much cheaper. Why bother?"**

They are, and most new capacity will be renewable. But grids in high-latitude countries need firm low-carbon backup. **The first 70-80% of a grid from solar and wind is cheap; the last 20-30% is expensive**, especially through winter with little sun and wind. In that last slice, a mix of nuclear + storage might end up cheaper than solar/wind + massive long-duration storage alone.

**"Once built, isn't it also expensive to run?"**

No — **running costs are low**. Fuel is a small share of nuclear costs; the plant produces power for 40-60 years once built. Fossil fuel plants keep buying fuel for the life of the plant. The high-cost bit is construction, not operations.

**"The West has proven it can't do this cheaply."**

Currently, yes. But that's a consequence of decades of not building. South Korea and China have shown you can build in the £2-3m/MW range. The fixes are consistency, standardisation and a stable regulatory environment.

## Go deeper

**Where the cost went.** In the US, plants that started construction in the 1960s cost around $1,000/kW. Vogtle today is ~$6,000/kW after inflation adjustment. So the cost is ~6× higher. Almost none of that gap is real technology change — it's the cost of building infrequently, changing designs, and revising regulations mid-project. The physics of splitting uranium hasn't got harder.

**The fixes (Ritchie's recommendations):**
- **Standardise the design.** Pick one reactor design that works and repeat it.
- **Stable regulation.** Not weaker — stable. Safety cannot come at the price of unbuilt plants, but rule shifts during construction destroy budgets without adding safety.
- **Keep the workforce.** Skilled nuclear engineers take years to train. Losing them between projects means paying to rebuild the workforce from scratch every cycle.

**One-off exception, one-off cost.** The West is essentially treating every new nuclear plant as a first-of-a-kind project. First-of-a-kind is always expensive in any industry. The way to get to cheap nuclear is to make sure the fifth, tenth, twentieth plant of a given design is being built.

**The finance side.** Nuclear has high upfront capital and long payback. Modern financial markets are structured for shorter payback windows, which biases against nuclear as an investment. **Regulated Asset Base (RAB)** models and government-backed loans are the standard fix. The UK has adopted RAB for Sizewell C.
