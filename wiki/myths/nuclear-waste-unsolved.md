# Myth: nuclear waste is an unsolved problem

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

We already know how to store nuclear waste safely — deep underground, in rock tested for stability. What we haven't done at scale yet is prove it in practice. Finland is about to. Nuclear waste is small in volume (all high-level waste ever produced would fit on a football pitch, 5 metres high), zero people have ever died from it, and much of it can be recycled. The public worry is bigger than the physical problem.

## Why it sounds right

Because "we haven't built a permanent storage site yet" is a real statement. Countries have talked about deep geological storage for decades, and until Finland's Onkalo opens, nobody can point at a working example. That gap between intention and execution feels like a hole in the safety case. It's a fair concern — just a different one from "we don't know what to do."

## What is actually true

Nuclear waste is not one thing. It's split into three categories, by volume and by radioactivity:

| Type | Share of volume | Share of radioactivity |
|---|---|---|
| Low-level (gloves, tools) | 92% | 1% |
| Intermediate-level | 7% | 4% |
| High-level (used fuel rods) | 1% | 95% |

- **Low-level waste** (mostly used gloves, mops, tools) is not very radioactive. It's stored near-surface in concrete vaults for ~100 years and mostly decays away in decades.
- **Intermediate-level waste** needs longer isolation in multi-layered casks.
- **High-level waste** is the small but serious 1% — mostly used fuel rods, holding 95% of the radioactivity.

The good news on high-level waste:

- **Up to 96% of the material can be recycled** back into fresh nuclear fuel. Countries doing this: France, Japan, Russia, China. Not doing it: the US, wasting a large potential energy source.
- Small volume: **~29,000 m³ of high-level waste worldwide by 2016**. Piled on a football pitch, that would be **~5 metres high** — tiny compared to fossil fuel waste.
- **Zero deaths or injuries** have ever been caused by nuclear waste storage.
- Nuclear is **the most regulated and monitored waste industry in the world**.
- **Coal ash contains mercury, arsenic, lead, cadmium and chromium** — and is often more radioactive than well-managed nuclear waste — but gets a fraction of the attention.

## If they push back

**"But no country has actually built a permanent underground site."**

**Finland has.** The **Onkalo repository** is 430 metres deep, in rock tested for stability, designed to keep waste isolated for at least 100,000 years. It's expected to open in the next few years. That is the real-world test. If Onkalo works, others follow with more confidence and a real cost benchmark.

**"But the US Yucca Mountain site failed."**

Yes. Yucca Mountain took decades and was cancelled, mostly for political reasons. That is a failure of policy, not of the underlying physics. Finland succeeded because it managed the local political process well from the start.

**"100,000 years is a long time to guarantee safety."**

It is, and this is where scientists genuinely disagree. Direct radiation exposure becomes safe within ~1,000 years. If waste ever contaminated water or food, some isotopes remain dangerous much longer — estimates range from 10,000 to 1,000,000 years. Deep geological sites are designed conservatively, to buffer the extreme end of that range.

## Go deeper

**Onkalo, Finland — the world's first working deep geological repository.** Excavated into 1.9-billion-year-old crystalline bedrock in Olkiluoto. Waste is sealed in copper canisters, packed in bentonite clay, then buried 430m underground. Designed for a 100,000-year isolation window. Onkalo becoming operational is arguably the biggest silent nuclear-industry milestone of the decade — most people have never heard of it.

**Reprocessing is the underused answer.** France's nuclear industry recycles up to 96% of used fuel back into new fuel. This shrinks the waste that needs long-term storage and reduces uranium mining. The US doesn't reprocess, mostly for historical policy reasons — losing an energy source estimated to hold enough power to run the entire country for a century.

**The volume comparison.** All the high-level nuclear waste produced worldwide since the 1950s would fit on a football pitch, five metres deep. A single coal power plant produces more waste ash in a month than that. The "vast quantities of nuclear waste" framing is not consistent with the actual volumes.

**The one real recommendation.** Finland's Onkalo needs to open, work, and be measured, so that other countries can replicate the model with realistic costs and public confidence. Everything else — reprocessing, standards, monitoring — is already well-established.
