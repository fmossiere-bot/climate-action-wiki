# Myth: nuclear waste is an unsolved problem

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

We know how to store nuclear waste safely, deep underground in stable rock, and Finland is about to prove it in practice. All the high-level waste ever produced would fit on a football pitch piled 5 metres high, much of it can be recycled, and nobody has ever died from it.

## Why it sounds right

"We haven't built a permanent storage site yet" is a true statement. Countries have talked about deep underground storage for decades, and until Finland's Onkalo opens, nobody can point to a working example. That gap between plan and practice feels like a hole in the safety case. It's a fair concern, but it's a different one from "we don't know what to do".

## What is actually true

Nuclear waste comes in three categories, which differ a lot in volume and radioactivity:

| Type | Share of volume | Share of radioactivity |
|---|---|---|
| Low-level (gloves, tools) | 92% | 1% |
| Intermediate-level | 7% | 4% |
| High-level (used fuel rods) | 1% | 95% |

Low-level waste is mostly used gloves, mops and tools, and it isn't very radioactive. It's stored near the surface in concrete vaults for about 100 years, and most of its radioactivity fades within decades. Intermediate-level waste needs longer isolation in multi-layered casks. **High-level waste** is the small but serious 1%, mostly used fuel rods, and it holds 95% of the radioactivity.

On high-level waste, the picture is better than many people assume:

- Up to 96% of the material can be recycled into fresh nuclear fuel. France, Japan, Russia and China do this. The US doesn't, and wastes a large potential energy source.
- The volume is small: about 29,000 m³ of high-level waste worldwide by 2016. Piled on a football pitch, that would be about 5 metres high, tiny next to fossil fuel waste.
- Nuclear waste storage has caused zero deaths or injuries.
- Nuclear is the most regulated and monitored waste industry in the world.
- Coal ash contains mercury, arsenic, lead, cadmium and chromium, and is often more radioactive than well-managed nuclear waste, yet it gets a fraction of the attention.

## If they push back

**"But no country has actually built a permanent underground site."**

Finland has. The **Onkalo repository** is 430 metres deep, in rock tested for stability, and designed to keep waste isolated for at least 100,000 years. It's expected to open in the next few years. If it works, other countries can follow with more confidence and a real cost benchmark.

**"But the US Yucca Mountain site failed."**

Yes. Yucca Mountain took decades and was cancelled, mostly for political reasons. That was a failure of policy rather than physics. Finland succeeded because it handled local politics well from the start.

**"100,000 years is a long time to guarantee safety."**

It is, and scientists disagree here. Direct radiation exposure becomes safe within about 1,000 years. If waste ever got into water or food, some isotopes would stay dangerous much longer, with estimates ranging from 10,000 to 1,000,000 years. Deep underground sites are designed cautiously, to cover the far end of that range.

## Go deeper

### Onkalo, Finland

Onkalo is set to be the world's first working deep geological repository. It is dug into 1.9-billion-year-old bedrock at Olkiluoto. Waste is sealed in copper canisters, packed in bentonite clay and buried 430m underground, with a 100,000-year isolation window. When it starts operating, it may be the most significant nuclear milestone of the decade, and most people have never heard of it.

### Reprocessing

France's nuclear industry recycles up to 96% of used fuel into new fuel. That shrinks the waste needing long-term storage and reduces uranium mining. The US doesn't reprocess, mostly for historical policy reasons, and so passes up an energy source estimated to hold enough power to run the whole country for a century.

### The volume comparison

All the high-level nuclear waste produced worldwide since the 1950s would fit on a football pitch, five metres deep. A single coal power plant produces more ash than that in a month. Talk of "vast quantities of nuclear waste" doesn't match the actual volumes.

### What still needs to happen

Onkalo needs to open, work and be measured, so other countries can copy the model with realistic costs and public confidence. Everything else, from reprocessing to standards and monitoring, is already well established.
