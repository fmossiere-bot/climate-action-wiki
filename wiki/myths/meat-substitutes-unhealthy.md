# Myth: meat substitutes are ultra-processed junk food

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

They tick some ultra-processed boxes — many ingredients, factory-made — but they miss the ones that actually cause harm: high calories, high saturated fat, high salt, low fibre. In the health evidence, the ultra-processed foods clearly linked to disease are **processed meats** and **sugary drinks**. Plant-based meats don't show the same link, and some studies find they're associated with lower disease risk. Grouping veggie burgers with fizzy drinks isn't supported by the science.

## Why it sounds right

Because "ultra-processed" as a category has a bad reputation, and rightly so — high consumption is linked to obesity, diabetes, cancer and heart disease. If plant-based meats meet the technical definition of ultra-processed, it's easy to lump them in. The mistake is treating "ultra-processed" as one homogeneous category when the evidence shows the harm comes from specific sub-groups (processed meats, sugary drinks), not from all processed food equally.

## What is actually true

**Nutrition compared to processed meat:**
- Meat substitutes tend to have **fewer calories, less total fat, less saturated fat**
- Usually **higher in fibre**
- Some match a beef burger's 20-25 g protein per serving; some come in lower at ~15 g
- Protein **quality** is worth noting: animal foods contain the full range of amino acids; plant foods often need combining (a varied plant diet does it easily)
- Substitutes often fall short on **calcium, zinc and vitamin B12** unless fortified — many products are now fortified

**The "ultra-processed" checklist** (typical criteria — 8 markers):

| Criteria | Processed meat | Plant-based meat |
|---|---|---|
| Convenient | ✅ | ✅ |
| High in calories | ✅ | ❌ |
| High in sugar | ❌ | ❌ |
| High in saturated fat | ✅ | ❌ |
| High in salt | ✅ | ❌ |
| Many ingredients | ✅ | ✅ |
| Low in fibre | ✅ | ❌ |
| Couldn't be made in a normal kitchen | ✅ | ✅ |

Plant-based meat ticks **3 of 8** markers. Processed meat ticks **6 of 8**. Not the same category on any measure that actually matters for health.

**What the health evidence actually says:**
- High **overall** ultra-processed food consumption is linked to obesity, diabetes, cancer, heart disease
- When researchers look inside the "UPF" category, the food groups doing the damage are **processed meats and sugary drinks**
- Other UPFs, including meat substitutes, **show no link to higher disease risk** — some studies even find **lower risk**
- More **plant-based protein** in the diet is associated with better health outcomes and lower early-death risk

## If they push back

**"But they're still ultra-processed."**

Yes, technically. But the "ultra-processed" category is heterogeneous. A veggie burger and a can of Coke both meet the definition, but they behave very differently in the body. The specific health harms come from calorie density, saturated fat, salt, low fibre, and specific ingredients like nitrates in processed meat. Meat substitutes don't hit those harm markers.

**"Cows are natural, factories aren't."**

Naturalness isn't a nutritional metric. A beef burger and a plant burger both come out of a factory to a large extent (mince, stabilisers, packaging). The question is what's inside them, not where they were assembled.

**"What about processing damaging the food?"**

Fair concern. Some processing does affect nutrition — very high heat, very long processing, chemical solvents. Modern extrusion and mycoprotein processes retain most nutritional value, though not all. If you're worried, tofu, tempeh and unprocessed legumes are the base-line reference — cheaper and healthier than most substitutes, though less convenient.

## Go deeper

**Why the NOVA classification is contested.** The **NOVA food classification system** (from Brazilian researcher Carlos Monteiro's team) is what most "ultra-processed" claims rest on. It sorts foods into four groups by degree of processing. Public health researchers argue NOVA is useful but blunt — it groups very different foods (a homemade wholegrain bread and a Twinkie can end up in the same category depending on how it's applied). More recent nutritional epidemiology tries to look at specific harm mechanisms rather than treating "ultra-processed" as a single risk category.

**The "convenience" tick is not a health flag.** One of the standard criteria for calling a food ultra-processed is that it's designed to be quick to prepare. This is orthogonal to health. Bagged salad is convenient; it's also healthy. Bacon is convenient; it's not.

**Fortification matters more than most people realise.** Many plant-based products are fortified with B12, calcium, iron and zinc — nutrients often present in animal foods but missing from purely plant sources. Fortification isn't a nutritional cheat, it's how public health has worked for decades (iodised salt, folic acid in bread). A fortified plant meat can meet or exceed the nutritional profile of the meat it replaces.

**The best diet is still the one you actually eat.** Even for someone convinced substitutes are perfectly fine nutritionally, the honest answer is that lentils, beans, tofu and tempeh are healthier and cheaper base ingredients than any meat substitute. Substitutes are a bridge for people transitioning away from meat, not the endpoint. Both are far ahead of processed meat on nutrition and climate.
