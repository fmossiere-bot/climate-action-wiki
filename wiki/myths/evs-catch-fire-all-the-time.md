# Myth: electric cars catch fire all the time

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

EVs actually catch fire much less often than petrol cars — about 19 times less often based on global data. What is true is that when they do catch fire, they are harder to put out. The fear is not proportionate to the risk. The fix is better battery safety and better training for fire services, not going back to petrol.

## Why it sounds right

Because EV fires make dramatic headlines. A lithium-ion battery fire burns hot, is hard to extinguish, and produces striking video. Any single EV fire tends to generate coverage that a petrol car fire never would. Repeat that a few times and the perception hardens: "EVs are catching fire." When a big fire in the London airport car park in 2024 was reported as an EV fire, that stuck — even after it turned out to have started in a **diesel Range Rover**.

## What is actually true

Data from multiple sources says EVs catch fire less often than petrol cars:

- **Global data 2010-2020**: about **0.004% of EVs and plug-in hybrids** caught fire vs **0.08% of petrol cars** — **petrol is 19 times higher**
- **Norway** (which has more EVs per capita than anywhere): fewer fires per 100,000 EVs than petrol, diesel or other cars, every year from 2018 to 2022. About **4 fires per 100,000 EVs** on the road — extremely rare
- **EV FireSafe**, an Australian Department of Defence-backed group, calculates the risk that a given EV catches fire at **0.0012%**

## If they push back

**"But when they burn, they burn hotter and longer."**

Correct, and this is the genuine safety issue. Lithium-ion batteries can undergo **thermal runaway** if they short-circuit in a crash — the reaction is self-sustaining and hard to stop. Fires burn hotter than petrol fires and take much longer to put out. The fire brigade needs different techniques and training.

**"So they're more dangerous when they do happen."**

More difficult to fight, yes. More likely to happen — no, much less likely. The two things are different. A rare fire that is hard to put out can still cause less overall harm than a common fire that is easy to put out.

**"The EV fleet is newer, so the comparison isn't fair."**

Partly true. EVs on the road are younger on average than petrol cars, and older cars catch fire more often through wear and tear. That may explain some of the gap. But even accounting for age, EVs come out with a lower fire rate.

## Also heard as

- "EVs spontaneously combust"
- "Lithium batteries are ticking time bombs"
- "You can't park an EV in an underground garage"
- "One EV fire could take down a whole car park"
- "Fire brigades can't put EV fires out"
- "Electric cars are a fire hazard"

## Go deeper

**Battery chemistry matters.** Not all EV batteries carry the same fire risk. **BYD's Blade battery** (lithium iron phosphate, LFP) is significantly less prone to thermal runaway than older nickel-manganese-cobalt (NMC) chemistries. As LFP becomes more common (it already dominates in China and is spreading in Europe), the average EV fire risk is falling further.

**The real gap is fire-service capability.** Many fire services around the world have not yet had specific EV battery-fire training. Groups like EV FireSafe already offer it. As the EV fleet grows, this training needs to reach every fire service. That is a real investment issue — not a reason to keep buying petrol cars.

**Media accuracy matters.** When any car fire happens, reporters often assume it's an EV before the facts are confirmed. That amplifies the perception gap. A confirmed diesel fire rarely gets corrected on the front page.
