# Myth: electric cars catch fire all the time

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

EVs actually catch fire much less often than petrol cars, about 19 times less often going by global data. What's true is that when they do burn, they're harder to put out, so the answer is better batteries and better training for fire crews, not going back to petrol.

## Why it sounds right

EV fires get dramatic news coverage. A lithium-ion battery fire burns hot, is hard to put out, and makes for vivid video. A single EV fire tends to get coverage that a petrol car fire never would. Repeat that a few times and people start to believe EVs are always catching fire.

When a big fire in a London airport car park in 2024 was reported as an EV fire, that stuck, even after it turned out to have started in a **diesel Range Rover**.

## What is actually true

Data from several sources says EVs catch fire less often than petrol cars:

- **Global data 2010-2020**: about 0.004% of EVs and plug-in hybrids caught fire, against 0.08% of petrol cars. That makes the petrol rate **19 times higher**.
- **Norway** (which has more EVs per person than anywhere else): fewer fires per 100,000 EVs than for petrol, diesel or other cars, every year from 2018 to 2022. That's about 4 fires per 100,000 EVs on the road, which is very rare.
- **EV FireSafe**, an Australian group backed by the Department of Defence, puts the risk that a given EV catches fire at 0.0012%.

## If they push back

**"But when they burn, they burn hotter and longer."**

That's right, and it's the real safety issue. If a lithium-ion battery short-circuits in a crash, it can go into **thermal runaway**: a reaction that feeds itself and is hard to stop. These fires burn hotter than petrol fires and take much longer to put out, so fire crews need different techniques and training.

**"So they're more dangerous when they do happen."**

More difficult to fight, yes. More likely to happen, no: much less likely. Those are two different things. A rare fire that's hard to put out can still cause less harm overall than a common fire that's easy to put out.

**"The EV fleet is newer, so the comparison isn't fair."**

Partly true. EVs on the road are younger on average than petrol cars, and older cars catch fire more often through wear and tear. That may explain some of the gap. But even allowing for age, EVs still have a lower fire rate.

## Also heard as

- "EVs spontaneously combust"
- "Lithium batteries are ticking time bombs"
- "You can't park an EV in an underground garage"
- "One EV fire could take down a whole car park"
- "Fire brigades can't put EV fires out"
- "Electric cars are a fire hazard"

## Go deeper

Battery chemistry matters. Not all EV batteries carry the same fire risk. **BYD's Blade battery** (lithium iron phosphate, LFP) is much less prone to thermal runaway than older nickel-manganese-cobalt (NMC) batteries. As LFP becomes more common (it already dominates in China and is spreading in Europe), the average EV fire risk is falling further.

The bigger gap is in fire services. Many around the world haven't yet had specific training on EV battery fires. Groups like EV FireSafe already offer it. As the number of EVs grows, that training needs to reach every fire service. It's a matter of investment, and no reason to keep buying petrol cars.

Media accuracy matters too. When a car fire happens, reporters often assume it's an EV before the facts are in. That widens the gap between perception and reality. A fire later confirmed as diesel rarely gets corrected on the front page.
