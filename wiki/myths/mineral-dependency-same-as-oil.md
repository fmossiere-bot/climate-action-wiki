# Myth: we'll just swap fossil fuel dependency for mineral dependency

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

Mineral supply is concentrated, but it's a different kind of risk: if someone cuts off your gas, the heating stops today, whereas if they cut off your minerals, the solar panels and turbines you already have keep working. You just can't build new ones for a while, and nobody can switch off the sun.

## Why it sounds right

The concentration figures are stark. China refines 80% of the world's rare earths, and the DRC mines 70% of its cobalt. Lithium is concentrated in Australia and South America. Put that way, it sounds as though we'd simply be depending on China instead of Saudi Arabia. What that misses is that fossil fuel dependency is about keeping the system running, while mineral dependency is about building it.

## What is actually true

Mining is concentrated in a few countries:

- More than 70% of cobalt is mined in the DRC
- More than 60% of rare earths and graphite are produced in China
- More than 70% of platinum comes from South Africa

Refining is even more concentrated. China refines more than 80% of the world's rare earths, dominates the refining of cobalt, lithium and copper, and also leads in making the finished products, batteries and EVs.

Fossil fuel dependency is a bigger problem for far more people. **86% of the world's population** live in countries that import fossil fuels, which leaves them exposed to supply shocks. Only 14% live in countries that export them. When Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, up to 50,000 people across Europe died from cold and energy poverty, and high energy prices pushed food prices up too, through the cost of fertiliser.

Renewables change that picture. If the world ran on renewables, 92% of the world's population would live in countries with enough sun or wind to cover at least 10× their own energy needs. Only 8% would have limited renewable resources.

## The key distinction

Fossil fuels are fuels. Cut the supply, and existing power plants stop working.

Minerals are for building. Cut the supply, and existing solar panels, turbines and EVs keep working. You just can't build new ones for a while.

So mineral dependency threatens the **speed of the energy transition**, while fossil fuel dependency threatens whether the lights stay on right now. These are very different risks. A country cut off from Chinese rare earths for 6 months stays warm, keeps driving and its EVs keep running. A country cut off from Russian gas for 6 months has people freezing to death.

## If they push back

**"So we'd just be dependent on China instead of Saudi Arabia."**

For the next stage of building, partly yes. But once the panels and turbines are installed, nobody can cut off the sun or the wind. Fossil fuels need an unbroken supply chain forever. Minerals need one only while we build.

**"China could weaponise rare earths."**

It tried briefly in the 2010 dispute with Japan. Prices spiked, and the world responded by finding sources outside China, so reserves and refining capacity elsewhere grew fast. Rare earths are harder to control than oil because they're found in many places. They just aren't refined in many places.

**"But we still need to build a lot for decades."**

Yes, which is why spreading supply matters. The US, Australia, Norway, Sweden and Portugal are all developing new mining and refining capacity. Norway recently discovered Europe's largest rare-earth deposit. The **US Inflation Reduction Act** is putting significant money into processing minerals at home. And AI-driven mineral discovery (KoBold Metals, HyperSpectral) is making new deposits easier to find. This is a supply problem that can be solved, not a trap.

## Go deeper

**Why "fuels versus building blocks" matters.** Think of the difference between running out of petrol and running out of steel. Without petrol, your car stops today. Without steel, you can't build new cars, but the ones you have keep going. The same difference applies to fossil fuels and minerals, which is why an economy that runs on oil is vulnerable in a different way from one built with minerals.

**Supply is already spreading out.**

- US: keeps finding new lithium deposits, and the Inflation Reduction Act is putting large sums into processing minerals at home
- Australia: keeps finding new rare-earth deposits
- Norway: recently discovered Europe's largest rare-earth source
- Portugal: developing lithium mining
- Indonesia: nickel processing capacity growing fast
- Ritchie's summary: "more of this, please"

**AI-driven mineral discovery.** Start-ups such as KoBold Metals and HyperSpectral use satellite images, geochemistry and machine learning to find deposits people might have missed. This has already led to significant new finds and is expected to speed up. One caveat: AI itself uses a lot of energy, so its own footprint has to be counted too.

**Refining is the real bottleneck, more than mining.** Even when mining spreads to more countries, refining often still happens in China (about 80% of rare earths, and most cobalt). What's short is refining capacity elsewhere. Building it is politically and environmentally complicated, because it's dirty, energy-hungry work, but it's happening, with new plants under way in Malaysia, Australia, France and the US.
