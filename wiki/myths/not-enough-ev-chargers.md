# Myth: there aren't enough EV chargers

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

It depends where you live, but over a million public chargers are going in worldwide every year and the EU has tripled its network in three years. The real gaps are for people who can't charge at home, and chargers that don't work when you get there.

## Why it sounds right

In a lot of places it's true. If you live in a city flat with no off-street parking, and the nearest public charger is a mile away, often broken, or priced like petrol, "there aren't enough chargers" is a reasonable complaint. Surveys of over 31,000 EV drivers across 15 countries keep finding that charger availability is their top concern, and **41% of would-be buyers say they'd only switch if chargers were as easy to find as petrol stations**.

## What is actually true

- Over a million public charging points are being installed worldwide every year
- The EU's charging network tripled in three years and is ahead of its own targets
- The global average in 2023 was **10 EVs per public charger**, but that hides huge variation:
  - New Zealand: 88 EVs per charger (thin network)
  - South Korea: 3 EVs per charger (dense network)
- The EVs-per-charger figure leaves out how fast each charger is (a fast charger serves more cars a day than a slow one), and where people actually charge (most charge at home)

Where people charge matters more than the public numbers suggest:

- Only about 5% of Americans rely mainly on public chargers
- In the UK, about 20% rely mainly on public chargers
- 70% of UK homes have off-street parking where they could charge, but only 30% of city EV drivers have that option
- About 30% of UK drivers charge at work

Even the oil industry is joining in: Shell and others are installing chargers at their own petrol stations.

## If they push back

**"There are barely any chargers on the motorway."**

It depends on the country. In much of Western Europe, rapid chargers on motorways are now common. In North America and the UK the gaps are smaller than they were three years ago. Where the network is thin, that's a real problem, and the answer is targeted investment.

**"Half the ones I've tried are broken."**

Reliability is the specific problem inside the wider one. The share of chargers that actually work matters more than how many are installed, and some networks are known for downtime. Rules on uptime and on being able to pay the same way everywhere would fix more than building extra chargers.

**"I live in a flat with no driveway, and rapid chargers are expensive."**

This is the clearest unfairness in the EV story. Fixing it needs **street-side and neighbourhood charging** for people without garages, priced closer to the cost of charging at home than to rapid-charger rates. It's a policy problem, not a physics problem.

## Go deeper

**Chicken and egg, in a good way.** Charger networks and EV sales feed each other: more chargers bring more EV buyers, which brings more chargers. Countries that get this loop going see the network grow quickly. Countries that wait for demand before installing stay stuck.

**Where to build next.** Most experts agree on this order of priority:

1. Dense cities, where many people can't charge at home
2. Rural and remote areas with thin transport infrastructure
3. Rapid chargers on the main motorway routes
4. Suburbs, where most people can already charge at home (lower priority)

**Reliability matters more than the raw count.** A charger that works 60% of the time serves fewer drivers than a smaller number that work 95% of the time. Public contracts should measure and enforce uptime, as well as the number installed.

**Public and private money both have a role.** Public money is needed to reach places private operators wouldn't go (rural and low-income areas), and private operators can run networks at scale where it pays. The network needs both.
