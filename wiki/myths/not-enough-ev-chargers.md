# Myth: there aren't enough EV chargers

> [!WARNING] Verdict: Mostly false

## Say this

Depends where you live. Some countries have plenty and are building fast. Others are behind. Globally, over a million public chargers are being installed every year, the EU tripled its network in three years, and even oil companies like Shell are adding chargers to petrol stations. The real problem is targeting the places that don't have home charging, and keeping the chargers working reliably.

## Why it sounds right

Because in a lot of specific places it is true. If you live in a city apartment with no off-street parking and the nearest public charger is a mile away, is often broken, or is charging petrol prices, "there aren't enough chargers" is not paranoia. Surveys of over 31,000 EV drivers across 15 countries keep finding charger availability as their top concern, and **41% of would-be buyers say they'd only switch if chargers were as easy to find as petrol stations**.

## What is actually true

- **Over a million public charging points** are being installed globally every year
- **EU charging network tripled in three years** and is ahead of its own targets
- Global average in 2023: **10 EVs per public charger** — but that number hides huge variation:
  - **New Zealand: 88 EVs per charger** (thin infrastructure)
  - **South Korea: 3 EVs per charger** (dense infrastructure)
- The EV-per-charger metric misses two things: **how fast** each charger delivers (a fast charger serves more cars per day than a slow one), and **where people actually charge** (most charge at home)

Home charging patterns matter more than public numbers suggest:
- **Only ~5% of Americans** rely mainly on public chargers
- **UK: ~20%** rely mainly on public
- **UK: 70% of homes have off-street parking** where they could charge — but **only 30% of city EV drivers** have that option
- **~30% of UK drivers charge at work**

Even the oil industry is joining in: **Shell** and others are installing chargers at their own petrol stations.

## If they push back

**"There are barely any chargers on the motorway."**

Depends on the country. In much of Western Europe, motorway rapid chargers are now common. In North America and the UK the gaps are smaller than they were three years ago. Where the network is thin, that's a real problem — and the fix is targeted infrastructure investment.

**"Half the ones I've tried are broken."**

Reliability is the specific problem inside the wider problem. Number of chargers installed matters less than percentage of chargers actually working. Some networks are notorious for downtime. Regulation on uptime and payment interoperability is the fix, not more construction.

**"I live in a flat with no driveway, and rapid chargers are expensive."**

The clearest inequality in the EV story. Fixing it needs **street-side and neighbourhood charging** for people without garages, at prices closer to home-charging cost, not rapid-charger cost. It is a policy problem, not a physics problem.

## Go deeper

**Chicken and egg — but a positive cycle.** Charger networks and EV adoption reinforce each other. Chargers installed → more EV buyers → more chargers installed. Countries that get this loop going see rapid build-out. Countries that wait for demand to justify installation stay stuck.

**Where to target next.** The priority order most experts agree on is:
1. **Dense cities** without home-charging options
2. **Rural and remote areas** with thin transport infrastructure
3. **Motorway rapid chargers** on the main long-distance routes
4. **Suburbs** where most people can already charge at home (lower priority)

**Reliability matters more than raw count.** A charger that works 60% of the time serves fewer drivers than a smaller number of chargers that work 95% of the time. Public procurement should measure and enforce uptime, not just numbers installed.

**Public + private.** Charger build-out needs public money to reach places private operators wouldn't (rural, low-income) and private operators to run at scale in profitable places. It's not one or the other.
