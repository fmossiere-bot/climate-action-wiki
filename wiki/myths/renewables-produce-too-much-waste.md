# Myth: solar panels and wind turbines create huge amounts of waste

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

For the same amount of electricity, solar creates about 50 times less waste than coal, and wind about 500 times less. Renewables aren't waste-free, but the photos of dumped blades leave out the coal ash we've been piling up quietly for decades.

## Why it sounds right

There are real photos, and they get shared a lot. A heap of old wind blades or dumped solar panels in a field makes a vivid image. What the photo doesn't show is the coal ash lagoons that have sat outside coal plants for a century, or the oil sludge left by decades of drilling. The dumping site you can see is small next to the fossil fuel one you can't.

## What is actually true

Waste per megawatt-hour (MWh) of electricity:

| Source | kg per MWh |
|---|---|
| Coal | 89 |
| Solar PV | 2 |
| Wind | 0.2 |

So coal creates about **50 times more waste than solar** and 500 times more than wind, for each unit of electricity.

Here is the waste over 25 years for one average UK person, if all their electricity came from a single source, set against other kinds of waste they produce:

| Source | Total waste (kg) |
|---|---|
| Municipal household waste | 12,100 |
| Coal | 10,700 |
| Plastic | 1,900 |
| Electronic waste | 600 |
| Solar PV | 200 |
| Wind | 20 |

Over 25 years, that person's coal waste would come to about 10 tonnes. Their solar waste would be 200 kg, and their wind waste 20 kg.

How toxic the waste is matters too:

- Wind turbines: 80-85% of their parts can be recycled. Blades are the difficult part. They are hard to recycle and often end up in landfill, but they are classed as non-hazardous in the US and Europe, so the health risk is low.
- Solar panels: silicon panels contain small amounts of lead, and cadmium telluride panels contain traces of cadmium (about 0.1% of the panel's weight). Both are manageable at the end of a panel's life if panels are collected properly.
- Coal ash: contains **mercury, arsenic, lead, cadmium and chromium**, which are far more dangerous, and a coal plant produces it for as long as it runs.

Switching from coal to renewables cuts both the amount of waste and how toxic it is.

## If they push back

**"But wind blades are ending up in landfill."**

Some are, and the industry is working on it. Siemens and others now make recyclable blades, and several European governments have proposed banning turbine blades from landfill by the end of the decade. The amount is tiny compared with coal ash landfills, but being smaller than something worse is no reason to stop tackling it.

**"Solar panels are full of toxic materials."**

Silicon panels contain small amounts of lead (in the soldering), and cadmium telluride panels contain traces of cadmium. Both can be recovered and reused, and manufacturers already do this where old panels are collected properly. The toxicity issue is real, but it is well understood and manageable.

**"We haven't figured out recycling yet."**

The aluminium and glass in solar panels can already be recycled: they're the same main materials as in drinks cans and bottles. Recycling rates are still low in many countries, but most panels installed today have another 10-20 years of life ahead of them. That gives the industry time to build up recycling, and countries like Germany and Japan are already ahead.

## Go deeper

**Coal was quietly the worst.** For most of the last century, coal ash was dumped in huge open lagoons next to power plants, in amounts far beyond anything modern renewables produce. Coal ash spills, among the worst at Kingston (Tennessee, 2008) and Dan River (North Carolina, 2014), have polluted large river systems. The photos of dumped wind blades on social media show waste from a much cleaner industry, not evidence of a problem on the same scale.

**Wind blades, the specific challenge.** Blades are made of fibreglass and epoxy resins that are hard to separate. Today most old blades go to landfill (where they are classed as non-hazardous), are shredded to fuel cement kilns, or are reused in structures such as bridges, playgrounds and benches. Fully recyclable blades, such as the Siemens Gamesa RecyclableBlade, are now coming into service. The industry knows about this problem and is working through it.

**Solar recycling is further along than most people think.** In established markets, aluminium frames and glass are already collected and recycled. Silicon wafers can be reused in new panels, and rare earths in the modules can be recovered. Raising recycling rates in every country is a question of policy (the EU WEEE Directive was updated in 2024), not an unsolved technical problem.

**Looking ahead.** Even if solar and wind use doubles, the waste they produce will still be tiny next to coal, plastic or household waste. It is a manageable industrial task, not a runaway problem.
