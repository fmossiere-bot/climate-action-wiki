# Myth: we need fossil fuels to build renewable energy

> [!WARNING] Verdict: False

## Say this

Every energy transition has used the old system to build the new one, and that doesn't mean we're stuck with it. A solar panel pays back the energy used to make it in less than a year, then keeps running for 25 to 30 years, and wind and nuclear pay back in a few months. So yes, we use some fossil fuels to build renewables today, but it's a small, short-term energy debt with a very large payoff.

## Why it sounds right

In the narrowest sense, it's true. Cement kilns, mining lorries and container ships still mostly run on fossil fuels, so a solar panel made today does carry a little fossil fuel inside it. When someone points that out and says "so renewables aren't really clean", the fact they start from is correct. It's the conclusion drawn from it that misleads.

## What is actually true

There is a measure made for this question: **EROI (Energy Return on Investment)**. It compares how much energy a system gives back over its life with the energy that went into building it.

- Solar panels take less than one year to pay back the energy used to build them. They then run for 25 to 30 years, producing roughly 25 times the energy it took to make them over their life.
- Wind and nuclear take a few months to pay back, then run for decades.
- An EROI below 1 means a system loses energy overall. Nothing on the modern grid comes anywhere near that.
- An EROI of 5 or less is weak. Once waste is properly accounted for, solar, wind and nuclear all score well above 10, the level most experts treat as "good enough" to run a modern society.

History helps here. **Every past energy transition** used the system of its day to build the next one. Timber-fired steam engines built the first coal mines. Coal-powered steelworks built the first oil rigs. Today, factories running on fossil fuels are building the machines that will replace them. This is what a transition looks like, not proof that it can't happen.

## If they push back

**"But mining and shipping are still fossil-fuel powered."**

That's right, and it's where the remaining work lies. Mining companies are already electrifying drills and heavy machinery, partly because it saves them money. Panel and turbine makers are moving to factories run on renewable power, for the same reason. So each new generation of renewables starts out cleaner than the last.

**"OK but the EROI numbers are still lower than for oil and coal at their peaks."**

That's true for some historical fossil fuel comparisons, but there is an "energy cliff" to bear in mind. Above an EROI of about 10, extra gains barely matter for running a modern society. Solar, wind and nuclear all sit comfortably above that line, and the physics doesn't require us to chase the highest possible number.

**"So we'll always need some fossil fuel input."**

Yes, for a shrinking share and for a shrinking length of time. The fossil fuel left in supply chains falls every year as manufacturing itself runs on electricity. Nothing in physics locks it in.

- "Wind turbines are made with fossil fuels"

- "Going green requires fossil fuels"
- "Renewables can't reproduce themselves"

## Go deeper

**The "energy cliff".** EROI doesn't scale in a straight line. Going from an EROI of 1 to 2 is a huge jump: you move from breaking even to producing net energy. Going from 2 to 3 still helps. By the time you reach an EROI of 10, you already keep 90% of the energy as net gain, and beyond that, extra EROI makes little difference to a society. That is why the debate over whether solar has an EROI of 15 or 25 doesn't really matter. Both are comfortably above what modern society needs.

**Where the myth usually comes from.** It tends to come from two places. One is commentary linked to the fossil fuel industry, which likes to describe renewables as living off fossil fuels. The other is sincere technical scepticism from people who read EROI papers from decades ago, when solar and wind really did score lower. Both are answered by current numbers: modern silicon solar panels and modern wind turbines beat the older estimates by a wide margin.

**One real caveat.** The EROI story works well for solar, wind and nuclear. It works less well for biofuels: some biofuel routes have an EROI close to 1, which means the "clean" credit disappears once the whole life cycle is counted. That is a good reason to be cautious about climate plans that lean heavily on biofuels, but it isn't an argument against the transition as a whole.
