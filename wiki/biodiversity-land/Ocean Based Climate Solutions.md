# Ocean-Based Climate Solutions

## What is it

The ocean covers more than 70% of the planet and already does a huge amount of climate work. It absorbs 30% of human CO₂ emissions and 90% of the excess heat. A growing number of **ocean-based climate solutions** aim to protect and strengthen that role, or to cut emissions by producing energy at sea. Project Drawdown reviewed more than 14 of these approaches in 2026 and ranked them by climate impact, readiness and risk.

The results are more mixed than the excitement around them suggests. A handful are ready to roll out at scale now. Others are worth funding as research. Some are not recommended at all, because they risk doing more harm than good.

## Why it matters

Cutting emissions on land is proving hard to scale fast enough, so ocean solutions have drawn growing attention and money. But as Project Drawdown researcher Christina Richardson points out, some of the biggest climate actions still available today are on land: large solar farms, onshore wind, changes in diet and protecting forests. Ocean solutions are a useful part of the toolkit. They do not replace urgent action elsewhere.

## What is ready now: offshore wind and coastal protection

**Offshore wind** is by far the leading ocean-based climate solution. By replacing fossil fuel energy, it could save an estimated 1.90–3.04 Gt CO₂-equivalent per year (gigatonnes, or billions of tonnes). That puts it among the most effective options available anywhere, on land or at sea. Where turbines go matters: offshore wind must avoid disturbing carbon-storing ecosystems such as seagrass beds and mangroves.

Protecting **coastal wetlands** (salt marshes, seagrasses and mangroves) is the next best ocean opportunity. These ecosystems are disappearing at roughly 1.2–3.5% of their global area every year. Stopping that loss keeps their stored carbon out of the atmosphere and keeps the protection they give coasts against storms. Together, protecting coastal wetlands and seaweed ecosystems could deliver 0.21–0.30 Gt CO₂-eq/year. Restoring these habitats adds a further, smaller benefit.

## What is worth pursuing but limited in scale

Reducing overfishing, protecting the seabed from bottom trawling, making fishing boats more efficient and improving fish farming can all help climate goals. But each delivers under 0.1 Gt CO₂-eq/year. They are best pursued for what they do for wildlife and food security, rather than as major climate solutions.

## What remains uncertain: novel marine carbon removal

Ocean alkalinity enhancement and ocean electrochemistry try to increase how much CO₂ the ocean can absorb by changing the chemistry of seawater. Seaweed farming for food is another emerging approach. These could matter one day, but none has yet shown that it works reliably at large scale, at a reasonable cost and without harming ecosystems.

## What is not recommended

Project Drawdown does not recommend **ocean biomass sinking**, artificial upwelling or ocean fertilisation. Artificial upwelling looks likely not to work. The other two interfere with ocean life in ways that could cause widespread harm that is hard to reverse if used at scale.

## The funding gap

Ocean protection currently receives around **$1.2 billion per year** worldwide. Scientists estimate it needs $16 billion per year to meet the 30x30 target (protecting 30% of the ocean by 2030). Offshore wind investment, despite strong commercial momentum, still falls well short of the $100 billion needed by 2050. Meanwhile, research into novel marine carbon dioxide removal (mCDR) needs roughly $2 billion over five to ten years just to answer basic questions about whether it can work.

Project Drawdown adds a warning: money for mCDR should not be taken away from proven solutions that are ready to use and already badly underfunded.

## Summary findings

- Offshore wind: 1.90–3.04 Gt CO₂-eq/year achievable, the largest single ocean climate opportunity.
- Coastal wetland and seaweed protection: 0.21–0.30 Gt CO₂-eq/year combined.
- All other ocean actions deliver under 0.1 Gt CO₂-eq/year each.
- Novel mCDR (alkalinity enhancement, electrochemistry): uncertain, early-stage, needs a lot of research and development.
- Ocean biomass sinking, artificial upwelling, ocean fertilisation: not recommended.
- Ocean protection funding gap: ~$1.2bn/year spent against ~$16bn/year needed for 30x30.
- Shutting down ocean science observatories (a trend under the Trump administration as of 2026) weakens our ability to evaluate any of these approaches.
