# Solar Farms and Pollinators: Why Panel Shade Isn't the Whole Story

## What is it

A 2025 study in *Biological Conservation* surveyed **20 solar parks across southern France** and found that pollinators, bees, hoverflies and butterflies, were **76% less active directly under solar panels**, and **plant-pollinator interactions dropped 86%**, compared with nearby open, sunny land. Researchers, led by Arnaud Lec'hvien, compared three zones at each site: under the panels, the partially-shaded "inter-row" gaps between panel rows, and fully sunny ground.

The cause is fairly simple: panels cast persistent shade that alters light and moisture, which suppresses the flowering plants pollinators feed on. Fewer flowers means fewer reasons for bees and butterflies to visit.

## Why it matters

Solar power is usually framed as an unambiguous environmental good, and on climate grounds it is. But ground-mounted solar farms are increasingly built on former grassland or farmland that already supports pollinator communities, and construction, shading and fragmentation from a solar park can measurably reduce insect life on that land. That matters beyond the solar park itself: pollinators support the reproduction of most flowering plants and underpin the food chains that surrounding farms and ecosystems depend on. As solar capacity expands globally, how a site is designed and managed, not just whether panels are installed, determines whether that expansion comes at a real ecological cost.

## What the research found

The clearest result was the shade gradient: pollinator visits and interactions were lowest directly under the panels, better in the partially-shaded inter-row strips, and highest in fully sunny open ground, tracking the amount of flowering vegetation each zone could support.

Just as striking was a management finding within the same study: **grazing reduced pollinator numbers more than mowing did**. That means solar operators have real influence over outcomes, independent of the panels themselves, through simple choices like switching from heavy grazing to mowing, or leaving strips of wildflowers untouched in the sunnier gaps between and around panels.

A companion study in *Environmental Research Letters*, "Solar park management and design to boost bumble bee populations," found that solar sites planted with full wildflower meadows hosted **almost twice as many foraging and nesting bumble bees** as sites where wildflowers were confined to narrow strips. In other words, deliberate design can turn a solar park into a pollinator asset rather than a liability.

## The land-management fix, not a panels-vs-nature choice

This isn't an argument against solar power. Several US states, including **Minnesota, Illinois and Maryland**, already run voluntary "pollinator-friendly solar" standards or scorecards, giving developers guidance and sometimes incentives to plant native, low-growing wildflowers and grasses under and around ground-mounted panels instead of standard turf. This sits within the wider practice of **agrivoltaics**: combining solar generation with compatible land uses such as pollinator habitat, livestock grazing or crop growing, an active research area at several US land-grant universities, though adoption still varies widely by state.

A broader review in *Conservation Letters* reaches a similar conclusion: solar development can alter habitat for plants, insects and other wildlife, but careful design and mitigation measures can minimise much of that impact. The review also notes that robust comparative data on solar and biodiversity is still relatively sparse, which is part of why studies like the French one matter.

## Key facts

- 20 solar parks surveyed in southern France; **76% fewer pollinator visits and 86% fewer plant-pollinator interactions** directly under panels vs. open sunny land ([Lec'hvien et al., 2025, *Biological Conservation*](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/international/us/researchers-studied-20-french-solar-farms-beneath-the-panels-pollinators-fell-76-and-plant-pollinator-interactions-plunged-86/articleshow/133319387.cms))
- Inter-row (partially shaded) zones fell between the under-panel and open-sun results
- **Grazing hurt pollinators more than mowing** within the same solar parks
- Solar sites with full wildflower meadows hosted **~2x more bumble bees** than sites with only narrow wildflower strips (*Environmental Research Letters*)
- Minnesota, Illinois and Maryland run voluntary pollinator-friendly solar standards
- *Conservation Letters* review: design and mitigation can minimise most impacts; comparative solar-biodiversity data is still sparse
