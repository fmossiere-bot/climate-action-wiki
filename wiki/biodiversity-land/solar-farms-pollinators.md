# Solar Farms and Pollinators: Why Panel Shade Isn't the Whole Story

## What is it

A 2025 study in *Biological Conservation* surveyed **20 solar parks across southern France**. It found that pollinators (bees, hoverflies and butterflies) were 76% less active directly under solar panels than on nearby open, sunny land, and that plant-pollinator interactions dropped by 86%. The researchers, led by Arnaud Lec'hvien, compared three zones at each site: under the panels, the partly shaded "inter-row" gaps between rows of panels, and fully sunny ground.

The cause is fairly simple. Panels cast constant shade, which changes the light and moisture underneath and holds back the flowering plants pollinators feed on. Fewer flowers means fewer reasons for bees and butterflies to visit.

## Why it matters

Solar power is usually seen as good for the environment without qualification, and on climate grounds it is. But ground-mounted solar farms are more and more often built on former grassland or farmland that already supports pollinators. Building work, shade and the way a solar park breaks up habitat can measurably reduce insect life on that land. The effect reaches beyond the park itself, because pollinators help most flowering plants reproduce and support the food chains that nearby farms and ecosystems rely on. As solar capacity grows worldwide, how a site is designed and managed decides whether that growth comes at a real cost to nature, as much as whether panels go up at all.

## What the research found

The clearest result was a shade gradient. Pollinator visits and interactions were lowest directly under the panels, better in the partly shaded strips between rows, and highest on fully sunny open ground. This followed the amount of flowering plants each zone could support.

The same study also looked at how the land was managed, and found that **grazing reduced pollinator numbers more than mowing did**. So solar operators have real influence over the outcome, separate from the panels themselves. Simple choices help, such as switching from heavy grazing to mowing, or leaving strips of wildflowers alone in the sunnier gaps between and around the panels.

A companion study in *Environmental Research Letters*, "Solar park management and design to boost bumble bee populations," found that solar sites planted with full wildflower meadows had **almost twice as many foraging and nesting bumble bees** as sites where wildflowers were kept to narrow strips. With deliberate design, a solar park can help pollinators rather than harm them.

## Fixing it through land management

None of this is an argument against solar power. Several US states, including **Minnesota, Illinois and Maryland**, already run voluntary "pollinator-friendly solar" standards or scorecards. These give developers guidance, and sometimes incentives, to plant native, low-growing wildflowers and grasses under and around ground-mounted panels instead of ordinary turf. This is part of the wider practice of agrivoltaics: combining solar power with compatible uses of the same land, such as pollinator habitat, grazing livestock or growing crops. It is an active research area at several US land-grant universities, though take-up still varies widely from state to state.

A broader review in *Conservation Letters* comes to a similar conclusion. Solar development can change habitat for plants, insects and other wildlife, but careful design and mitigation can reduce much of that impact. The review also notes that solid comparative data on solar and biodiversity is still fairly thin, which is one reason studies like the French one are useful.

## Key facts

- 20 solar parks surveyed in southern France, with **76% fewer pollinator visits and 86% fewer plant-pollinator interactions** directly under panels than on open sunny land ([Lec'hvien et al., 2025, *Biological Conservation*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2025.111209))
- Inter-row (partly shaded) zones fell between the under-panel and open-sun results
- **Grazing hurt pollinators more than mowing** within the same solar parks
- Solar sites with full wildflower meadows had **~2x more bumble bees** than sites with only narrow wildflower strips (*Environmental Research Letters*)
- Minnesota, Illinois and Maryland run voluntary pollinator-friendly solar standards
- *Conservation Letters* review: design and mitigation can reduce most impacts, but comparative data on solar and biodiversity is still sparse
