# Grassland carbon stocks: the overlooked climate ally

## What is it

When most people think of "nature stores carbon", they picture rainforest. The reality is that **open ecosystems — grasslands, savannas, shrublands and other non-forested landscapes — can store vast amounts of carbon too, often more than nearby forests, mostly in their soils**. These ecosystems also provide water security, biodiversity and food, but are largely invisible to climate-finance mechanisms like REDD+, which were built around trees.

A 2026 policy brief in *PLANTS, PEOPLE, PLANET* by Franciele Peixoto, Amy Zanne, Natashi Pilon and colleagues makes the specific case for Brazil's **Cerrado**, the world's most biodiverse savanna. The numbers are striking.

## Why it matters

If the climate finance system is systematically mis-allocating money toward forests at the expense of open ecosystems that store more carbon, that is a major design flaw. Add the water-security angle — open ecosystems feed the groundwater that keeps forests like the Amazon alive — and the forest-vs-grassland framing turns out to be false: they are two sides of the same hydrological system. Clearing one damages the other.

## The Cerrado as a specific case

- Covers much of central Brazil; the **most biodiverse savanna in the world**
- Home to **>4,000 native plant species, >1,000 animal species** (pumas, tapirs, maned wolves, giant anteaters)
- **Soils store up to 1,200 tonnes of carbon per hectare — six times the above-ground carbon of Amazon rainforest vegetation**
- Some of this soil carbon is **~20,000 years old**. If released, it does not come back
- Feeds the **headwaters of two-thirds of Brazil's major waterways**, including the Amazon River
- **>46% of the Cerrado has been cleared** for crops and pasture (per 2022 study), mostly for soybean cultivation to feed livestock
- Agribusiness logic: "sacrifice the Cerrado to protect the Amazon". **The water hydrology makes that logic wrong** — clearing Cerrado dries out the Amazon

## Why open ecosystems don't fit current carbon finance

**REDD+** and most carbon-credit programmes measure forest carbon above ground: tree trunks, branches, leaves. Open ecosystems have smaller or no trees, but store carbon below ground, in deep soil and root systems. The measurement and certification frameworks have no agreed methods for belowground carbon in these ecosystems, so projects to protect them cannot issue credits or access climate funds.

The result: a specific **mis-allocation of climate finance**. The ecosystems that store the most carbon per hectare — and whose carbon is oldest and hardest to replace — are precisely the ecosystems that get the least climate-finance attention.

## The historical reason

Natashi Pilon (UNICAMP) traces the bias to colonial times: "When the Europeans were mapping the land in Brazil, the forests were 'good' because they had wood, but in the Cerrado, it was difficult to produce food without Indigenous knowledge or modern technology, fires would happen, and the trees were smaller and twisted — so they considered it a wasteland."

That "wasteland" framing is baked into today's scientific data (there is far less of it for open ecosystems) and today's climate policies (which were designed around forests).

## The tree-planting trap

**Tree-planting projects** — well-intentioned, often well-funded — can **actively destroy** open ecosystems. Planting trees on grassland or savanna disrupts root systems, changes soil chemistry, uses more water, and can release the ancient soil carbon the ecosystem was storing.

Pilon: *"It is also essential to protect these areas from tree-planting projects, which may be well-intentioned but threaten the region's water supply, biodiversity and carbon stocks."*

## Beyond the Cerrado

The principle generalises. Other major open ecosystems around the world — the Argentine Pampas, the African savannas, the Kazakh steppe, the Mongolian grasslands, the North American prairies, Australian grasslands — provide similar carbon-storage, water-regulation and biodiversity benefits. All are relatively neglected in climate-finance design.

## What is changing

Following presentations of the Peixoto brief at **COP16 (biodiversity, Cali, 2024)** and **COP30 (climate, Belém, 2025)**, Brazil's Ministry of the Environment established a working group to develop a **national strategy for conservation and restoration of open ecosystems**, including their incorporation into climate policies. This is the first concrete policy response.

What is needed next:
- Carbon-credit methodologies that include **belowground carbon** in deep soils and root systems
- National strategies that explicitly include open ecosystems in climate and biodiversity targets
- Protection from tree-planting programmes
- Better scientific data (open ecosystems are historically under-studied)
