# Grassland carbon stocks: the overlooked climate ally

## What is it

When people think of nature storing carbon, they usually picture rainforest. But **open ecosystems**, such as grasslands, savannas and shrublands, can store a great deal of carbon too, often more than nearby forests. Most of it sits underground, in deep soil and roots. These landscapes also hold water, shelter wildlife and grow food, yet the main climate funding schemes, such as REDD+, were designed around trees and largely leave them out.

A 2026 policy brief in *PLANTS, PEOPLE, PLANET* by Franciele Peixoto, Amy Zanne, Natashi Pilon and colleagues makes the case using Brazil's Cerrado, the most biodiverse savanna in the world.

## Why it matters

If climate money flows to forests while ignoring landscapes that hold even more carbon, a large share of nature's carbon store goes unprotected. Water makes the point sharper. Open ecosystems let rain soak into the ground and refill the groundwater that rivers, and forests like the Amazon, depend on. So forests and grasslands are not rivals. They belong to the same water system, and damaging one harms the other.

## The Cerrado as a specific case

> - Covers much of central Brazil and is the **most biodiverse savanna in the world**
> - Home to more than 4,000 native plant species and more than 1,000 animal species, including pumas, tapirs, maned wolves and giant anteaters
> - Its soils store up to **1,200 tonnes of carbon per hectare**, six times the above-ground carbon of Amazon rainforest vegetation
> - Some of that soil carbon is about 20,000 years old. Once released, it does not come back on any human timescale
> - Feeds the headwaters of two-thirds of Brazil's major rivers, including the Amazon
> - More than 46% of the Cerrado has been cleared for crops and pasture (2022 study), mostly to grow soya for animal feed
> - Agribusiness often treats the Cerrado as the area to "sacrifice" so the Amazon can be spared. The water system makes that a false trade: clearing the Cerrado dries out the Amazon

## Why open ecosystems don't fit current carbon finance

REDD+ and most carbon-credit schemes measure carbon above ground, in trunks, branches and leaves. Open ecosystems have few or small trees and keep their carbon below ground. There are no agreed methods yet for counting that underground carbon, so projects that protect these places can't issue credits or tap climate funds.

The result is that the ecosystems storing the most carbon per hectare, much of it old and irreplaceable, get the least attention from climate finance.

## The historical reason

Natashi Pilon (UNICAMP) traces the bias back to colonial times: "When the Europeans were mapping the land in Brazil, the forests were 'good' because they had wood, but in the Cerrado, it was difficult to produce food without Indigenous knowledge or modern technology, fires would happen, and the trees were smaller and twisted, so they considered it a wasteland."

That view still shapes things today. There is far less scientific data on open ecosystems, and climate policy was built with forests in mind.

## The tree-planting trap

Tree-planting projects are usually well meant and often well funded, but on grassland or savanna they can do real harm. New trees disturb root systems, change the soil, use more water and can release the old carbon the land was holding.

Pilon puts it simply: "It is also essential to protect these areas from tree-planting projects, which may be well-intentioned but threaten the region's water supply, biodiversity and carbon stocks."

## Beyond the Cerrado

The same holds elsewhere. The Argentine Pampas, Africa's savannas, the Kazakh steppe, Mongolia's grasslands, the North American prairies and Australia's grasslands all store carbon, regulate water and support wildlife, and all are largely overlooked when climate finance is designed.

## What is changing

After the brief was presented at COP16 (biodiversity, Cali, 2024) and COP30 (climate, Belém, 2025), Brazil's Ministry of the Environment set up a working group to draft a **national strategy** for conserving and restoring open ecosystems, including bringing them into climate policy. It is the first concrete policy response.

What would help next:
- Carbon-credit methods that count carbon in deep soils and roots
- National climate and biodiversity targets that include open ecosystems
- Protection from tree-planting programmes
- More research, since these ecosystems have been studied far less than forests
