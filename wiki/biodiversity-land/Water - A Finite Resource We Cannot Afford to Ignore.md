Fresh water is one of the most essential resources on the planet, and one of the most taken for granted. Human health, food production, industry and ecosystems all depend on it. It is also finite, increasingly scarce, and under growing pressure from population growth and climate change.

Freshwater is one of the recognised planetary boundaries, and scientists now conclude that we have already crossed it. We are drawing on and disrupting water systems faster than they can recover, and the consequences are becoming easier to see around the world.
 
 > Fresh water is one of the 9 Planetary Boundaries we need to stay within to keep the planet habitable.


---

### How Much Fresh Water Is There?

Only 3% of all water on Earth is fresh. Of that, around 2.5% is locked in ice, soil and the atmosphere. The liquid fresh water in lakes and rivers is a remarkably small amount: gathered into a single ball, it would measure only about 56 kilometres across.

Each year we already withdraw roughly 17% more water than nature can replace. Some countries go much further. China extracts around 22% more than can be naturally replaced, and India around 52% more.

Global water demand is projected to grow by 55% by 2050, driven by population growth, more farming and industrial use.

---

### Where Does Water Actually Go?

Most of us think of water use in terms of showers, taps and washing machines. In reality, household use is a fairly small part of the picture. **Around 87% of all the water people consume globally goes to irrigated agriculture.** That is twenty-two times more than the household use of all 8 billion people in the world combined.

This is where the idea of a water footprint helps. Everything we consume has one: the total volume of fresh water used to produce it, often in a completely different part of the world.

Some numbers that show the scale:

- 1 kg of beef takes around 15,400 litres of water to produce
- 1 kg of chocolate takes around 17,200 litres
- 1 kg of roasted coffee takes around 18,900 litres
- 1 kg of nuts takes around 9,000 litres
- 1 kg of rice takes around 2,250 litres
- 1 kg of vegetables takes on average around 320 litres

Through the food and products they buy, the average consumer uses around 5,000 litres of water a day, anywhere from 1,500 to 10,000 litres depending on diet and where they live. Most of this water is invisible, because it is built into what we eat, wear and use.

Swapping beef for chicken a few times a week can cut a household's yearly water footprint by hundreds of thousands of litres. What we choose at the supermarket has a real effect on water systems, often in countries far from our own.

### Blue Water, Green Water and the Planetary Boundary

Scientists now separate two kinds of water when they assess our impact on the global water cycle:

- **Blue water** is the surface water and groundwater we take directly from rivers, lakes and aquifers.
- **Green water** is the moisture in soil that plants absorb and return to the air through evaporation and transpiration.

For a long time, studies of water stress looked mainly at how much blue water we extract. More recent science also looks at how human activity, especially changes in land use, deforestation and climate change, is altering the green water cycle. That wider view led scientists in 2022 and 2023 to conclude that the planetary boundary for freshwater has been crossed. We are no longer within safe limits for the global water system.

This matters because changes to the green water cycle affect soil moisture, the health of plants, rainfall patterns and, in the end, the stability of major ecosystems such as the Amazon rainforest.

### Climate Change Is Making It Worse

Climate change does more than reduce rainfall in some regions. It disrupts the water cycle itself. For each degree Celsius of warming, the atmosphere holds around 7% more water vapour, which makes rainfall heavier in some places and droughts deeper in others.

The communities hit hardest are usually those that did least to cause climate change. The Tana watershed in Ethiopia was long known for plentiful water. Its communities now face shortages, caused by a mix of local population growth, poor infrastructure and global forces entirely outside their control: rising temperatures, shifting rainfall and the demands of international trade.

---

### Virtual Water and Global Trade

A less visible side of the problem is what researchers call **virtual water**: the water built into food and goods that are traded between countries. Half of the virtual water imported by high-income countries comes from regions already under high water stress. In effect, wealthy countries are importing water scarcity from poorer ones.

If current trends continue, virtual water trade is projected to triple by the end of this century. That raises hard questions about fairness, sovereignty and who is responsible for water as a shared resource.

At the UN Water Conference in 2023, the first gathering of its kind in nearly 50 years, the idea of water as a global common good was a central theme. There is still no consensus on it, but the conversation is moving in that direction.

### Water in Ireland
#ireland 

Ireland has some of the best water quality and cleanest beaches in Europe, and its climate has usually brought enough rain to meet most needs. However, Irish water use per person is well above the European average. Climate change and a growing population are expected to put supply under more pressure in the medium to long term.

> The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) plays a central role in managing and protecting Ireland's water. It monitors the ecological health of rivers, lakes, estuaries and coastal waters under the EU Water Framework Directive, oversees groundwater quality, regulates drinking water standards and inspects wastewater treatment systems.

Groundwater in Ireland starts as rain that seeps through soil and bedrock, then feeds rivers, wetlands and drinking water supplies. It can be polluted by pesticide spreading, chemical spills and leaking septic tanks. The EPA runs a national groundwater monitoring programme to track its quality and flag risks.

For drinking water, the EPA carries out audits and keeps a Remedial Action List of supplies that fall below standard. It works to remove risks from harmful bacteria, disinfection by-products, lead in old pipes and pesticides, and publishes an annual report on the quality of both public and private supplies.

Half of the septic tank systems in Ireland fail inspection. That is a serious risk to groundwater and public health in rural areas that cannot connect to mains drainage.

> **An example of recent problems: Lough Neagh** 
> Lough Neagh went through an unprecedented crisis, as algae spread through the lake, damaging water quality and putting its wildlife at risk.
> 
>  **Lough Neagh is the biggest freshwater lake, by surface area, in Ireland and Britain, supplies 40 per cent of the North's drinking water, and sustains a major eel-fishing industry.**
>  
>  **What has been causing this?** 
> - Noxious blooms covered large parts of the lough last summer, and also affected other waterways and beaches in the region
> - Nitrogen and phosphorus from farm fertiliser running off fields are believed to be a major cause
> - The spread of the invasive zebra mussel is also thought to have played a part: the mussels make the water clearer, so more sunlight gets in and the algae grow faster
> - Climate change is another factor, with the highest water temperature at Lough Neagh recorded last June

### What We Can Do

The problem can feel overwhelming, but there are real, practical steps at every level.

> At home, simple habits add up: shorter showers, fixing leaks, running washing machines and dishwashers only when full, and taking care with garden watering. A shower running at 10 to 15 litres per minute uses a lot of water quickly.

The bigger difference, though, comes from what we eat. Moving towards less water-hungry foods, especially eating less beef, is one of the most effective personal choices there is. Wasting less food matters too, because wasted food carries all the water used to grow it.

At policy level, the priority is to treat water as a global commons rather than a local utility. That means coordinated governance, fair pricing and real accountability, especially for the industries and trade flows that use the most.


> **Some simple actions you can take**
> - Turn off the tap when brushing your teeth
> - Look into your invisible water footprint
> - Check for leaks
> - Take shorter showers (a shower uses 10-15l per minute)
> - Fit low-flow taps
> - Collect rainwater
> - Only run the washing machine when it is full
> - Use a dishwasher rather than washing by hand
> - Make your garden watering more efficient

# Companies working on the problem

## Water tech emerging in France

> According to the UN, 700 million people in 43 countries already face water shortages

Saving water, by using existing supplies more carefully and more efficiently, matters. Technology can help, and a group of French water tech companies is emerging. Like green tech start-ups, they struggle to find funding, but their ideas are worth exploring, from limiting tap use to cutting shower use.

- Some sell mainly to businesses (B2B), others to households (B2C)

## La Vendée is trialling the reuse of used water

> Les Sables d'Olonne is the only city in Europe, along with Barcelona, to start reusing water from its wastewater treatment plant

The city and the area around it take most of their water from rivers and lakes, and need an extra 6-7 million m3. Veolia was selected to build the plant. When water leaves the treatment station, it goes through reverse osmosis, which removes particles, bacteria and micro-pollutants.
- It takes several steps to turn it into drinking water.
