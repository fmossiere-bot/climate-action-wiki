The ocean covers most of our planet and does a huge amount of quiet work to keep the climate stable. It takes in heat and carbon, feeds billions of people, and holds a large share of life on Earth. It is one of our strongest allies against climate change, which is why protecting it has moved up the global agenda.

## Key facts

- The total surface area of the Earth is about **510 million square kilometres**, and more than **70%** of it (around 71%) is covered by water.
- The ocean absorbs about **90% of the extra heat** caused by human-driven global warming.
- It also takes in roughly **a quarter of our CO2 emissions** (around 25% to 30%).
- This makes the ocean one of the best natural allies we have in slowing climate change.

## How the ocean stores carbon

The ocean captures carbon through two processes, one physical and one biological.

1. **The physical process** works because CO2 dissolves in water. CO2 from the air dissolves naturally into the sea, and this happens more easily in cold water. Cold water is denser, so it sinks, carrying the dissolved carbon down with it.
2. **The biological process** is driven by photosynthesis in phytoplankton. These microscopic algae use sunlight to take in CO2 and turn it into organic matter and oxygen. When they die, some of them sink to the bottom of the ocean, taking their carbon with them.

We often hear that forests are the main store of carbon, but the ocean plays an equally large part through its "sea forest" of phytoplankton. As Cécile Guieu, a researcher at the Villefranche Oceanography Laboratory (LOV), points out, one big difference is speed: forests take a very long time to grow back, while algae can rebuild their biomass in a matter of days.

## Why protecting the ocean matters

Marine protected areas (MPAs) help the ocean keep absorbing carbon and give marine life space to recover. Limiting destructive and unsustainable fishing is part of the same effort.

- In December 2022, 196 countries agreed the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, which set the target of protecting **30% of land and sea by 2030** (often called 30x30).
- That target is still a long way off. Only about **8% of the ocean** is currently designated as a marine protected area, and just **2.8%** is considered effectively protected.
- The EU has set its own goal of protecting **30% of its sea area by 2030**.
- **France has the second largest maritime zone in the world**, largely thanks to its overseas islands, which gives it a big role and responsibility.

> A new era for the high seas. The High Seas Treaty, formally the Agreement on Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction (BBNJ), is the first legal framework able to create protected areas in international waters, which cover roughly two-thirds of the ocean. It was adopted in 2023, reached the 60 ratifications needed in September 2025, and entered into force on 17 January 2026.

The **UN Ocean Conference (UNOC)** is the main meeting where countries push for better protection of international waters by 2030. The third conference (UNOC3) was held in Nice, France, in June 2025. It produced the Nice Ocean Action Plan and more than 800 voluntary commitments, and helped build momentum for the High Seas Treaty and the 30x30 target.

## Fishing pressure

### Progress on sustainable fishing

Fishing more sustainably means leaving enough fish in the sea for populations to renew themselves. France is a useful example, as one of the largest fishing nations in Europe.

- According to the research institute Ifremer, **51% of the 327,000 tonnes** of fish landed in mainland France in 2021 came from sustainably fished stocks, up from 48% the year before.
- This is a large improvement on the year 2000, when only around 15% of the catch came from sustainable stocks.
- It is still well short of the EU target of having all stocks fished at sustainable levels.

### Illegal fishing

Illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing (often shortened to IUU) is a major threat. It is hard to measure exactly, but estimates suggest it makes up roughly a fifth of the global catch, with some estimates around 15%.

- **China has the largest fishing fleet in the world.** Its distant-water fleet accounted for about **44% of the world's visible fishing activity** between 2022 and 2024, and it is widely ranked as the top offender for illegal fishing.
- Monitoring is improving. Norway has developed satellite tracking for illegal fishing that it can share to help patrol vulnerable areas.
- For more on how large fleets fish unseen in so-called "dark zones", see [this YouTube video](https://youtu.be/2tuS1LLOcsI?si=5r_3nSgdV7KTMNHI).




## About whales

**Blue whales** live for 90 years, travel hundreds of thousands of miles, and absorb the equivalent of 33 tons of carbon over their lifetime (https://www.oneearth.org/great-whales-sequester-more-carbon-than-previously-thought/).

> Did you know?
> The blue whale very nearly went extinct. Its numbers fell from 350,000 a few hundred years ago to 4,700 in 2001. Nearly 99% of blue whales disappeared. Today there are an estimated 15,000, but the population is recovering slowly.

Whale hunting goes back a long way. In the 1800s whales were an essential source of oil, which was used for lighting. By the 1900s that reason no longer held, yet hunting carried on. It peaked in the 1960s, when an estimated 700,000 whales were killed in that decade alone.

Two moments in the 1970s helped turn public opinion and pave the way for a ban on whaling: recordings of whale song released in the 1970s, and the 1975 Greenpeace protest. Whale numbers have recovered a great deal since then, and there are positive signs. Today the biggest risks are shipping routes, and the floating islands of plastic rubbish (mostly discarded fishing nets) that lie on the same routes whales use. Both kill whales indirectly.

In response, the Dominican Republic became the first country to restrict shipping routes around its island. It recognised the wider benefits that whales, sperm whales in this case, bring to the whole marine ecosystem: more plankton, which captures carbon and feeds other life, and in turn more sustainable fishing for local communities.



## About coral reefs
- Coral reefs cover only a tenth of 1% of the ocean's area
- That is about 350,000 km2
- Yet they support 1/3 of all marine species
- They are very sensitive to changes in temperature
- They can grow even in waters poor in nutrients, thanks to a closed loop with algae called zooxanthellae


Coral bleaching is an ongoing problem:
- A major bleaching event happened in 1998, with 8% of the world's reefs bleached
- Bleaching is happening more and more often, mostly because of climate change and warmer water.

> Is there any hope of saving them?
> **Coral reef: 165,000 km²**
> 	The Wildlife Conservation Society and Macquarie University have mapped more than 165,000 square kilometres of coral reefs that have a strong chance of surviving global warming. That is three times more than earlier estimates, and an area about the size of Tunisia. These are zones that are spared from extreme heat, corals that resist bleaching, and reefs that are able to recover quickly. "This research shows that there is, on a global scale, a set of coral reefs able to survive and recover from the climate crisis," says Emily Darling, co-author of the study.



## Phytoplankton: an essential part of the food chain
Phytoplankton are food for blue whales. They also drive the largest daily migration on Earth, as fish and other creatures rise from the deep each night to feed on them. Phytoplankton grow with sunlight and capture CO2 as they do. They then either get eaten or sink all the way to the deep ocean, where their carbon can stay locked away for thousands of years.


> Did you know?
> Studies show that phytoplankton produce half of all the oxygen in our atmosphere, and carry out about half of all the photosynthesis on Earth. Through them and through CO2 dissolving in seawater, the ocean takes up around a quarter of the CO2 we emit.

- Sperm whales feed in the deep but defecate at the surface. Their poo contains iron and nitrogen, which fertilise the sunlit surface water and feed blooms of phytoplankton.

## Mangroves

> Did you know?
> - Mangroves can thrive in salty conditions, enabling them to provide vital habitats for both marine and land species as well as flood protection for coastal settlements.
> - Mangroves protect coasts from sea surges, and they are also a haven for sea life, sheltering young fish.
> - They capture more carbon than any forest on land: 907 tons per hectare, against 350 tons per hectare for a boreal forest.

Our world needs its mangroves. The more we protect them and help them grow back, the better. They are among the richest places for biodiversity on Earth.

> An inspiring story from Costa Rica
> A notable mangrove restoration effort in Costa Rica has focused on the Térraba-Sierpe National Wetland, which lost about 40% of its mangroves after the banana boom of the 1980s collapsed. An invasive fern took over the damaged areas and stopped the mangroves from growing back naturally. The project involved clearing the fern, planting mangrove saplings and looking after them over time.
> 
> The restoration has brought real benefits for **both nature and local communities**. The restored mangroves provide habitat for marine life, including the piangua (a mud cockle), which is an important local food and source of income. Communities were paid for the restoration work, which gave them another, sustainable source of income and reduced the pressure on overharvested resources like the piangua. The approach worked both for the ecosystem and for the local economy, and could be expanded.
>
> 

Mangrove forests are remarkable coastal ecosystems that thrive where the land meets the sea. They have many unusual features that let them survive in conditions that would kill most other plants. Here are some interesting facts about them.

### Incredible adaptations

- **Saltwater specialists:** Mangroves can live in salty or brackish water. Some species have root systems that filter out as much as 90% of the salt from seawater, while others push excess salt out through glands on their leaves, where it can sometimes be seen as tiny salt crystals.
- **"Breathing" roots:** The soil where mangroves grow is often low in oxygen. To cope, many species have roots that grow above ground. These include stilt roots and pneumatophores (roots that stick up out of the mud like snorkels), which let the trees take in oxygen straight from the air.
- **Sturdy support systems:** The dense, tangled roots of many mangroves anchor the trees in soft mud, so they can withstand the daily rise and fall of the tides and the force of waves and storms. This makes some species look as if they are standing on stilts.
- **Unusual reproduction:** Mangroves reproduce in a special way called vivipary. Their seeds sprout and start to grow while still attached to the parent tree. These seedlings, called propagules, can float long distances for up to a year before finding a good spot to take root and grow into a new tree.

### Vital ecosystem services

- **Coastal protection:** Mangrove forests act as natural barriers against storms, tsunamis and coastal erosion. Their dense roots absorb the energy of waves and reduce their height and force. A wide belt of mangroves is estimated to cut storm wave heights by as much as 80%.
- **Biodiversity hotspots:** Mangrove forests are very diverse ecosystems. Their tangled roots are nurseries for many kinds of marine life, including commercially important fish, crabs and shrimp. The canopy and surrounding areas are home and feeding ground for many birds, reptiles and mammals, including some endangered species.
- **Natural water filters:** Mangrove roots trap sediment and pollution washing off the land, which keeps the water cleaner. This helps nearby ecosystems such as coral reefs and seagrass beds, which can be damaged by too much sediment.
- **Carbon sinks:** Mangroves are very good at storing carbon, often called "blue carbon". By the figures above, they can store more than twice as much carbon per hectare as a boreal forest on land (907 against 350 tons), which makes them a strong ally against climate change. The carbon is stored in their leaves, their roots and the soil beneath them.


> Where can mangroves be found?
> - Mangrove forests grow on tropical and subtropical coastlines in over 100 countries
> - Indonesia has the most mangroves in the world, with almost a fifth of the global total.
> - Globally, as much as 80% of the world's fishing catch may depend directly or indirectly on mangrove habitats.
> - The loss of mangrove forests is a serious concern. Much of it is caused by human development, aquaculture (such as shrimp farming) and agriculture.

## Oceanic islands and seamounts

> Many seamounts are in international waters, and industrial fishing takes place on them with subsidies from only a handful of countries (such as Portugal). The species and habitats these trawlers destroy will take centuries to recover.


> Did you know? The inspiration of Papahānaumokuākea
> Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument, in the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands, is one of the world's largest marine protected areas and a UNESCO World Heritage site. It is valued for its unique natural ecosystems and for its deep cultural importance to Native Hawaiians.

> **Importance for biodiversity**: Papahānaumokuākea is a global hotspot for biodiversity, largely because it is isolated and still in a relatively untouched state.

- **High endemism:** The monument is home to over 7,000 marine species, and about 25% of them are **endemic**, meaning they are found nowhere else on Earth.
- **Apex predators:** Its coral reefs are among the last in the world to be dominated by top predators like sharks and jacks, a sign of a very healthy, intact ecosystem.
- **Critical habitat:** The area provides essential breeding and nesting grounds for many endangered species. This includes over 90% of the world's Hawaiian monk seals, nearly all of the critically endangered Laysan ducks and Nihoa finches, and a vast number of seabirds.
- **Coral reefs:** The monument contains the deepest and most northerly coral reefs in the world, and covers about 70% of the coral reef area under US jurisdiction.

>**How it is protected**: Papahānaumokuākea is protected in several layers.

- **Marine National Monument:** Set up in 2006, it was the first marine protected area of its kind. Its boundaries were greatly expanded in 2016, making it one of the largest conservation areas in the world.
- **Co-management:** It is managed jointly by four "co-trustees": the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), the State of Hawaiʻi and the Office of Hawaiian Affairs.
- **No-take policy:** The core of the monument has a strict **no-take policy**, which bans fishing and the removal of any resources. Access is tightly restricted and needs a permit.
- **National Marine Sanctuary:** The marine part of the monument was also recently made a National Marine Sanctuary, which adds another layer of legal protection and a long-term conservation framework.

> Why it matters: Papahānaumokuākea is significant for reasons that go beyond its size and biodiversity.

- **Cultural heritage:** To Native Hawaiians, the islands are sacred, with deep cosmological and traditional importance. They are believed to be where life began and where ancestral spirits return after death.
- **Scientific reference site:** Because it is close to untouched, scientists use it as a reference point. It lets them study healthy marine ecosystems and better understand the effects of climate change and other human pressures on less protected areas.
- **Global conservation model:** Its shared management and strong protection serve as a model for large-scale marine conservation around the world, and have inspired other protected areas. It shows that very large stretches of ocean can be protected effectively.


#### Seamounts are underwater mountains that rise from the seabed but do not reach the surface. They are often volcanic in origin and cone-shaped, but the term can also refer to any isolated rise of 1,000 metres or more.

**How many seamounts are there?**

> No one knows exactly how many seamounts there are, but estimates suggest **over 100,000** worldwide. Less than 1% have been explored in detail by scientists. Most are in the Pacific Ocean, and some of the largest and best known form the Emperor Seamount chain, an extension of the Hawaiian Islands.

**Why are they important?**: Seamounts matter a great deal for ocean life and are considered biodiversity hotspots for several reasons:

- **Habitat for deep-sea life:** They offer a rare hard surface in the deep ocean where corals, sponges and other invertebrates can attach. These form dense, complex communities that give shelter and food to many other species.
- **Nutrient upwelling:** Seamounts can change ocean currents. As water flows over and around them, it is pushed upwards, a process called upwelling. This brings nutrient-rich water from the deep to the surface, feeding plankton and drawing in a wide range of marine life, from small fish to large predators like sharks and whales.
- **"Oases in the desert":** Because of the upwelling and the variety of habitat, seamounts are often "oases of life" in a deep sea that is otherwise sparsely populated. They serve as feeding grounds, spawning sites and stepping stones on migration routes for many species.
- **High endemism:** Because many seamounts are isolated, a large number of endemic species have evolved there: plants and animals found nowhere else on Earth.

**Can they be protected?**: Yes, seamounts can be and are being protected, but it is difficult.

- **Marine Protected Areas (MPAs):** Some seamounts lie within national marine sanctuaries and MPAs, such as those in the Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument. These areas often have strict rules against fishing, mining and other extraction.
- **International efforts:** Many seamounts are in the high seas, outside any single country's control, which makes protection difficult. However, international agreements and organisations increasingly recognise seamounts as "Vulnerable Marine Ecosystems" (VMEs). This status can lead to limits on damaging activities like deep-sea bottom trawling, which can wreck fragile seamount ecosystems.
- **New treaties:** The new UN High Seas Treaty sets out a framework for creating MPAs in international waters. This could make a big difference for seamount conservation, allowing these habitats to be protected on a much larger scale.

## Southern Ocean


> Did you know? Facts about the Antarctic Ocean
> - The Southern Ocean takes up about 3/4 of all the extra heat absorbed by the ocean, and around 40% of the human-made CO2 the ocean absorbs, helped by its abundance of sea life, including phytoplankton

> Climate change means less salt in the water and a slowing of the current that brings phytoplankton to the surface. It is a kind of positive feedback loop: warming weakens the ocean's ability to take up carbon, which adds to the warming.

### Krill
> Krill are essential to the richness of life in the Southern Ocean. They feed whales and other mammals. This tiny crustacean underpins the entire food web of the sub-Antarctic islands.

For a while, krill were caught in huge quantities by Russian and Norwegian ships. This has slowed down but is still happening. The krill is used for pet food, oils and fish farm feed.

> Did you know? Inigo Everson
> Inigo Everson's research was instrumental in establishing the **Commission for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR)**, the first fisheries convention to manage a whole ecosystem rather than a single species. His work on Antarctic krill and other marine life gave the scientific basis for cautious catch limits, helping to keep the Southern Ocean's food web sustainable.

He first set foot on South Georgia in 1964 and went back around 20 times. By 1971 the effects of intense fishing were already visible: that year, the Soviets had taken 500,000 tonnes of krill and finfish.

Inigo's research in the 60's made the difference. He was the first to spot the effect of overfishing krill and its knock-on effect on the richness of the Southern Ocean.

###### 2 treaties made this possible: the ban on sealing in the mid 60's and the global moratorium on commercial whaling in 1986

## 2030 goal: protect 30% of the ocean

The 30x30 goal is huge and could change a great deal. One of the challenges is that we also want the remaining 70% of the ocean to be fished sustainably, and there is no clear agreement on that yet.


> Climate change is already with us. Warmer water and ocean heatwaves are already melting sea ice, disrupting ocean currents, bleaching coral reefs and causing marine species to migrate. Climate migration seems to be happening far faster in the ocean than on land.


To meet these new challenges, and to stand a real chance of restoring our ocean and stabilising our world, changes in policy will not be enough on their own. We also need to change how we look at things.

Local examples that seem like one-offs can provide the blueprint and the evidence for bigger initiatives. Individual stories can also help change how we see ourselves as a species.




> Did you know? A fact about pH
> Most people underestimate ocean acidification, but it plays a big part and deserves our attention. More than 90% of the heat from global warming is absorbed by the ocean. The ocean acts like a buffer, protecting us from the worst effects, but it suffers as a result. As the ocean heats up, nutrient cycles are disrupted and food chains break. At the same time, carbon emissions are making the ocean more acidic, and acidification has driven mass extinctions several times in the past:
> - 66 million years ago, a drop in pH of 0.25 wiped out 75% of marine species.
> - On our present emissions path, ocean pH could drop by 0.4 by the end of the century.

## 2026 update: UN World Ocean Assessment III

The UN's third World Ocean Assessment, published on World Oceans Day 2026 and drawing on nearly 600 scientists from 86 countries, found the oceans under "severe and accelerating" pressure. Its main findings:

- **Sea level rise has doubled in speed**, from 2mm per year before 2015 to 4.3mm per year by 2023.
- 16% of all the ocean heat absorbed since 1955 was taken up after 2018 alone, so warming is speeding up.
- The ocean has now absorbed 90% of the excess heat and 30% of the CO₂ released by human activity since industrialisation.
- 52.1 million tonnes of plastic enter the ocean every year, adding to 24.4 trillion microplastic particles that affect more than 4,000 marine species.
- Ocean currents, including the AMOC (Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, which includes the Gulf Stream), are changing. Their future effects are described as "poorly understood" but potentially severe. See [AMOC Collapse and Carbon Release](../climate-science/amoc-collapse-carbon.md).
- Only 27% of the ocean floor had been mapped as of 2025. Deep-sea ecosystems remain largely unknown.
- The High Seas Treaty came into force in 2026, giving legal protection to the two-thirds of the ocean outside any country's jurisdiction.
- Ocean governance remains "fragmented" across sectors and regions, and the report calls for much better coordination.

More than a third of the world's population lives within 100km of a coastline. Eleven per cent live on land less than 10 metres above sea level, directly exposed to rising seas.
