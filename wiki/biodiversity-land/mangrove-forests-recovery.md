# Mangrove Forests Recovery

## What is it

**Mangrove forests** are salt-tolerant trees that grow along tropical and subtropical coasts. They are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth. For decades they were cleared quickly to make way for fish farms, farmland and towns. A 2026 study has found a real turning point, though: since 2010, the world has been gaining more mangroves than it loses.

The study used Landsat satellite images, which pick up changes in the tree canopy better than earlier methods. They showed far more regrowth than previous surveys had recorded.

## Why it matters

Mangroves do a lot for their size, both for the climate and for people. They store **up to five times more carbon per hectare than forests on land**, which makes them important carbon sinks. Their tangled roots soak up the energy of waves, protecting millions of people on the coast from storm surges and tsunamis. They are also nurseries for fish and other sea life, and coastal communities rely on the fisheries they support. When mangroves are lost, coastlines become more exposed to climate impacts. Restoring them stores carbon and makes those coasts more resilient at the same time.

## The scale of the loss, and the reversal

Between the 1980s and 2010, over 12,000 sq km of mangroves were cleared, an area the size of Jamaica. Fish farming (especially shrimp farming) and coastal building were the main causes across Asia, Africa and the Americas.

Since 2010, the picture has changed. Total net losses since the 1980s are now down to around 849 sq km. Recovery has come mostly from **natural regeneration**, not planting campaigns: once people stop clearing, mangroves spread on their own wherever conditions allow. Clearing in Indonesia has slowed markedly, partly because the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami changed attitudes. People saw that coasts protected by mangroves suffered far less damage. Myanmar saw a similar shift after Cyclone Nargis in 2008, backed up by a national logging ban in 2016.

The remaining forests are also in better shape. The share of **closed-canopy mangroves**, the richest and most carbon-dense type, has grown by nearly 20% since the 1980s.

## Where the problems remain

The recovery is uneven. West and Central Africa are still hotspots of destruction. The Niger Delta stands out: oil pipelines cut straight lines through the mangroves, and pollution is badly damaging local ecosystems. Tropical cyclones also remain a serious threat and have caused some of the largest losses recorded in a single year.

Some of the new growth comes with a caveat. In Brazil, new mangroves have spread along rivers carrying nutrient-rich sediment. Part of that nutrient load comes from deforestation and mining upstream, so some of the gains for mangroves reflect damage elsewhere.

## Key facts

- Mangroves store up to **5× more carbon per hectare** than forests on land.
- From the 1980s to 2010, **12,000+ sq km** were cleared worldwide, the size of Jamaica.
- Since 2010, the world **gains more than it loses**, and the net historical loss is down to ~849 sq km.
- Closed-canopy (carbon-dense) mangroves have grown by **nearly 20%** since the 1980s.
- Indonesia and Myanmar lead the recovery, while the **Niger Delta** and West and Central Africa remain under threat.
- Pollution from oil pipelines does particularly severe damage in the Niger Delta.
- Natural regeneration, not replanting, is the main driver of recovery. ([BBC, 2026](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cn4pk07npvvo))
