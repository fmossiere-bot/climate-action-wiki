# Five lessons from UNEP's Emissions Gap Report 2026

**Source:** Sustainability Decoder, 29 October 2026. https://sustainabilitydecoded.beehiiv.com/p/five-key-lessons-from-unep-s-emissions-gap-report-2026

## What is it

Each year the UN Environment Programme (UNEP) publishes its **Emissions Gap Report**, which compares where countries' policies and pledges are taking global emissions with where they need to be to meet the Paris Agreement. The 2026 edition is called *The Point of Return*. The Sustainability Decoder newsletter drew five lessons from it, aimed at people working in sustainability who want to put individual announcements into a bigger picture.

The main numbers (2.8°C of warming under current policies, 2.1°C if pledges are kept) are covered in the wiki's summary of [New Scientist's report on the same study](un-overshoot-2.8c-newscientist-2026.md). This newsletter adds detail on emissions trends, on why clean energy has not yet cut fossil fuel use, and on what makes climate policy work.

## Why it matters

The report accepts that passing 1.5°C, averaged over decades, can no longer be avoided. That can sound like the end of the story. The newsletter's point is the opposite: how high temperatures go, and for how long they stay above 1.5°C, still depends on choices made now. Every extra tenth of a degree, and every extra year above the line, raises the risk of severe and irreversible harm, for coastal families, farmers and city dwellers alike.

## Key facts

- Exceeding 1.5°C on a multidecadal basis is now unavoidable. The aim becomes limiting the peak, shortening the overshoot and keeping a credible path back to 1.5°C by 2100.
- Global greenhouse gas emissions reached an estimated **58.4 billion tonnes of CO2 equivalent** in 2025, broadly the same as in 2024.
- That flat total hides rising emissions from burning fossil fuels, offset by lower land-use emissions, partly because wildfires were less severe than in the exceptional previous season. UNEP says it is too early to call a peak.
- On the report's lowest-overshoot path, global emissions must fall by 45% by 2035 compared with 2025. Fully delivering national pledges would cut them by only 11 to 15%.
- Solar and wind have grown faster than anyone expected a decade ago, yet global supply of coal, oil and gas is still rising.
- More than 220 policy evaluations give causal evidence that climate policies do cut emissions, across sectors and countries.

## Clean energy is growing, fossil fuels are not yet shrinking

The newsletter's fourth lesson is the one most often missed in good-news stories. Record solar and wind installations are real, but they only help the climate if they push out coal, oil and gas. So far they have mostly been added on top. Whether extra clean power actually replaces fossil fuels depends on the rest of the system: grids that can carry it, energy efficiency, and switching transport, buildings and industry to electricity. The newsletter argues that installed capacity is the wrong yardstick. Absolute emissions are the clearest measure of progress.

## Policies work when they are well designed

The fifth lesson is quietly encouraging. Climate policies have measurably cut emissions, but the successes are concentrated in certain countries, sectors and periods, and many measures have never been properly evaluated. Piling on more policies does not automatically deliver more cuts. The approaches that work tend to combine regulation, public investment, incentives and carbon pricing in ways that suit local conditions. The report's central challenge, as the newsletter puts it, is to extend what already works across the whole economy, much faster.

## Limits of the figures

This is a secondary summary of the UNEP report, written by a sustainability newsletter. The 58.4 billion tonne estimate carries uncertainty, which is why UNEP will not say whether 2025 emissions rose or fell slightly. The 45% figure belongs to the lowest-overshoot scenario, which itself depends on large-scale carbon removal later in the century.
