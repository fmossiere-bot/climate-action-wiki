# Ça bouge en ville: how French cities are changing transport (Eco360, September 2026)

**Source:** Eco360 magazine, n°17, September-October 2026, pages 36-41 (Marc Lomazzi)

## What it is

A six-page feature in the French magazine *Eco360* about how French cities are rethinking transport. For decades the answer to congestion was more roads and more metro lines. The article argues that the aim has changed: cities now want transport that fits local needs and uses less energy, rather than simply moving more people.

It looks at three approaches spreading across France. The first is **urban cable cars**, such as Câble C1, which opened in the Paris suburbs in December 2025. The second is fully free public transport, as in Dunkerque and Montpellier. The third is on-demand transport booked through an app, now running in more than 200 urban areas, including rural council groups. The piece ends with national figures on transport emissions and the carbon footprint of each way of getting around.

## Why it matters

The article offers a rare reality check on free public transport as a climate measure. France's national audit court (the Cour des comptes) and the French Senate both found that few drivers leave their cars when buses and trams become free. Many more of the new passengers used to walk or cycle, which can even push emissions up. Free transport can still be good social policy, helping families with the cost of living. Its climate case is weaker than its supporters often claim.

On-demand transport, by contrast, has moved well beyond the pilot stage. More than 200 French urban areas use app-based systems like Padam Mobility, and 6 in 10 rural council groups (about 60%) have adopted the idea. The national green fund, the Fonds vert, helps pay for it. For people in the countryside without a regular bus, that is a real change.

The piece also gives clear national numbers for French readers. A petrol car emits 142 g of CO2 per passenger-kilometre against 67 g for an electric car, the TGV emits just 2.9 g, and transport makes up 34% of France's emissions. These figures help ground everyday choices and policy debates alike.

## Key facts

### Urban cable cars

- **Câble C1** (Val-de-Marne, Paris region) opened on 13 December 2025. At 4.5 km it is the longest urban cable car in France. It links Villeneuve-Saint-Georges, Limeil-Brévannes and Valenton to the Créteil-Pointe-du-Lac station on metro line 8, and works with the standard Navigo pass. It was needed because motorways, high-speed rail lines and rail yards cut the area off.
- Grenoble had France's first line in 1934. The city is home to **Poma**, the world's leading cable-car maker, founded by the engineer Jean Pomagalski.
- Brest opened the first true urban cable car in 2016 (under 500 m long). It carried 1 million passengers in 2024.
- Toulouse's Téléo, opened in 2022, has carried more than 5 million passengers.
- Angelo in Ajaccio, Corsica, opened in 2025 and links the coast to the upper town.
- The main criticism: a cable car carries 10 to 50 times fewer passengers a day than a tram or metro.

### Free public transport

- Ticket sales cover only part of running costs: 40% in the Paris region, 33% in other regions, and just 18% in towns of under 100,000 people (Cour des comptes).
- Compiègne was first, in 1975. About 40 towns followed, including Morlaix, Libourne, Niort, Figeac, Gap, Bourges and Obernai.
- Dunkerque, in 2018, was the first city of more than 100,000 people to make transport free. It costs under 3% of the local budget. The mayor, Patrice Vergriete, calls it "good for families' spending power, good for the environment and good for social ties".
- Montpellier has gone free step by step since 2020 under its mayor, Michaël Delafosse. A family with two children saves **€1,258 a year**, and the scheme costs €40 million in 2026.
- The Cour des comptes called Montpellier's results "mixed". There are more passengers and the network is overloaded, but it found no positive effect on health or the environment, and warned the scheme could become a "financial dead end".
- In Montpellier, most new bus and tram users used to walk or cycle. Few are former drivers.
- The French Senate found that carefully targeted reduced fares can be as useful as making everything free, and that free transport alone "cannot start a revolution".

### On-demand transport

- About 1 in 3 French people live in rural or semi-rural areas, which cover 70% of the country. Only 20% of them have a regular bus line.
- Saint-Brieuc launched the first on-demand taxi scheme in 1990 (Taxitub, now Proxitub). More than 300 such networks were set up between 1990 and 2005.
- A new app-based wave, led by the Paris start-up **Padam Mobility**, now runs in more than 200 urban areas. Examples include Jyvais in Meaux, Résago in Nancy, Tadi Lib' in Brittany and TBM Flex' in Bordeaux.
- 6 out of 10 rural council groups have adopted it (Cerema).
- France's Fonds vert (green fund) pays for on-demand transport as well as car-pooling, car-sharing and electric bike leasing.
- The French companies Navya and EasyMile run self-driving shuttles on short, fixed routes.

### Transport emissions and how people travel in France

- Transport produces **34% of France's greenhouse gas emissions**, more than any other sector.
- Private cars make up 54% of transport emissions and lorries 24%.
- Share of journeys: 62.8% by car (including electric cars), 23.7% on foot, 9.1% by train, 2.7% by bike (including e-bikes), 1.7% other.

## Practical advice from the magazine

The magazine matches the way you travel to the distance. For short trips, walk. For 3-15 km, take a bike, an e-bike or an electric car. For 15-200 km, choose an e-bike, an electric car, a bus or a regional train. Beyond 200 km, take the TGV or an electric car, and fly only for trips over 1,000 km when the train is not realistic. As the magazine puts it: "The car does not have to be ruled out, as long as you avoid driving alone."

On cable cars, its view is balanced. They make sense where hills, rivers or motorways make a tram or metro impossible, but they will never carry as many people.

## Limits of the figures

This is a magazine feature, not peer-reviewed research. The carbon figures come from reliable bodies: Ademe's Impact CO2 tool, Cerema, the UIC, the WHO and the EEA. Even so, the exact numbers depend on assumptions such as how old a vehicle is, how many people are in it and how the electricity is made. The findings of the Cour des comptes and the Senate on Montpellier are the strongest policy evidence in the piece, and the part most worth citing. English-language coverage of free public transport rarely carries these French audit figures, which makes the article useful for readers outside France too.
