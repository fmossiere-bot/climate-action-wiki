# Ça bouge en ville — how French cities are changing transport (Eco360, September 2026)

**Source:** Eco360 magazine, n°17, September-October 2026, pages 36-41 (Marc Lomazzi)

## What it is

Six-page feature in the French magazine *Eco360* on how French cities are rethinking urban transport beyond the classic "build more roads and metro lines" model. **The core argument: the goal is no longer to move more passengers, but to offer transport that fits local needs and uses less energy.** The piece covers three new approaches now scaling across France — **urban cable cars** (Câble C1 opened in the Paris suburbs in December 2025), **fully free public transport** (Dunkerque, Montpellier) and **app-driven on-demand transport** (over 200 urban areas, including rural council groups). It closes with national transport-emissions data and the carbon footprint of each way of getting around.

## Key facts

### Urban cable cars

- **Câble C1 (Val-de-Marne, Paris region)** opened 13 December 2025 — 4.5 km, longest urban cable car in France. Links Villeneuve-Saint-Georges, Limeil-Brévannes and Valenton to Créteil-Pointe-du-Lac metro station (line 8). Uses standard Navigo pass. Needed because the area is cut off by motorways, high-speed rail lines and rail yards
- **Grenoble (1934)** — first French line, home of **Poma**, world leader in cable cars (founded by engineer Jean Pomagalski)
- **Brest (2016)** — first real urban cable car (<500 m); **1 million passengers in 2024**
- **Toulouse Téléo (2022)** — >5 million passengers since opening
- **Angelo in Ajaccio (Corsica, 2025)** — coast to high city
- Carries **10 to 50× fewer passengers per day** than a tram or metro — the main criticism

### Free public transport

- **Ticket cover of running costs**: Paris region 40%, other regions 33%, towns under 100,000 only 18% (Cour des comptes)
- **Compiègne (1975)** was first; ~40 towns followed (Morlaix, Libourne, Niort, Figeac, Gap, Bourges, Obernai)
- **Dunkerque (2018)** — first city of >100,000 to go free; costs <3% of local budget. Mayor Patrice Vergriete: *"good for families' spending power, good for the environment and good for social ties"*
- **Montpellier (from 2020)** — step-by-step under Michaël Delafosse; family of two children saves **€1,258/year**; scheme costs **€40 million in 2026**
- **Cour des comptes assessment of Montpellier:** "mixed" results — more passengers and overloaded network, but **no positive effect on health or environment**; warns scheme could create a "financial dead end"
- **French Senate**: targeted reduced fares can be as useful as full-free; free transport alone "cannot start a revolution"
- **In Montpellier**, new bus/tram users are mostly former walkers and cyclists, not former drivers

### On-demand transport

- **1 in 3 French people live in rural / peri-urban areas** (70% of the country), but only **20% have regular bus lines**
- **Saint-Brieuc (1990)**: first on-demand taxi scheme (Taxitub, now Proxitub); 300+ networks set up 1990-2005
- **Modern app-based wave** (Padam Mobility — Paris start-up) now in **200+ urban areas** (Jyvais Meaux, Résago Nancy, Tadi Lib' Brittany, TBM Flex' Bordeaux)
- **6 of 10 rural council groups** have adopted it (Cerema)
- Backed by France's **Fonds vert** (green fund), covering on-demand transport plus car-pooling, car-sharing, electric bike leasing
- **Self-driving shuttles**: French companies **Navya** and **EasyMile** deploy driverless shuttles for short fixed routes

### Transport emissions and modal share in France

- **Transport = 34% of French GHG emissions** (biggest single source)
- **Private cars = 54% of transport emissions**; **lorries = 24%**
- **Modal share:** 62.8% car (incl. EVs), 23.7% walking, 9.1% train, 2.7% bike (incl. e-bike), 1.7% other

## Notable framings

- The magazine's distance-matched advice: **short = walk; 3-15 km = bike / e-bike / EV; 15-200 km = e-bike, EV, bus, regional train; >200 km = TGV or EV; >1,000 km = plane when train is not realistic**
- *"The car does not have to be ruled out, as long as you avoid driving alone."*
- On free public transport in Montpellier (Cour des comptes): worth doing but **not primarily an environmental lever** — the drivers who switched are few, the walkers who switched are many
- On cable cars: a legitimate answer where terrain or infrastructure makes classic transit impossible, but limited in scale compared with tram or metro

## Why it matters

Three things:

1. **A rare empirical check on "free public transport" as a climate measure.** The Cour des comptes and Senate findings are blunt: the modal shift from cars is small; the modal shift from walking and cycling is larger (and can be counter-productive on emissions). Free transit is defensible as social policy; the climate case is weaker than its advocates claim. That is worth documenting.
2. **On-demand transport scaling beyond the pilot phase.** 200+ French urban areas using Padam Mobility-style software, with 60% of rural council groups adopting, is a genuine deployment story not just a prototype. The Fonds vert as a funding channel matters here.
3. **Clean national-level transport data for France.** The 142 vs 67 g/pkm (petrol vs EV), the 2.9 g/pkm for TGV, the 34% transport share of national emissions — these are the kinds of numbers that anchor conversations about individual action and policy for a French audience.

## Caveats

- **Magazine piece, not peer-reviewed.** The CO2/passenger-km figures are sourced from Ademe's Impact CO2 tool, Cerema, UIC, WHO and the EEA — all legitimate — but the specific numbers can vary by assumption (vehicle age, occupancy, grid mix).
- **The Cour des comptes/Senate findings on Montpellier are the strongest single policy claim** and should be the one carried forward if any single piece of this is cited.
- **No English equivalent** of this material is readily available — most English coverage of free public transport doesn't carry the French audit-level numbers.

## Tone and angle

French-magazine civic-journalism register — factually dense, policy-aware, neither cheerleading nor cynical. Cites both the Cour des comptes (critical) and local mayors (favourable). The CO2/pkm table at the end is the kind of calibration tool that French climate conversations often use and that is less common in English-language transport coverage.
