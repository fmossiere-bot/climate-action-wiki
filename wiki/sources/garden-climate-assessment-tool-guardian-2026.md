# A climate tool for gardeners

**Source:** Guardian, Miatta Mbriwa, 3 November 2026. https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/nov/03/garden-climate-assessment-tool-drought-gardening

## What is it

A group of leading UK gardening and heritage organisations is building a free online tool that predicts how well a plant will cope with the future climate of a chosen place. The **Garden Climate Assessment Tool** (GCAT) is being developed by the Future National Gardens Group, which includes the Royal Horticultural Society, the National Trust, English Heritage and the Royal Botanic Gardens.

Gardeners type in a list of plants, and the tool weighs factors such as average yearly temperature, the plant's natural range, winter cold and how dry the climate is. It builds on a tree-only tool that Botanic Gardens Conservation International launched in Australia in 2022, extended here to all plants. It currently holds up to 80,650 plant types and data from 289 UK gardens.

## Why it matters

Gardeners used to be able to rely on what worked for their parents. As Sam Brockington of Cambridge University Botanic Garden put it, "we're moving into climate scenarios that we just don't have the traditions to understand fully." In 2026 drought was still declared in most of England in late October, and summer heatwaves scorched gardens and parks.

The tool is meant to help people plan ahead instead of reacting after plants die. That can mean moving away from thirsty classics of the English garden, such as lush lawns, hydrangeas and delphiniums. One garden designer said she is now wary of planting silver birch unless the ground is moist, after seeing the trees suffer over two very hot summers. Letting go of a familiar look can be hard, and campaigners say gardeners need support to make the change.

## Key facts

- Professional gardeners began trialling the tool in November 2026. There is no release date yet, but the RHS hopes to open it to the public in the long term.
- It will also flag plants already struggling in UK gardens, so collections can phase them out gradually.
- The east of England is the driest region in the UK.
- Soil type is not yet part of the tool. A local gardening society pointed out that chalk and clay create very different conditions, and guessed that 70% of plants suggested for her area would not grow on her chalk.
- Designers say advice is becoming more local: what works now depends on your own garden and location.
