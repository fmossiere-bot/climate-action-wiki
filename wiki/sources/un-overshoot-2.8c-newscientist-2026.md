# UN says 1.5°C is over, world on track for 2.8°C: Emissions Gap Report 2026

**Source:** New Scientist, Madeleine Cuff, 21 October 2026 (updated 20 October 2026), on the UN Environment Programme's annual Emissions Gap Report 2026

## What it is

New Scientist reports on the UN's 2026 Emissions Gap Report, released ahead of COP31 in Antalya. The report finds that it is now impossible to limit warming to 1.5°C without **overshoot**. That means the world will go past 1.5°C for a while, then have to pull temperatures back down using machines and nature to remove carbon from the air, through direct air capture, tree planting and similar methods.

The best realistic path, which the report calls the "lowest possible overshoot", would see warming peak at 1.7-1.8°C, with a 50% chance of falling back to 1.5°C by 2100. Current plans fall well short even of that. Policies in place today point to 2.8°C by 2100. If every country delivered its pledges, that would fall to 2.1°C, which is worse than last year's projection of 1.9°C. The main reason is the US leaving the UN climate process.

## Why it matters

The UN now says officially that 1.5°C will be passed. The 2024 and 2025 reports built towards this, and the 2026 report says it directly. That changes the conversation at COP31 in Antalya. Countries will no longer argue about whether 1.5°C is still reachable, but about how big and how long the overshoot will be.

The projection if all pledges are met worsened from 1.9°C to **2.1°C** in a single year, mainly because of the US withdrawal. It is one of the clearest measures yet of what that exit has cost. Patrick Greenfield's reporting in the Guardian points the same way for nature, with 12.1% of global biodiversity funding gone.

The report also relies openly on carbon removal technology. Reaching even the lowest-overshoot path depends on methods that scientists such as Bob Watson doubt can work at the scale needed, for both political and technical reasons. That puts a question mark over every national climate plan that leans on them.

## Key facts

- Under current climate policies, warming reaches **2.8°C by 2100**.
- If every national pledge is delivered, warming reaches 2.1°C, worse than last year's 1.9°C projection. The report says the change is "driven mainly by the US's exit from the UN climate process".
- The "lowest possible" overshoot path peaks at 1.7-1.8°C, with a 50% chance of returning to 1.5°C by 2100.
- That path needs emissions to fall by 18% by 2030, 45% by 2035 and 69% by 2040, compared with 2025 levels.
- Current pledges would deliver cuts of only 5-8% by 2030 and 11-15% by 2035, about 3-4 times too slow.
- Last year's report (2025) already said passing 1.5°C was unavoidable. This year's overshoot pathways are explicitly designed to keep 1.5°C on life support.
- The overshoot scenarios need "vast amounts of global cooling" from direct air capture and other technologies. Many scientists doubt these can be delivered at scale.

## What it means for the "it's too late" argument

The wiki's [it's too late myth page](../myths/its-too-late.md) gave a realistic warming range of 1.8-3°C: about 2.5-3°C under current policies, about 2.4°C if 2030 targets were met, and about 1.8°C if net-zero pledges were delivered. This report moves those numbers up. Current policies now point to 2.8°C, and delivered pledges to 2.1°C.

The main point of that page still holds. A rise of 2.8°C is not 5-6°C, and every tenth of a degree avoided still spares people harm. The question has moved from "can we hit 1.5°C" to "how small and short can we keep the overshoot", but it is still a question about how much damage we can prevent, not whether to try.

## Voices in the piece

Bob Watson, emeritus professor at the University of East Anglia and a former chair of the IPCC, is openly doubtful about relying on carbon removal: "We don't know whether we can overshoot. We don't know if any of those technologies are real, and so when people assume that we can meet 1.5°C or 2°C, and the assumption is based on overshooting and coming back, I think they're in cuckoo land."

António Guterres, the UN Secretary-General, insists the goal is not abandoned: "Overshoot is not surrender. We can still keep it as small and as short as possible, and bring temperatures back below 1.5 degrees by the end of the century."

Joeri Rogelj of Imperial College London says overshoot should be seen "as a failure that we try to rectify". He argues it is still worth keeping the 1.5°C goal rather than switching to a weaker 2°C or 2.5°C target: "The target is still to limit harm to vulnerable and poor populations... In the public debate, this failure to stay within this most ambitious limit should re-emphasise the need to stay as close to it as possible."

## Limits of the figures

The lowest-overshoot scenario depends on removing carbon from the air on a huge scale. Many of the technologies it needs, such as direct air capture at the scale of billions of tonnes (Gt), do not exist yet. Watson's "cuckoo land" remark sums up that doubt. The 2.8°C figure is also a central estimate under current policies, and the real outcome could be higher or lower.
