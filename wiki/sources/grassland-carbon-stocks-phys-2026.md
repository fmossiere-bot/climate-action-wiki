# Massive carbon stocks in grasslands ignored by climate finance (Peixoto et al., PLANTS, PEOPLE, PLANET)

**Source:** Phys.org / Cary Institute of Ecosystem Studies, 23 October 2026. https://phys.org/news/2026-10-massive-carbon-stocks-grasslands-climate.html

**Journal reference:** Peixoto F.P. et al., "Policy Brief: Brazil's open ecosystems: Carbon stocks overlooked by climate finance", *PLANTS, PEOPLE, PLANET* (2026), DOI 10.1002/ppp3.70292

## What it is

A policy brief in the journal *PLANTS, PEOPLE, PLANET* about **open ecosystems**: grasslands, shrublands and savannas. The authors argue that these places hold huge amounts of carbon, yet climate finance ignores them because its standards and methods, such as REDD+, were built for forests.

The lead author is Franciele Peixoto, an environmental analyst for the state of Goiás in Brazil. Her co-authors are Amy Zanne of the Cary Institute and Natashi Pilon of UNICAMP in Brazil. They focus on Brazil's Cerrado, the most biodiverse savanna in the world. Its soils can store up to 1,200 tonnes of carbon per hectare, six times more than the vegetation of the Amazon rainforest. Some of that soil carbon is 20,000 years old, so once it is lost it cannot be replaced on any human timescale. 46% of the Cerrado has already been cleared for crops and pasture.

## Why it matters

Planting trees feels like an obvious good, and in many places it is. This brief is a reminder that it depends where. The Cerrado's carbon sits mostly underground, in soils and deep roots, and it has taken thousands of years to build up. If climate finance keeps rewarding only forests, money may flow away from places that store more carbon and towards projects that damage them.

Water is the other reason to pay attention. Open ecosystems let more rain soak into the ground, refilling the groundwater that feeds rivers, including the Amazon downstream. Clearing the Cerrado can dry out the Amazon itself, which turns the usual "forest versus grassland" thinking around.

The brief has already had an effect. After it was presented at COP16 and COP30, Brazil's Ministry of the Environment set up a working group on a national strategy for open ecosystems. Policy can move before carbon markets catch up.

## Key facts

- Cerrado soils store up to **1,200 tonnes of carbon per hectare**, 6 times the above-ground carbon of Amazon rainforest vegetation.
- Some Cerrado soil carbon is about 20,000 years old. Once lost, it does not come back in decades or centuries.
- The Cerrado has more than 4,000 native plant species and more than 1,000 animal species. It feeds the headwaters of two-thirds of Brazil's major waterways, including the Amazon River.
- More than 46% of the Cerrado's original land cover has been cleared for crops and pasture, according to a 2022 study.
- Open ecosystems like the Cerrado are left out of REDD+ and carbon-credit programmes. These were designed for forests, so grassy landscapes do not fit their rules for measuring and certifying carbon.
- The bias has old roots. When Europeans mapped Brazil, forests were seen as "good" because they gave wood, and the Cerrado was written off as "wasteland". That colonial view has shaped science and policy ever since.
- The brief was presented at COP16 (biodiversity, Colombia) and COP30 (climate, Brazil). Brazil's Ministry of the Environment then set up a working group to develop a national strategy for open ecosystems.

## Voices in the piece

Franciele Peixoto, the lead author, explains why grasslands fall through the cracks: "All of the standards and methods for carbon markets are based on forested areas, so there is no way right now to include grasslands, savannas and other open ecosystems. Open ecosystems are under threat and continue to be neglected by climate policies that focus exclusively on forests and trees."

Amy Zanne of the Cary Institute stresses how slowly this carbon builds up: "Trees in the Amazon can grow back in decades to centuries, the carbon in some of the Cerrado soil has been there for as long as 20,000 years and cannot be replaced so quickly." She also warns that sacrificing the Cerrado will not save the Amazon: "Agribusinesses are converting massive amounts of Cerrado to grow soybeans to feed to livestock, without realising that they're going to lose the Amazon by drying out these biomes."

Natashi Pilon of UNICAMP, a co-lead, focuses on water: "People tend to think of forests as being important for the water supply, but actually, open ecosystems can be as important or even more important." On tree planting, she adds: "It is also essential to protect these areas from tree-planting projects, which may be well-intentioned but threaten the region's water supply, biodiversity and carbon stocks."

## Limits of the figures

The 1,200 tonnes per hectare figure is an upper value for Cerrado soils, not an average for every grassland. Whether the same holds for open ecosystems elsewhere in the world is not shown here. If it does, forest-first climate finance may be sending money to the wrong places on a large scale.
