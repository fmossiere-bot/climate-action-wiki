# Massive carbon stocks in grasslands ignored by climate finance — Peixoto et al., PLANTS, PEOPLE, PLANET

**Source:** Phys.org / Cary Institute of Ecosystem Studies, 23 October 2026 — https://phys.org/news/2026-10-massive-carbon-stocks-grasslands-climate.html
**Journal reference:** Peixoto F.P. et al., "Policy Brief: Brazil's open ecosystems — Carbon stocks overlooked by climate finance", *PLANTS, PEOPLE, PLANET* (2026), DOI 10.1002/ppp3.70292

## What it is

A new policy brief in *PLANTS, PEOPLE, PLANET* argues that **open ecosystems — grasslands, shrublands and savannas — are major carbon stores that climate finance systems ignore because their standards and methods (like REDD+) were built for forests**. Lead author **Franciele Peixoto** (environmental analyst, state of Goiás, Brazil), with co-authors **Amy Zanne** (Cary Institute) and **Natashi Pilon** (UNICAMP, Brazil), focuses on Brazil's **Cerrado** — the most biodiverse savanna in the world — where **soils can store up to 1,200 tonnes of carbon per hectare, six times more than Amazon rainforest vegetation**, and where some soil carbon is **20,000 years old** and cannot be replaced on human timescales. **46% of the Cerrado has already been cleared for crops and pasture.**

## Key facts

- **Cerrado soils store up to 1,200 tonnes of carbon per hectare** — 6× the above-ground carbon of Amazon rainforest vegetation
- Some Cerrado soil carbon is **~20,000 years old**; once lost, it does not come back in decades or centuries
- The Cerrado has **>4,000 native plant species**, >1,000 animal species, and feeds the headwaters for **two-thirds of Brazil's major waterways including the Amazon River**
- **>46% of the Cerrado's original land cover has been cleared** for crops and pasture (per 2022 study)
- Open ecosystems like the Cerrado are **not included in REDD+ or carbon-credit programmes** — these were designed for forests, so grassy ecosystems do not fit the measurement and certification frameworks
- Historical bias: when Europeans mapped Brazil, forests were "good" (wood), the Cerrado was "wasteland" — this colonial-era framing shaped scientific and policy priorities ever since
- The policy brief was presented at **COP16 (biodiversity, Colombia) and COP30 (climate, Brazil)**; following its presentation, Brazil's Ministry of the Environment established a working group to develop a national strategy for open ecosystems

## Notable quotes

- **Franciele Peixoto** (lead author): *"All of the standards and methods for carbon markets are based on forested areas, so there is no way right now to include grasslands, savannas and other open ecosystems. Open ecosystems are under threat and continue to be neglected by climate policies that focus exclusively on forests and trees."*
- **Amy Zanne** (Cary Institute, co-author): *"Trees in the Amazon can grow back in decades to centuries, the carbon in some of the Cerrado soil has been there for as long as 20,000 years and cannot be replaced so quickly."* And on sacrificing the Cerrado to save the Amazon: *"Agribusinesses are converting massive amounts of Cerrado to grow soybeans to feed to livestock — without realising that they're going to lose the Amazon by drying out these biomes."*
- **Natashi Pilon** (UNICAMP, co-lead): *"People tend to think of forests as being important for the water supply, but actually, open ecosystems can be as important or even more important."* And on tree planting: *"It is also essential to protect these areas from tree-planting projects, which may be well-intentioned but threaten the region's water supply, biodiversity and carbon stocks."*

## Why it matters

Three things:

1. **A specific, numerate case against "trees everywhere is always good".** 1,200 tonnes/hectare of 20,000-year-old carbon in Cerrado soils is a sharper number than most grassland-carbon claims. If this generalises to other open ecosystems globally, "forest first" climate finance is systematically mis-allocating resources.
2. **The water-security angle flips the forest-vs-grassland frame.** Open ecosystems let more water infiltrate the soil, which refills groundwater that feeds the Amazon downstream. Clearing Cerrado dries out the Amazon — a specific hydrological mechanism, not an abstract one.
3. **The brief has already changed Brazilian policy.** Following COP16 and COP30, Brazil's Ministry of the Environment set up a working group to develop a national strategy for open ecosystems. That shows how climate-finance methodology shifts can arrive via policy briefs even before carbon markets catch up.
