# No European bank commits to keep fossil fuel finance out of the Coral Triangle

**Source:** BankTrack News, first published 17 September 2026, revised 5 October 2026. https://www.banktrack.org/article/bnp_paribas_commits_to_protect_coral_triangle_with_new_commitment_on_lng_terminals

## What is it

In June 2026, 36 environmental and community groups wrote to 24 European commercial banks. They asked the banks to stop financing fossil fuel projects, and the companies behind them, in the world's most important areas for wildlife. Their main example was the **Coral Triangle**, the sea area between Indonesia, Malaysia, Papua New Guinea, the Philippines, the Solomon Islands and Timor-Leste. It is the richest marine region on Earth.

Seventeen banks replied. Most pointed to policies they already have, such as excluding certain areas, checking certain sectors more carefully, or following voluntary standards like the Equator Principles. None of the 24 committed to rule out fossil fuel finance in critical biodiversity areas.

BNP Paribas went furthest. It said that, as of July 2026, it does not finance oil and gas exploration or production in the Coral Triangle, nor any LNG terminals there, floating or onshore. BankTrack first reported this as a new commitment. The bank then contacted BankTrack to say it had made no new commitment and had only described its current financing. BankTrack corrected the article on 5 October.

## Why it matters

Bank policies on fossil fuels usually focus on climate. This letter adds a second argument: drilling, pipelines and gas terminals also destroy habitats directly, through spills, noise, dredging and construction on fragile coasts. More than 120 million people in the wider Coral Triangle area rely on its reefs, seagrass and mangroves for food, income and protection from storms.

The BNP Paribas episode shows a gap that runs through bank climate policy. A bank can truthfully say it is not financing something today, while keeping the freedom to do so tomorrow. Only a written commitment closes that door. BNP Paribas's formal policy only excludes upstream oil and gas projects and LNG export terminals that run on more than 10% shale gas.

## Key facts

- The Coral Triangle holds 76% of the world's coral species and more than 2,000 species of reef fish.
- More than 100 offshore oil and gas blocks already operate in the Coral Triangle Initiative area. About 24% of coral habitats, 22% of seagrass meadows and 37% of mangroves sit inside oil and gas concessions.
- More than 450 further offshore blocks are being explored. If they were all developed, oil and gas infrastructure would grow from 1% to 16% of the area.
- As of January 2024, 19 LNG terminals were operating in the area and 31 more were planned or being built. Most sit within 10 kilometres of coral, seagrass or mangroves.
- Worldwide, average vertebrate populations have fallen by more than 70% since 1970, and nearly 50,000 species are threatened with extinction.
- The groups ask banks to stop project finance for fossil fuel infrastructure in critical biodiversity areas (except for decommissioning), stop corporate finance for companies developing new projects there, and require a Paris-aligned phase-out plan from companies with existing projects.

## Limits of the source

BankTrack is a campaign group and one of the letter's signatories, so this is an advocacy report rather than an independent review. The figures on concessions and terminals come from a Coral Triangle risk assessment it links to, which uses its own boundary for the area: the scientific Coral Triangle plus a 50-kilometre sea buffer and a 10-kilometre land buffer.
