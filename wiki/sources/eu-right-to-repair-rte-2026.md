# EU right-to-repair rules enter force in Ireland (RTÉ, August 2026)

**Source:** RTÉ Business, Brian O'Donovan, 24 August 2026. https://www.rte.ie/news/business/2026/0824/1588931-repair-rules/

## What it is

RTÉ reports that the **EU Right to Repair Directive** came into force in Ireland in August 2026. Once the seller's guarantee has run out, you can now ask the manufacturer to repair a product that can technically be fixed. The repair must come at a reasonable price and within a reasonable time.

The rules cover household and electronic goods such as washing machines, vacuum cleaners, mobile phones and tablets. Ireland is also developing a national repair platform, RepairMyStuff.ie, to help people find someone to fix their things.

Behind the law is a waste problem. Across the EU, people throw away goods that could still work, and this adds up to an estimated 35 million tonnes of waste every year.

## Why it matters

Until now, the manufacturer's responsibility mostly ended when the guarantee did. Under the Directive, repair on request becomes an obligation for years after you buy something. That gives companies a reason to design products that can be fixed in the first place, rather than dealing with the problem only at the recycling stage.

It could also revive a trade that has been fading. For about two decades, small repair shops have struggled as goods became cheaper to replace than to fix. The European Commission expects the rules to bring €4.8 billion in growth and investment across the EU, which could help repair cafés, small electronics repairers and refurbishment start-ups.

There is a climate angle too. Making a new device uses materials and energy, so every phone or washing machine kept in use for another 2-3 years avoids the emissions of making a replacement. This matches the argument in the [IPBES Nexus Assessment](ipbes-nexus-assessment-2024.md) that taking fewer materials out of the ground and throwing fewer away is a climate measure in its own right. Earlier reporting on puffer jackets found that only 1 in 10 items is repaired, and that repair adds about 2.5 years of useful life. The new rules put legal weight behind that kind of repair.

## Key facts

- Throwing away goods before the end of their useful life creates **35 million tonnes** of waste a year across the EU.
- The European Commission expects the rules to bring €4.8 billion in growth and investment within the EU.
- Products covered include washing machines, vacuum cleaners, mobile phones and tablets, along with other categories under the Directive.
- The right applies after the seller's guarantee has expired. You can ask for a repair, not only a replacement, at a reasonable cost.
- Manufacturers must repair technically repairable products on request, give easy-to-find information about repair services, and sell spare parts at a reasonable price.
- RepairMyStuff.ie will be developed further to serve as Ireland's national repair platform.
- The Directive is part of the EU's wider circular-economy laws, alongside the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR).

## Voices in the piece

Two Irish ministers are quoted. Peter Burke, Minister for Enterprise, Tourism and Employment, oversaw how Ireland brought the rules into law. He said: "These regulations will make it easier for consumers to choose repair over replacement, while also creating opportunities for Irish businesses operating in the repair and refurbishment sector. In implementing the Directive, we have sought to strike the right balance between supporting consumers, encouraging sustainable economic activity and ensuring that businesses are not faced with unnecessary regulatory burdens."

Darragh O'Brien, Minister for Climate, Energy and the Environment, linked the rules to Ireland's climate targets: "These new regulations are important in helping to meet our climate goals by realigning how we produce, consume, and value materials."

## Limits of the figures

The article is straight policy news and gives few numbers beyond the €4.8bn and 35 million tonne figures. It does not say which product categories count as technically repairable, or how a "reasonable" price will be judged. Those questions will be answered as the Directive is tested in practice.
