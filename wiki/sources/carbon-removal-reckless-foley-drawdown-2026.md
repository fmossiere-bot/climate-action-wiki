# Why assuming massive growth in carbon removal is reckless

**Source:** Project Drawdown, Jonathan Foley, October 2026. https://drawdown.org/insights/heres-why-assuming-massive-growth-in-carbon-removal-is-reckless-and-unwarranted

## What is it

Jonathan Foley, a climate scientist and executive director of Project Drawdown, takes aim at future climate scenarios that assume huge amounts of carbon dioxide will be pulled out of the air later this century. A recent set of scenarios used by climate modellers and the IPCC (the CMIP7 scenarios, produced by a separate group of modellers) includes some that remove 10 to 30 billion tonnes of CO2 a year by 2100 using new engineered methods. That would be a **5,000- to 15,000-fold increase** on today.

Foley compares this to the old joke about the economist stranded with tins of food who says, "First, assume we have a can opener." The scenarios come from economic models, he argues, and rest on assumptions rather than evidence that the technology can grow that much.

## Why it matters

If governments and companies believe large-scale removal is coming, they can justify cutting emissions more slowly now. Foley calls this "predatory delay": favouring the promise of future technology over action today. With the world set to pass 1.5°C, some people talk of overshooting and then cooling the planet with carbon removal. Foley says the removal needed for that is nowhere in sight. The surest way to stop warming is still to stop emitting.

## Key facts

- "Conventional" removal, mostly forests regrowing on cleared land, is close to 2 billion tonnes of CO2 a year. But this regrowth is already counted in land-use emission figures, so counting it again as removal is double-counting.
- "Novel" removal, which takes CO2 out and stores it durably, is about 0.002 billion tonnes a year. That equals about 17 minutes of humanity's yearly greenhouse gas emissions.
- Most novel removal comes from BECCS (burning biomass for power and storing the CO2). Whether it is truly carbon negative depends on where the biomass comes from.
- Biochar is next. Project Drawdown estimates it could remove at most about 0.7 billion tonnes a year, but it removes almost nothing today. Feedstock is the limit.
- Direct air capture costs more than $1,000 per tonne, and costs have stayed stubbornly high. Capturing 1 billion tonnes a year, 1.6% of current emissions, would cost over $1 trillion a year.
- Storing 1 billion tonnes a year underground would need thousands of new dedicated wells each year, more than the whole US oil and gas industry drills today.
- Forest, peatland and land restoration could remove a few billion tonnes a year for a few decades, small against the roughly 60 billion tonnes the world emits each year, and vulnerable to fire and ploughing.

## What Foley thinks is realistic

Foley expects carbon removal to stay close to today's level: a couple of billion tonnes a year from forests and soils, and a small fraction of a billion tonnes from novel methods. He sees biochar from waste as the most promising novel route. He is harsh on direct air capture, calling it "a billion-dollar boondoggle", partly because captured CO2 is often used for enhanced oil recovery, pumping more oil out of the ground. Ocean alkalinity enhancement and enhanced rock weathering might work, he says, but may be impossible to measure and verify in practice, so they should be funded as pilots and watched carefully.

This is an opinion piece by a scientist with a clear view, and it is more pessimistic than some other experts. Its numbers, though, come from published sources such as Carbon Brief's state of carbon removal analysis and BloombergNEF's work on direct air capture costs.
