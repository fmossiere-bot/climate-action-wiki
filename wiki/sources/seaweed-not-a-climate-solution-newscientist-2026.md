# Why growing seaweed isn't a climate solution — Filbee-Dexter et al., PLOS Biology 2026

## What it is

New Scientist coverage (Alec Luhn, **8 September 2026**) of a **PLOS Biology study by Karen Filbee-Dexter (University of Western Australia) and colleagues** arguing that **kelp restoration and farming will not be a major climate solution**, and that the priority should shift from growing more seaweed to protecting what is left. **The critical fact: with ~60% of the world's kelp forests already in decline, they could eventually emit more CO2 than they absorb — and the ~50,000 tonnes/year CO2 drawdown from ambitious restoration projects is dwarfed by the ~14 million tonnes/year global kelp loss.**

## Key facts

- Seaweeds absorb an estimated **3.7 billion tonnes of CO2/year via photosynthesis**, drawing carbon from seawater (which pulls more from the air). Most of it is re-released when the seaweed is digested or decomposed near the coast; **only a small fraction** sinks to the deep sea as "blue carbon" for thousands of years
- **Global kelp forests cover ~640,000 km²** — slightly larger than France
- **~60% of kelp forests are in decline** due to ocean heat. Filbee-Dexter's group concludes these ecosystems could eventually **emit more CO2 than they absorb**
- Global kelp loss is now emitting an estimated **14 million tonnes of CO2 per year**
- A Norway experiment restored **70 hectares** of kelp by poisoning seaweed-eating sea urchins — but the drawdown was only about **50,000 tonnes CO2/year**
- Preserving kelp is **at least 8× cheaper per hectare** than restoring it, yet **only 2% of kelp forests are in protected areas**
- **US start-up Running Tide** raised $70 million to grow kelp on wooden buoys designed to sink into the deep sea; **it had difficulty growing kelp in the open ocean and shut down** in 2025
- **Seaweed farming now takes up ~4,000 km²** of ocean, only about a third of the annual kelp loss. In a study earlier in 2026, **9 out of 11 seaweed farms emitted more CO2 than they sequestered** once boat fuel and plastic lines were counted
- **Kelp Blue** and other aquaculture firms have promised to sequester and offset hundreds of millions of tonnes of CO2 — a promise this study says is not credible

## Notable quotes

- **Karen Filbee-Dexter (UWA):** *"Instead of asking how much carbon we can remove with seaweeds, we should be asking how much carbon we can stop from being released as these ecosystems are damaged by climate change."*
- **Filbee-Dexter:** *"Seaweed restoration and farming is unlikely to be a major climate change solution. But that doesn't mean it isn't worthwhile."* Restoration still supports biodiversity, and farming can make low-carbon alternatives to fossil-based products like fertiliser
- **Pamela Walsh (Queen's University Belfast, dissenting):** scientists don't yet know how much carbon seaweed sequesters well enough to write it off. *"Blue carbon is part of the puzzle. It can make a contribution. The question is how that is determined and validated."*

## Why it matters

Two things:

1. **A specific climate-offset claim is deflated.** Seaweed cultivation has been marketed as a way to remove **hundreds of millions of tonnes of CO2** and has attracted significant venture capital (Running Tide's $70m and shutdown is the illustrative case). The study finds farm-level emissions from operations often exceed sequestration, and the fraction of biomass that reaches the deep sea is small.
2. **The preservation-vs-restoration priority flips.** The paper argues protecting existing kelp is far more cost-effective than restoring lost kelp, but only 2% of kelp forests are protected. This mirrors the mangrove and peatland pattern: "don't lose what you have" beats "restore what you lost", every time.

There is a valid dissent: Pamela Walsh's point that the science is not yet strong enough to write blue carbon off. The reasonable middle ground is that **kelp restoration remains worthwhile for biodiversity and coastal fisheries** — as [Formosa](taiwan-offshore-wind-reef-fish-2026.md), [Hywind](hywind-scotland-robot-reef-survey-2026.md) and [French sea-urchin control](../climate-adaptation/artificial-reefs-coastal-protection.md) show — but that governments and companies should stop treating seaweed as a serious CO2 offset.

## Tone and angle

Straight environmental science reporting. Filbee-Dexter is given room to state the case; Walsh gets the counter-view. Notes both the biodiversity and low-carbon-fertiliser upsides even while ruling out the climate-offset claim. No greenwashing framing, though the Kelp Blue and Running Tide references invite it.
