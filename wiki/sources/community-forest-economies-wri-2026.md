# Forest communities are showing what a better economy looks like: WRI

**Source:** World Resources Institute (WRI), Wanjira Mathai, November 2026. https://www.wri.org/insights/community-led-forest-communities-economy

## What is it

Wanjira Mathai, WRI's managing director for Africa and global partnerships, writes about a learning visit to the Brazilian Amazon in March 2026, a few months after the COP30 climate summit in Belém. In the riverside community of Urucureá, people weave baskets from tucumã palm fibre, gather tonka beans from cumaru trees for perfume, tap copaíba trees for oil used in soaps and medicines, and turn cassava into foods such as tucupi sauce.

This is what Brazilians call the **sociobioeconomy**: economic life built on the link between communities and living forests. Mathai uses the wider term "community-led forest economies" for economies run mainly by Indigenous Peoples, Afro-descendant Peoples and local communities. Similar traditions include social forestry in Indonesia, community forestry in the Congo Basin, and community forest enterprises in Mexico and Central America.

## Why it matters

Indigenous Peoples and local communities hold or manage lands containing **54% of the world's remaining intact forests**. Research shows these lands often have more species and less deforestation than land run by governments or companies, and at lower cost. Yet the people protecting them carry the risks and get little of the value. Global Witness recorded at least 124 land and environmental defenders killed worldwide in 2025, 26 of them in Brazil.

Mathai's central argument is that this needs a shift in power. Communities should be treated as leaders, rights holders and business owners, not as beneficiaries of other people's projects. That includes respecting free, prior and informed consent: the right to say yes or no to plans affecting their land, with full information, without pressure and before anything starts.

## What would help

- **Rewarding stewardship.** UNEP's State of Finance for Nature 2026 report estimates that $7.3 trillion flowed into activities that harm nature in 2023, against $220 billion for nature-based solutions, a ratio of more than 30 to 1.
- **Direct finance.** A 2025 analysis by Rainforest Foundation Norway and the Rights and Resources Initiative found that funding for community land rights stayed below 1% of climate-related aid and philanthropy between 2011 and 2024.
- **Secure land rights.** At COP30, 15 governments backed a commitment to recognise community rights over 160 million hectares by 2030, and a linked pledge committed $1.8 billion.
- **Infrastructure and markets.** Communities need working capital, transport, renewable energy, internet and healthcare, plus fairer prices. In cocoa, FAO-highlighted research found farmers get about 11% of a chocolate bar's value, against a combined 70% for brands and retailers.

## Key facts

- Turiarte, a cooperative led by a young woman at the time of the visit, sells handicrafts and community tourism, helping women earn their own income.
- Amélias da Amazônia, a women-led business in the Tapajós National Forest, makes beauty and food products from forest resources such as tonka beans.
- The Ecocentro da Sociobioeconomia in Santarém gathers producers under one roof for processing, transport and sale, strengthening their bargaining power.
- Public buying, such as school lunch programmes, can give local producers steadier demand.
- The Bioeconomy Challenge, launched at COP30, and the Pan-Amazon Network for Bioeconomy both involve WRI.
