# Collapse of key ocean current may release billions of tonnes of carbon

**Source:** New Scientist, Alec Luhn, 13 April 2026 (updated 20 May 2026). The study was published in Nature Communications Earth & Environment (DOI: 10.1038/s43247-026-03427-w). The lead researcher is Da Nian of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, and Johan Rockström is a co-author.

## What it is

The AMOC (Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation) is a system of ocean currents that includes the Gulf Stream. Scientists have long worried it could collapse as the climate warms. This article reports new computer modelling of what that collapse would do on the other side of the world, in the Southern Ocean around Antarctica.

The answer is a chain reaction. If the AMOC stops, deep water in the Southern Ocean starts mixing upwards and gives up the carbon it has stored for a very long time. The study puts that release at up to **640 billion tonnes of CO₂**, enough to warm the planet by an extra 0.2°C on top of the other effects of a collapse.

## Why it matters

The AMOC has already weakened by about 15%. The study finds that above 350 ppm of CO₂ in the air (ppm means parts per million), a collapse, once it starts, could not be reversed. We are already at 430 ppm.

That does not mean collapse is imminent. It could be decades or centuries away. The worry is about when we lock it in, not when we feel it. Rockström puts it this way: "What matters is not the impact time, it is the commitment time. That commitment time may be within the next 25 to 50 years. It's literally now."

For people in northern Europe the stakes are close to home. The AMOC carries warm water north and keeps our winters mild.

## Key facts

- The AMOC brings warm, salty water to northern Europe, which keeps winters mild.
- Meltwater from Greenland is diluting the current. Fresher water is lighter, so it sinks more slowly, and that sinking is what drives the AMOC.
- The AMOC has already declined by about 15%. A collapse could come anywhere from decades to centuries from now.
- Above 350 ppm of CO₂ (we are at 430 ppm), a collapse would be irreversible once triggered.
- A collapse would set off convection in the deep Southern Ocean. That water holds huge amounts of carbon, from dead plankton and from CO₂ absorbed from the air.
- Up to 640 billion tonnes of CO₂ could escape near Antarctica, adding 0.2°C of warming over 1,000+ years.
- Other known effects of a collapse: the Arctic cooling by 7°C, Antarctica warming by 6°C, disrupted monsoons in Africa and Asia, and a possible collapse of the East Antarctic Ice Sheet, which would raise sea levels by metres.
