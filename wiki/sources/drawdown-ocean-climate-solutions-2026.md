# What floats and what sinks: ocean-based climate solutions (Project Drawdown, 2026)

**Source:** Project Drawdown, Christina Richardson PhD, 2 June 2026. https://drawdown.org/insights/what-floats-and-what-sinks-when-it-comes-to-ocean-based-climate-solutions

## What it is

Christina Richardson, a research fellow at Project Drawdown, looked at 14+ ocean-based climate solutions and ranked them by how ready they are and how much they could cut emissions. **Offshore wind** comes out far ahead of everything else.

Protecting coastal wetlands (mangroves, salt marshes and seagrasses) and seaweed ecosystems is worth doing, even though the climate gain is modest. Newer ways of pulling carbon out of the sea, known as marine carbon dioxide removal (mCDR), are still very uncertain. Three ideas, sinking ocean biomass, artificial upwelling and ocean fertilisation, are not recommended at all.

## Why it matters

The ocean gets a lot of attention as a climate saviour, and plenty of start-ups promise to use it to soak up carbon. This ranking is a useful reality check. The biggest ocean contribution is clean electricity from offshore wind, not clever chemistry.

It also points out that the strongest climate actions available today are mostly on land: solar farms, onshore wind, changing what we eat and protecting forests. The ocean still matters a great deal for nature and for the people who rely on it, but protecting it is badly underfunded.

## Key facts

- Offshore wind could achieve 1.90 to 3.04 Gt CO2-eq a year (billions of tonnes of greenhouse gases avoided), making it the top ocean-based solution.
- Protecting coastal wetlands and seaweed ecosystems together could deliver 0.21 to 0.30 Gt CO2-eq a year.
- Every other ocean action (restoring ecosystems, reducing overfishing, protecting the seafloor, improving fish and shellfish farming) delivers under 0.1 Gt CO2-eq a year each.
- Ocean alkalinity enhancement and ocean electrochemistry are uncertain and still in research and development.
- Sinking ocean biomass, artificial upwelling and ocean fertilisation are not recommended: they are likely to be ineffective or risky for marine life.
- Ocean protection receives about $1.2 billion a year, against an estimated need of about $16 billion a year to meet the 30x30 target (protecting 30% of the ocean by 2030).
- The highest-impact climate actions today are mostly on land: utility-scale solar, onshore wind, diet change and forest protection.
