# How offshore wind may change marine food webs: Phys.org

**Source:** Phys.org, provided by the Biodiversity Research Institute, 5 November 2026. https://phys.org/news/2026-11-explores-offshore-marine-food-webs.html. Study: Kimberly A. Lato et al., "Assessing potential impacts of offshore wind development on U.S. marine ecosystems using food web modeling", *Scientific Reports* (2026). https://dx.doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-63407-0

## What is it

Offshore wind turbines stand on foundations that add hard surfaces to a seabed that is often soft sand or mud. Mussels, barnacles and other small animals settle on them, and the structures start to act as **artificial reefs**. A team led by Kim Lato, a scientist at the Biodiversity Research Institute (BRI) and the University of New Hampshire, asked what that does to the wider marine food web: who eats whom, and how energy moves from the smallest creatures up to fish.

The researchers pulled together existing ecological data and built food web models for three offshore wind sites in southern New England, on the US Atlantic coast. They compared conditions before construction with observed or projected conditions about four years after.

## Why it matters

Offshore wind is growing fast along the US Atlantic coast and in Europe's seas, so knowing what it does underwater matters. Earlier wiki pages describe single surveys that counted new fish and corals on turbine bases. This study goes a step further by modelling the whole system, which helps decide what to monitor and how to manage future wind farms.

## Key facts

- The models suggest offshore wind may push food webs towards a **more mature and stable** state, though results varied between sites and between measures.
- Animals living in the sediment turned out to be especially important to how the food web works in every scenario.
- Among creatures growing on turbine structures, bivalves such as mussels were the most influential, with the potential to change energy flows between species.
- The team found large gaps in the data, especially on microorganisms and the processes that recycle nutrients and dead material on the seabed.
- This is the first publication from BRI's project on how seabed changes linked to offshore wind affect US marine ecosystems.

## Limits

These are models built from existing data, not new field measurements, and they look only about four years past construction. The authors present the work as a framework for future research and monitoring rather than a final answer.
