# Positive Tipping Points, Part I: Urgency, by Tim Lenton

**Source:** Tim Lenton, *Positive Tipping Points*, Part I "Urgency" (Chapters 1-6). Plain-language summary prepared from photographs of the pages.

## What it is

This is a summary of Part I of Tim Lenton's book *Positive Tipping Points*, called "Urgency", which covers Chapters 1-6. Lenton's case is that slow, step-by-step change on climate is no longer fast enough. Only **reinforcing feedbacks** in society, technology and nature, which he calls "positive tipping points", can move quickly enough. He uses history and current data to show that these are real and can be set off on purpose.

He describes himself as a "conditional optimist": if enough people act, the good tipping points can flip, and if they don't, the bad ones win. Lenton coined the term "tipping elements" with John Schellnhuber and first listed 9 climate tipping points in 2008. The updated list has 16, and 5 of them are already in the danger zone at today's warming of about 1.25°C.

## Why it matters

Lenton offers the most detailed public map of where the climate's tipping points sit, and of what could trigger good ones on the other side. His framework now shapes how many European scientists and policymakers talk about "abrupt change", both the frightening kind (the AMOC ocean current, Greenland, the Amazon) and the hopeful kind (electric cars in Norway, the end of coal in the UK).

His ideas also sit behind campaigns for transitions that move "five times faster". For a reader who feels the climate news is all bad, the book is a reminder that sudden change can work in our favour too.

## Tipping points as reinforcing feedback (Chapters 1-2)

A tipping point comes when the forces holding a system steady weaken and a reinforcing feedback takes over. After that, change is fast, hard to stop and hard to reverse. Shocks in human networks, such as the 2021 heat dome, the Ever Given blocking the Suez Canal in 2021, COVID and the 2008 financial crisis, show how small causes can have huge effects when networks amplify them.

The same mechanics can work for good. Norway's electric cars went from 0.25% of new sales in 2010 to **84% in 2024**, following the S-shaped curve typical of new technology. UK coal fell from 40% of electricity in 2012 to 2% in 2019 once a carbon price made coal plants lose money. Worldwide, wind and solar double about every 3 years, and solar is now forecast to supply more than 50% of the world's electricity by mid-century.

Three feedbacks drive new technology. The first is Wright's Law, or learning by doing: costs fall by about 20% every time production doubles. The second is economies of scale, and the third is the build-out of supporting infrastructure. In Norway, tax policy tipped buyers towards electric cars by removing the price gap.

## The climate tipping points already close (Chapter 4)

There are sixteen tipping elements, and five are in the danger zone at 1.25°C:

- **Greenland ice sheet:** as it melts, its surface drops into warmer air and melts faster. It holds 7 m of sea-level rise.
- **West Antarctica (Thwaites, Pine Island):** the ice sits below sea level. At 1.5°C it could all be lost, giving more than 3 m of sea-level rise over centuries.
- **Winter Arctic sea ice:** could tip at just over 1.5°C, starting in the Barents Sea.
- **AMOC (the Atlantic current system):** has slowed by about 15% in 50 years. A collapse would cool the UK, dry out Europe, cut tropical rainfall worldwide and halve the wheat and maize belts.
- **Subpolar gyre (North Atlantic):** a circulating current that could switch off within 10 years at somewhere between 1.1-3.8°C. Lenton says "almost nobody has heard of it, but it may be one of the nearest tipping points".
- **Amazon:** already 18% cleared. Warming plus deforestation could turn about 40% of what is left into dry savanna.
- **Permafrost:** holds twice as much carbon as all human CO2 emissions so far. Large-scale thaw is likely at about 1.5°C and full collapse at ≥4°C, which would add about 10% to warming.
- **Coral reefs:** 70-90% lost at 1.5°C and almost all at 2°C. 500 million people depend on them.

## The climate niche and its migration (Chapter 5)

Humans live within a "climate niche". Most people live where the yearly average temperature is about 13°C (London 11, New York 12, Beijing 13), with a second, smaller group at 25-27°C, mainly in monsoon India. That niche has barely moved in 6,000 years. Warming is now shifting it fast, and the burden falls mostly on the world's poorest people.

**Every 0.1°C of extra warming pushes more than 100 million more people into extreme heat.** At 1.5°C, about 400 million people are exposed. At 2.7°C, where current policies lead, it is about 2 billion, including more than 600 million in India and more than 300 million in Nigeria. Holding warming at 1.5°C instead of 2.7°C cuts the number exposed by a factor of five worldwide, six for India and seven for Nigeria.

The unfairness is plain. The lifetime emissions of just 1.2 average Americans are enough to push one future person into extreme heat, and the people who will suffer emit half the world average.

## Averting disaster: the Titanic warning (Chapter 6)

Lenton compares the climate to the Titanic: six ice warnings were ignored, the radio operators were busy, and the ship turned too slowly. Even if emissions stopped today, warming of 0.1-2°C is already committed. If greenhouse gas levels froze now, there would be a more than 80% chance of passing 1.5°C and more than 50% of passing 2°C. Current policies point to about 2.7°C. If every announced target were met, it would be about 1.8°C. None of these paths reach net zero.

- **Carbon budget:** at the start of 2023, about 250 Gt CO2 was left for a 50:50 chance of staying at 1.5°C. At current rates it will be gone before 2030. Staying at 1.5°C needs emissions to fall 8% every year. COVID gave a 5% cut for one year, and only the Great Depression reached 10%.
- **1.7°C:** possible but "extremely hard". It needs 10% a year cuts across all sectors, halving emissions by 2038 and net zero by about 2050. Simon Sharpe's *Five Times Faster* path (7% a year) gives 1.7°C.
- **Methane:** could be halved in ten years. Half of those cuts cost nothing, and over half of the cuts to fossil methane would make money.
- **Sulphur pollution:** acts as "a brake", cooling the planet roughly as much as methane warms it, but it kills about 5 million people a year. Cleaning the air removes the brake, so methane cuts need to move faster.

## Objections Lenton addresses

On the claim that **changing fast costs too much**, Lenton leaves the economics for Part II. The ethical question, he says, is whether money is the right measure when lives are at stake. On the Titanic, third-class passengers missed the lifeboats.

On the fear that **fast change will cause a financial crash**, he argues that fossil assets losing value is a reason to make the financial system stronger, not a reason to keep burning fuel.

On the hope that **geoengineering will fix it**, he notes that solar geoengineering "changes the climate in new ways", creates new political disputes and does nothing about the cause. He mentions it but leaves it out of the book.

## Key facts

- 5 of 16 climate tipping elements are already in the danger zone at 1.25°C, and every reassessment raises the risk.
- The AMOC has slowed about 15% in 50 years. A collapse would end farming in the UK and shrink the world's grain belts.
- Every 0.1°C of warming puts more than 100 million more people into extreme heat.
- Keeping to 1.5°C rather than 2.7°C cuts the number exposed to extreme heat 5× worldwide, 6× for India and 7× for Nigeria.
- Norway's electric cars went from 0.25% to 84% of sales in 14 years. UK coal fell from 40% to 2% of electricity in 7 years. Wind and solar double every 3 years.
- The budget for 1.5°C was about 250 Gt CO2 at the start of 2023, which needs cuts of 8% a year (COVID gave 5%).
- Cleaning up sulphur pollution removes about 0.4°C of hidden cooling, which makes methane cuts more urgent, not less.
