# A healthier global diet could cut farm emissions by 85%

**Source:** ScienceDaily, London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine press release, 23 July 2026. https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260716023557.htm

**Peer-reviewed study:** Gibson M et al. (2026), *Food systems transformation would reshape global agriculture*, *Nature*, DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-10775-2

## What it is

A press summary of a new paper in *Nature* by a team from the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (LSHTM) and Cornell, working with 10 modelling groups. It asks what would happen to farming if the world adopted the **planetary health diet** set out by the EAT-Lancet 2025 Commission, while also farming more efficiently and halving food loss and waste.

The paper puts economic and land-use numbers on that shift: which parts of farming would shrink, which would grow, by how much and where. It follows on directly from the [EAT-Lancet 2025 planetary health diet](../sectors/agriculture-food/eat-lancet-planetary-diet-2025.md).

## Why it matters

The EAT-Lancet Commission had already made the health case for changing diets, and sketched the climate case. This paper adds the money and the map. That matters most for the people who would feel the change first: livestock farmers and the communities that depend on them.

The 85% figure needs a word of explanation. It refers to CO2 from land-use change, such as clearing forest for pasture or crops. The 2025 EAT-Lancet report cited a smaller 15% cut, but that was for non-CO2 farm emissions like methane, so the two numbers measure different things. Planning a fair transition for farmers alongside a change in diets is the gap this study helps fill.

## Key facts

- Net CO2 emissions from agricultural land-use change could be **85% lower in 2050 than in 2020** if the world took up the recommended diet along with efficiency gains and less food waste.
- Global farmland use would fall by up to 6% compared with current trends.
- The value of livestock production would fall by about 42% (about $630 billion) between 2020 and 2050. Ruminants such as cattle and sheep would fall by about 70% (about $274bn), meaning around 400 million fewer ruminant animals worldwide.
- The value of vegetable, fruit, nut and legume production would rise by about 57% (about $890 billion).
- The EAT-Lancet 2025 estimate still stands: the shift could prevent 15 million premature deaths a year.
- Earlier research puts the hidden costs of today's food system at $10–20 trillion a year, through ill health, environmental damage and lost productivity.

## Winners and losers by region (2050 compared with 2020)

- **United States**: total farm production value down 21% (–$76bn). Crop value up 20% (+$40bn), livestock value down 73% (–$116bn).
- **India**: total value up 46% (+$198bn). Crops up 65% (+$208bn), livestock down 8% (–$10bn). India would gain economically as well as environmentally.
- **Europe**: total value down 35% (–$190bn). Crops down 8% (–$22bn), livestock down 66% (–$168bn).

The differences come down to each region's farming systems, current diets, production costs, available land, trade and the kinds of food it grows.

## Limits of the figures

The model assumes what the authors call a "costless consumer preference shift". In other words, it assumes people change what they eat willingly, with no money, cultural or practical barriers. In real life, healthy food is not equally affordable or easy to get everywhere. The scenarios are foresight modelling, an early guide to possible futures rather than a forecast. Without careful policy, the change would also cause severe economic disruption for communities that depend on livestock.

## Voices in the piece

Dr Matt Gibson, the lead author at LSHTM and Cornell, does not play down the upheaval: "Transforming food systems would deliver enormous potential benefits to our health and the environment but, as our results make clear, they would also lead to fundamental changes to global agriculture and affect the lives of millions of farmers and food producers. Rather than using these results as an excuse for inaction, it's critical that governments rise to the challenge and make difficult decisions for the good of our health and the planet. This means confronting powerful groups that profit from the status quo."

Daniel Mason-D'Croz of Cornell argues the planning has to start now: "A transformation of this magnitude cannot begin in 2050. Foresight modeling like that highlighted in this study is a valuable tool to inform actions today."
