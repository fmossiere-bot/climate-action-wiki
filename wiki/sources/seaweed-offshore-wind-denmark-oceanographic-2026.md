# Offshore wind farms hold key to sustainable seaweed at scale

**Source:** Oceanographic Magazine, Rob Hutchins, 4 August 2026 — https://oceanographicmagazine.com/news/offshore-wind-farms-hold-key-to-sustainable-seaweed-at-scale/

## What it is

Danish researchers at **Aarhus University**, led by senior researcher **Annette Bruhn**, have spent 15 years refining methods to grow **sugar kelp** in Danish waters and have shown that **offshore wind farms can double as seaweed cultivation sites** — seaweed lines strung between turbine arrays in the North Sea, sharing service vessels and infrastructure already built for energy production. The technology works; the market and supply chain around it do not yet exist.

## Key facts cited

- **"Multi-use"** concept: seaweed cultivation integrated into offshore wind infrastructure, using no additional marine space
- Offshore conditions (high water exchange, stability, nutrient availability) may be among the **most productive cultivation settings available anywhere** — higher yields and quality than expected
- Sugar kelp cultivation actively **removes nitrogen and phosphorus** from coastal waters, complementing nutrient-pollution reduction efforts
- Seaweed requires **no land, no irrigation, no synthetic fertiliser** — grows on sunlight and ambient seawater nutrients
- Uses: eaten directly, processed into gelling agents/stabilisers/antioxidants, animal feed, bio-based materials
- Denmark's offshore wind expansion will dedicate large new areas of the North Sea to energy generation by 2030 — researchers argue the unused space between turbines is too significant to leave idle
- The bottleneck is **not the science but the market**: Bruhn calls for food manufacturers, retailers, meal-kit companies and canteens to create demand, drawing a parallel with legumes, which went from marginal to mainstream in European diets over roughly a decade

## Why it matters

This is a distinct case from the wiki's existing seaweed-as-carbon-removal framing: here seaweed is a **food and biorefining crop**, and the innovation is **spatial co-location with offshore wind** rather than carbon sequestration. It also strengthens the broader "renewables plus co-benefit" pattern already documented for offshore wind (reef biodiversity at Formosa and Hywind) and solar (peatland birds) — here the co-benefit is food production rather than biodiversity.
