# Offshore wind farms hold key to sustainable seaweed at scale

**Source:** Oceanographic Magazine, Rob Hutchins, 4 August 2026. https://oceanographicmagazine.com/news/offshore-wind-farms-hold-key-to-sustainable-seaweed-at-scale/

## What it is

Researchers at Aarhus University in Denmark, led by senior researcher Annette Bruhn, have spent 15 years improving ways to grow **sugar kelp** in Danish waters. They have shown that offshore wind farms can double as seaweed farms. Lines of seaweed are strung between the turbines in the North Sea, and the farms share the service boats and equipment already in place for the wind farm.

The growing method works. What is missing is a market and a supply chain to buy and process the seaweed.

## Why it matters

Most of the wiki's seaweed coverage is about seaweed as a way to remove carbon. Here it is a crop, grown for food and for raw materials. The new idea is putting it in the same space as offshore wind, rather than locking away carbon.

It also adds to a pattern already seen with renewables: a wind or solar site that brings a second benefit. Offshore wind farms at Formosa and Hywind have become reefs for marine life, and some solar farms on peatland have helped birds. In this case the extra benefit is food.

## Key facts

- The idea is called "multi-use": growing seaweed inside offshore wind farms, so no extra sea space is needed.
- Offshore waters, with strong water flow, steady conditions and plenty of nutrients, may be among the most productive places anywhere to grow seaweed. Yields and quality were higher than expected.
- Sugar kelp takes nitrogen and phosphorus out of coastal waters, which helps where farm run-off has polluted the sea.
- Seaweed needs no land, no watering and no synthetic fertiliser. It grows on sunlight and the nutrients already in seawater.
- It can be eaten as it is, turned into gelling agents, stabilisers and antioxidants, used as animal feed, or made into bio-based materials.
- Denmark's offshore wind expansion will give large new areas of the North Sea over to energy by 2030. The researchers argue the empty space between turbines is too big to leave unused.
- The obstacle is **the market, not the science**. Bruhn wants food manufacturers, retailers, meal-kit companies and canteens to create demand. She compares it to legumes such as beans and lentils, which went from marginal to mainstream in European diets in roughly a decade.
