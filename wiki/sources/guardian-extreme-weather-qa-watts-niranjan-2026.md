# "The obsession with endless growth can only end in tears": your questions on extreme weather answered

**Source:** The Guardian, Ajit Niranjan and Jonathan Watts, 3 August 2026. https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/aug/03/the-obsession-with-endless-growth-can-only-end-in-tears-your-questions-on-extreme-weather-this-summer-answered

## What it is

A reader Q&A, in the style of a Reddit "ask me anything", with two Guardian environment writers. Ajit Niranjan covered Europe's heat and wildfires this summer, and Jonathan Watts covered floods in Asia.

They answer questions on how many people wildfires and heatwaves kill, how scientists link extreme weather to climate change, degrowth economics, climate anxiety, and reasons for hope.

## Why it matters

The piece brings together, in one place, questions many people are quietly asking after a hard summer. It adds sourced figures on deaths from wildfire smoke and from heatwaves linked to warming, which the wiki did not have before.

It also gives space to the worry many readers feel. The two writers take different views on what one person can do, and both are worth hearing.

## Key facts

- Wildfires kill relatively few people directly: hundreds a year worldwide, compared with thousands from floods and hundreds of thousands from heatwaves.
- Wildfire smoke is a different story. It is estimated to cause **1.5 million deaths a year** worldwide.
- The 2023 Canadian wildfire season alone was linked to roughly 80,000 smoke-related deaths, 20,000 of them in Europe.
- Rapid attribution studies have found that 40-70% of the death toll from major recent European heatwaves can be put down to human-caused warming.
- Linking floods and wildfires to climate change is harder. Even so, the rainfall behind some European floods has been shown to be up to 20% stronger or more likely because of it.
- Meat and dairy are responsible for 12-20% of planet-heating emissions.

## Growth and economics

Watts calls the "obsession with endless GDP growth on a finite planet" unsustainable. He points readers to Kate Raworth's *Doughnut Economics*, Bill McKibben's *Economics as if the World Mattered* and the degrowth work of Jason Hickel. In his words, the world needs "not just economic degrowth but ecological regrowth": an economy that rewards restoring damaged ecosystems.

He also questions mainstream climate economics. Drawing on the commentator Genevieve Guenther, he criticises the Nobel laureate William Nordhaus for assuming climate change will only dent GDP slightly. That estimate leaves out the full costs to labour productivity, infrastructure, insurance and property away from the coast. Most economic models also assume warming will rise smoothly and steadily, even though scientists warn of tipping points.

## Reasons for hope

Watts names four. The energy transition, especially in China. The growing movement to give legal rights to nature. Other ways of thinking from the Global South and Indigenous communities. And the prospect of the world's population peaking before 2050.

## What one person can do

The two writers lean different ways here. Niranjan argues that people have more say than they realise, through what they eat, how they travel and what they buy.

Watts puts political and system-wide change first: taxes on fossil fuels, taxes on wealth and investment in renewables. He notes that oil companies have long promoted the idea of a personal "carbon footprint" to shift the blame away from themselves.
