# Nitrogen-doped carbon materials for lower-energy carbon capture (Chiba University)

**Source:** SciTechDaily, 30 April 2026. https://scitechdaily.com/scientists-just-made-carbon-capture-much-cheaper-and-easier/

The underlying peer-reviewed study is Kondo K. et al., "Viciazites: Carbon materials with adjacent nitrogen functionalities for advanced CO2 capture", *Carbon* (2026). DOI: 10.1016/j.carbon.2026.121405

## What it is

A team at Chiba University in Japan, led by Associate Professor Yasuhiro Yamada and Associate Professor Tomonori Ohba, has developed a new kind of solid material for capturing CO2. They call it **viciazites**. It is a carbon backbone with nitrogen-containing groups (mostly –NH2, a type of amine) placed deliberately side by side.

In tests, the material gave up most of its captured CO2 at temperatures below 60°C. Today's main capture systems need more than 100°C to do the same. A lower temperature means the material could be reset using spare heat from factories, rather than heat produced on purpose, which would cut running costs a lot.

## Why it matters

Running costs are one of the biggest reasons carbon capture and storage (CCS) is not used more widely. The [carbon-capture-will-fix-it myth](../myths/carbon-capture-will-fix-it.md) page notes that only 45 CCS sites run worldwide and that 80% of US projects have failed, mostly on cost and on the technology not being ready.

A material that captures CO2 as well as today's systems but can be reset at about half the temperature could change those sums. That matters most for industries such as cement and steel, where emissions are hard to avoid by other means.

## Key facts

- The main capture technology today, aqueous amine scrubbing, has to heat large volumes of liquid to above 100°C to release the captured CO2. This heating is the main reason it is expensive.
- Viciazites are solid carbon fibres with amine groups placed next to each other on purpose.
- They are made by taking a compound called coronene, adding bromine, then exposing it to ammonia gas. The method puts the –NH2 groups side by side 76% of the time.
- Two other versions were also made: one with adjacent pyrrolic nitrogen (82% selectivity) and one with adjacent pyridinic nitrogen (60% selectivity).
- The groups were applied to activated carbon fibres to make a usable capture material. The –NH2 and pyrrolic versions captured more CO2 than untreated fibres. The pyridinic version showed little improvement.
- Most of the captured CO2 was released below 60°C, so waste heat could be enough to reset the material.
- The pyrrolic version needs higher temperatures but may last longer, because it is chemically more stable.

## Voices in the piece

Dr Yasuhiro Yamada, one of the team leaders, sums up the result: "Performance evaluation revealed that in carbon materials where NH2 groups are introduced adjacently, most of the adsorbed CO2 desorbs at temperatures below 60°C. By combining this property with industrial waste heat, it may be possible to achieve efficient CO2 capture processes with substantially reduced operating costs."

## Limits of the evidence

This is a lab-scale result in a peer-reviewed journal. The next steps have not happened yet: making the material in industrial quantities, testing it at real cement or steel plants, and checking the costs at scale. Materials research at this stage often takes 5-10 years to reach real use. It is a promising signal, not a solved problem, and the researchers themselves say only that lower costs "may be possible".
