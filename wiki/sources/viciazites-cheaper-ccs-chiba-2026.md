# Nitrogen-doped carbon materials for lower-energy carbon capture — Chiba University

**Source:** SciTechDaily, 30 April 2026 — https://scitechdaily.com/scientists-just-made-carbon-capture-much-cheaper-and-easier/
**Peer-reviewed study:** *Carbon* (2026), Kondo K. et al., "Viciazites: Carbon materials with adjacent nitrogen functionalities for advanced CO2 capture" — DOI: 10.1016/j.carbon.2026.121405

## What it is

Announcement of a new class of solid **carbon adsorbents called "viciazites"**, developed by a team at **Chiba University, Japan**, led by **Associate Professor Yasuhiro Yamada** and **Associate Professor Tomonori Ohba**. The materials are designed to carry **adjacent nitrogen-containing groups (mostly –NH2, primary amine)** in controlled positions on a carbon backbone. Testing shows they release captured CO2 at **temperatures below 60°C** — much lower than the 100°C+ needed to reset a conventional aqueous amine scrubbing system. Lower desorption temperature means the materials can potentially run on **industrial waste heat** rather than active heating, substantially reducing operating costs.

## Key facts

- Current dominant carbon capture technology (**aqueous amine scrubbing**) requires heating large volumes of liquid to **above 100°C** to release captured CO2 — the main driver of high operating cost
- **Viciazites** — solid carbon fibres with amine groups controlled to sit adjacent to each other
- **Synthesis:** coronene compound → bromination → ammonia gas exposure. 76% selectivity for adjacent –NH2 positions
- Two additional variants: adjacent pyrrolic nitrogen (82% selectivity); adjacent pyridinic nitrogen (60% selectivity)
- Applied to **activated carbon fibres** to create usable adsorbents
- CO2 uptake performance: adjacent –NH2 and adjacent pyrrolic groups showed improved uptake vs untreated fibres; adjacent pyridinic showed little improvement
- **Desorption:** most captured CO2 released at temperatures below 60°C — enabling waste-heat-driven regeneration
- Pyrrolic variant needs higher temperatures but may be more durable long-term due to stronger chemical stability

## Notable quote

**Dr Yasuhiro Yamada:** *"Performance evaluation revealed that in carbon materials where NH2 groups are introduced adjacently, most of the adsorbed CO2 desorbs at temperatures below 60°C. By combining this property with industrial waste heat, it may be possible to achieve efficient CO2 capture processes with substantially reduced operating costs."*

## Why it matters

The **operating cost of CCS is currently one of the biggest reasons it isn't deployed widely** — the [carbon-capture-will-fix-it myth](../myths/carbon-capture-will-fix-it.md) documents that only 45 CCS sites are running globally and 80% of US projects have failed, mostly on cost and technology readiness. A material that captures at similar effectiveness but regenerates at **half the temperature** could genuinely change the CCS economic case, particularly for **hard-to-abate industrial applications** like cement and steel.

**Caveat:** this is a lab-scale demonstration in a peer-reviewed journal. The next steps (scale-up to industrial adsorbent quantities, real-world validation at cement or steel plants, cost validation at scale) are all yet to happen. Materials science research at this stage often takes 5-10 years to reach deployment. This is a promising signal, not a solved problem.
