# Women farmers are challenging Ireland's farming model

**Source:** The Journal (Ireland), opinion by Dr Pippa Hackett, November 2026. https://www.thejournal.ie/readme/farming-in-ireland-7207505-Nov2026/

## What is it

An opinion piece by **Pippa Hackett**, a former Green Party Minister of State at the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine, who runs a farming and bloodstock business with her husband in Co Offaly. She argues that modern Irish farming was built on growth (more inputs, more output, more profit) and on control and fear: fear of trying something different, of what the neighbours will think, of weeds, of the inspector.

In her view, the profits mostly go further up the supply chain, while the farmer at the bottom is squeezed and left exposed to climate shocks, volatile markets and rising input costs. She sees the dairy sector, the most profitable, as perhaps the most vulnerable, because the model that worked 10 or 15 years ago no longer holds: rye grass struggles in a changing climate and fertiliser is no longer cheap.

## Why it matters

Hackett links the well-documented decline of Irish habitats, biodiversity, water, air and soil to this farming system, and to farmers who have little room to change it. Hackett's argument is that change will come less from rules than from farmers who choose a different path: going organic, adopting regenerative practices or selling directly to customers. Many of the people she meets taking that path are women.

This is an opinion piece, written by a politician with a clear position, so it is best read as one voice in the debate rather than as evidence.

## Key facts

- The UN has named 2026 the International Year of the Woman Farmer. Hackett spoke on a panel about women in farming at the Burren Winterage Festival.
- She contrasts the "table thumping" of male-dominated farm organisation meetings with women-led discussions focused on solving problems on their own farms.
- She quotes Mary Robinson: "climate change is a man-made problem that requires a feminist solution".
- Women in Irish farming have long been invisible: they could only pay contributory pensions on their farm work from 2014, and they are often passed over as successors to the family farm.
- She names Hannah Quinn Mulligan and Darina Allen among the women challenging the system.
- Agriculture is about 12,000 years old, she notes, yet policy still follows a 50 to 60 year old model dating from the Green Revolution.
