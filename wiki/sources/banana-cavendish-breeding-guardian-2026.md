# Have scientists found a way to save the banana from disaster? (Guardian, Nicola Davis)

**Source:** Guardian, Nicola Davis, 22 October 2026. https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2026/oct/22/have-scientists-finally-found-a-way-to-save-the-banana-from-disaster

## What it is

A Guardian explainer on the race to save the Cavendish banana, the one in almost every shop, from a fungal disease that is spreading around the world.

The news hook is Yelloway, a research partnership between Chiquita (the world's largest banana company) and the crop-improvement firm KeyGene. In October 2026 it announced a **fully fertile Cavendish line**, nicknamed "Yellowdish". Because it can produce seeds, it can be cross-bred with other bananas to create new varieties that resist disease and still sell well.

The piece sets this next to two other research programmes. Prof James Dale's team at Queensland University of Technology in Australia has made a GM Cavendish called QCAV-4, which carries resistance to Tropical Race 4 taken from a wild banana. It is approved for people to eat in Australia and New Zealand. Dr Jose De Vega at the Earlham Institute in Norwich is using gene editing to tweak the Cavendish's own DNA so it matches resistant relatives.

## Why it matters

Bananas feed a lot of people and support a lot of farmers, and almost all the bananas traded around the world are one genetically identical plant. That makes them a clear example of how farming can adapt to new threats, and of the trade-offs involved. The three approaches (cross-breeding, GM and gene editing) each raise different questions about regulation, biodiversity and whether shoppers will accept the result.

Chiquita's Peter Stedman makes the deeper point. An improved Cavendish is still a monoculture, a single crop variety grown everywhere, and it stays exposed to the next disease. Real resilience means growing several commercial varieties. The same tension between diversity and efficiency runs through much of farming's response to a changing climate.

## Key facts

- The world produces about **139 million tonnes of bananas a year**. Among fruits, only tomatoes are eaten more. There are more than 1,000 varieties, but the Cavendish makes up almost all commercial bananas sold.
- The Cavendish is itself a survivor. It replaced the Gros Michel variety, which Panama disease wiped out in the 1950s.
- The current threat is Tropical Race 4 (TR4), a strain of Panama disease. It has spread from South-East Asia to Australia, Africa, the Middle East and Latin America. The most worrying recent outbreak was in Ecuador, the world's largest banana exporter, in December 2025.
- Cavendish bananas are sterile and seedless. They are triploid, meaning they have three sets of chromosomes. New plants are grown from offshoots, so they are almost genetically identical. That gives uniform fruit, but it also means one disease can hit the whole crop.
- Yelloway's fully fertile Cavendish ("Yellowdish") can be cross-bred without GM. How it was produced has not been fully disclosed.
- Queensland's QCAV-4 carries a resistance gene from a wild banana, introduced with the help of a bacterium. It is approved for eating in Australia and New Zealand, and researchers say it tastes like a normal banana.
- The Earlham Institute sequences banana varieties that respond differently to TR4, looks for immunity genes the Cavendish lacks or carries in a weak form, and then edits the Cavendish's own copy. In England and the EU, gene editing is treated as "precision breeding", not GM.

## Voices in the piece

Peter Stedman, Chiquita's sustainability director, says the aim is to move on from the Cavendish altogether: "We are not trying to preserve Cavendish. We're trying to replace Cavendish because we believe at the core of any sustainable farming system is biodiversity and genetic diversity." He is sceptical of GM and editing for the same reason: "Every time you are editing or improving Cavendish, you're not creating a new variety, you're not creating biodiversity, you're just maintaining a monocrop."

Dr Jose De Vega of the Earlham Institute points to the alarm over Ecuador: "The most worrying recent report came in December 2025 from Ecuador, the world's largest banana exporter." He explains his method simply: "Once we identify a candidate gene, the idea is to edit the Cavendish's own copy so it matches the copy in the resistant cousin. That keeps everything that makes a Cavendish a Cavendish."

Prof James Dale of QUT thinks Yelloway's cross-breeding could help with regulators and public opinion, but notes that "whatever they generate is going to be different to Cavendish". Any new variety, he says, has to resist disease, yield well and taste good.

## Limits of the story

Yellowdish was only announced in October 2026, and the details are not public. Getting from a fertile line to bananas in shops will take years. Taste is an open question: QCAV-4 passed that test, but cross-bred varieties may be quite different from the Cavendish people know. Rules also vary. The EU and UK treat gene editing as "precision breeding", while some countries treat it as GM.
