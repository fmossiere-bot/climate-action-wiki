# Why a record-hot ocean is supercharging the El Niño effect (Forbes, July 2026)

**Source:** Forbes contributor column, Ingmar Rentzhog (CEO and founder of We Don't Have Time), 9 July 2026. https://www.forbes.com/sites/we-dont-have-time/2026/07/09/why-a-record-hot-ocean-is-supercharging-the-el-nio-effect/

## What it is

A long Forbes column arguing that **the ocean is the climate story most people are missing**. Ingmar Rentzhog brings together several things happening in summer 2026.

A strengthening El Niño, the natural warming of the tropical Pacific that shifts weather worldwide, is building over the hottest oceans ever recorded. Earlier in the year, marine heatwaves touched about 82% of the global ocean. Coral reefs went through the fourth global bleaching event. A major US ocean monitoring network was nearly switched off, then rescued. The High Seas Treaty came into force. And cleaner shipping fuel removed pollution that had been shading the ocean, which makes cutting methane quickly all the more urgent.

## Why it matters

The ocean soaks up most of the extra heat we add to the planet, so it is where the warming shows most clearly. This column links pieces of the story that sit on separate wiki pages, such as the risk of [AMOC collapse and Southern Ocean carbon release](../climate-science/amoc-collapse-carbon.md), [glacier loss](../biodiversity-land/Glaciers%20in%20Retreat%20-%20%20Facts,%20Risks,%20and%20What%20Can%20Be%20Done.md), [ocean-based climate solutions](../biodiversity-land/Ocean%20Based%20Climate%20Solutions.md) and the [UN world ocean assessment](un-world-ocean-assessment-2026.md). It ties them to a live event, the 2026 El Niño, which will shape weather and fishing news for the next 12 to 18 months.

It also shows how fragile the science can be. The near-loss of the US Ocean Observatories Initiative echoes the [cuts to AMOC monitoring](amoc-monitoring-guardian-2026.md) reported in June 2026. Without these sensors, it gets harder to see what the ocean is doing.

## Key facts

**The heat**

- The ocean covers about 71% of the planet and has **absorbed more than 90% of the excess heat** from global warming.
- Ocean heat content rose for the ninth record year running in 2025 (Inside Climate News). Kevin Trenberth (NCAR) says the sea takes in heat equal to roughly 12 Hiroshima bombs every second.
- On 21 June 2026, global sea surface temperature reached 21.0°C, a record for the time of year, beating 2023 and 2024 (Copernicus Marine data).
- In the first half of 2026, marine heatwaves touched about 82% of the global ocean at some point. The Mediterranean was worst hit, with strong to extreme heat over about 80% of it.

**El Niño**

- In June 2026 NOAA declared El Niño under way. It gave a 63% chance of a very strong event (more than 2.0°C above normal in the tropical Pacific) at the November–January peak, which would be one of the biggest since records began in 1950.
- The WMO's July outlook expects a wetter southern Europe and south-western US; a drier Australia, India, Horn of Africa, Central America and Caribbean; stronger hurricanes in the central and eastern Pacific; and a quieter Atlantic season.
- A Nature Communications study (2024) found that deep-ocean warming alone could make extreme El Niños 40–80% more common, and that the effect lasts after emissions stop.
- Carlo Buontempo of the Copernicus Climate Change Service calls the situation "uncharted territory". No El Niño has ever formed on an ocean this hot.

**Coral and the warming path**

- NOAA's Coral Reef Watch declared the fourth global mass bleaching event, which hit about 84% of the world's reefs across all three oceans from 2023 to mid-2025. A study in Coral Reefs found heat stress ran without a break from 2018–2025.
- The IPCC expects 70–90% of warm-water corals to be lost at 1.5°C of warming, and more than 99% at 2°C. Warming reached 1.37°C in 2025.
- The UN's 2025 Emissions Gap Report puts the world on track for about 2.8°C this century, and 3–4°C or more if fossil fuel expansion continues.

**Politics and monitoring**

- The High Seas Treaty came into force on 17 January 2026. It is the first binding deal covering the two-thirds of the ocean that lies beyond national borders, with 80+ signatories.
- The US Ocean Observatories Initiative has about 900 deep-sea sensors in a $386m network. The National Science Foundation ordered it shut down in May 2026, then reversed the decision in June after public outcry and a rare cross-party revolt in Congress.

**Shipping pollution and quick wins**

- A 2020 shipping rule cut the sulphur in ship fuel by about 80%. NASA saw the bright "ship tracks" over major shipping lanes drop sharply. Some studies suggest losing that reflective pollution may have helped speed up the ocean warming behind the 2023 records.
- The IPCC estimates that sulphate pollution has hidden roughly 0.4°C of warming.
- According to the IEA, clean energy now attracts almost twice as much investment each year as fossil fuels.
- Cutting methane and other short-lived climate pollutants could avoid up to about 0.5°C of warming by mid-century.

**What the ocean is worth**

- The OECD says the ocean supports more than 3% of world GDP and feeds about 3 billion people.
- The High Level Panel for a Sustainable Ocean Economy warns that the current path could cost more than $8 trillion by 2050. A finding linked to the World Resources Institute says every $1 put into healthy-ocean projects returns at least $5.

## Voices in the piece

Kevin Trenberth, a climate scientist at NCAR, calls ocean heat "the clearest single sign the planet is warming: unlike the air, it barely wobbles. It just keeps rising." Carlo Buontempo, who heads the Copernicus Climate Change Service, describes the situation as "uncharted territory." Helen Findlay of Plymouth Marine Laboratory warned that without the US sensor network, scientists would have to "navigate an increasingly volatile ocean with diminishing visibility."

## Limits of the source

This is an opinion column by a climate campaigner. Rentzhog is open about running We Don't Have Time, and the piece ends with calls to act: phase out fossil fuels, cut methane, protect blue carbon habitats such as mangroves and seagrass, and fund ocean monitoring. The technical claims trace back to named sources (NOAA, WMO, Copernicus, the IPCC and peer-reviewed studies), but lines such as "the sea has been paying our debts quietly" are his own framing.
