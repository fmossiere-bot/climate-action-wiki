# IPBES Nexus Assessment: Interlinkages among Biodiversity, Water, Food and Health (Summary for Policymakers, 2024)

## What it is

The **IPBES Nexus Assessment** is the first major UN scientific assessment to look directly at how five global crises affect each other: biodiversity loss, water availability and quality, food insecurity, health risks and climate change. IPBES is the UN's science panel for nature, the biodiversity equivalent of the IPCC.

It was written by 165 selected experts, plus 70 contributing authors, from every region of the world. They drew on 6,500 publications as well as Indigenous and local knowledge. Governments approved it at IPBES Plenary 11 in Windhoek, Namibia, on 10-16 December 2024. The co-chairs were Pamela McElwee (USA), Paula Harrison (UK) and David Obura (Kenya).

Its main finding is that tackling biodiversity, water, food, health and climate separately is failing on every front. Only joined-up ("nexus") decisions can deliver the Sustainable Development Goals, the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework and the Paris Agreement together.

## Why it matters

We tend to file climate, nature, food and health under different ministries, budgets and news sections. This report says they are one problem seen from five angles. A farm policy changes water use, which changes wildlife, which changes disease risk and diets. It is likely to shape how governments, funders and campaigners think about "transformative change" for the next decade.

## How the crises have interacted so far

Over the past 30-50 years, every biodiversity indicator assessed has fallen by between 2 and 6 per cent per decade. Global economic, population and trade trends have made things worse: 10 of 12 indicators of these indirect drivers have risen since 2001. They have intensified the five direct drivers of nature loss: changes in land and sea use, overexploitation, climate change, pollution and invasive alien species.

Freshwater life is declining fastest. The causes are heavy water withdrawal (farming alone accounts for about **80% of humanity's water demand**), damaged wetlands and forest loss. Coral reefs matter to nearly 1 billion people living within 100 km of them, and about one third of reef-building species are already at high risk of extinction. Food production has grown, but at a cost to nature, water and climate. 42% of the world's population could not afford a healthy diet in 2021, and about 3 million deaths in 2017 were linked to diets low in whole grains.

## What the future could look like

If current trends continue, every one of the five areas gets worse. The cost of dealing with biodiversity loss would **double if action is delayed by 10 years** (from 2021 to 2030), and delaying climate action adds at least $500 billion a year.

Plans that focus on one area only, such as biodiversity alone, food alone or climate alone, fall short because they ignore the trade-offs between them. Better outcomes need combined action: sustainable healthy diets, joined-up management of land and sea, nature protection across land, freshwater and ocean, and less pollution in all three.

## What can be done

The authors assessed **71 response options** in 10 categories: conserve, restore, manage, consume sustainably, reduce pollution and waste, integrate planning, manage risk, ensure rights and equity, align financing, and other. Many are cheap, such as agroecology (farming that works with natural processes), ecological intensification, health impact assessments and integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (farming several species together so one's waste feeds another).

Some of the measures that help nature most were not designed for nature at all. Sustainable healthy diets, rights-based approaches and Indigenous food systems often do more for biodiversity than biodiversity-only measures. Taken together, the 71 options would support all 17 SDGs, all 23 Kunming-Montreal targets and the Paris Agreement's long-term goals on cutting emissions and adapting at the same time.

## Governance and the money gap

Today's governance is split into silos and focused on the short term, which undermines the very goals it is meant to serve. The world's economic and financial systems put **$7 trillion a year** into activities that harm nature and the other nexus areas.

Against that, the biodiversity finance gap alone is $0.3-1 trillion a year. Meeting the SDG targets on water, food, health and climate needs at least $4 trillion a year in extra investment. The report calls for decision-making that is more joined-up, inclusive, fair, accountable and adaptable. It also calls for measures of progress beyond GDP, a real say for Indigenous peoples and local communities, and access to finance for developing countries, including tackling debt as a barrier to a fair transition.

## Summary findings

- $7 trillion a year is invested in activities that damage biodiversity and the other nexus areas.
- The biodiversity finance gap is $0.3-1 trillion a year, and related SDGs need at least $4 trillion a year more.
- 42% of the world's population could not afford a healthy diet in 2021, rising to 86% in low-income countries and 70% in lower-middle-income countries.
- 80% of humanity's water demand goes to food production.
- Every biodiversity indicator assessed has fallen by 2-6% per decade for 30-50 years.
- The cost of biodiversity action doubles if delayed 10 years, and delayed climate action adds at least $500bn a year.
- 71 response options were assessed. Implemented together, they support all 17 SDGs, all 23 GBF targets and the Paris Agreement.
