# Plant-based dog foods provide almost all the nutrients pets need: Brociek et al., PLOS One (2025)

**Source:** New Scientist, Christa Lesté-Lasserre, 3 September 2025 (updated 17 September 2025)

**Journal reference:** *PLOS One* (2025), Rebecca Brociek et al., DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0328506

## What it is

A peer-reviewed study of 25 commercial dry dog foods, 19 with meat and 6 plant-based, by Rebecca Brociek and David Gardner at the University of Nottingham. New Scientist reported on it.

The main result cuts both ways. **None of the 25 foods**, meat or plant, fully met official nutrition guidelines for dogs, even though all were sold as nutritionally complete. But the plant-based foods held up well against the meat ones on protein, fat and essential amino acids. They fell short in only two areas: iodine, which seaweed can easily supply, and vitamin B.

## Why it matters

Pets eat a lot of meat, and producing that meat has a climate cost. If plant-based food can keep dogs healthy, it gives owners a real way to cut that part of their household footprint.

Many people who avoid meat for ethical or climate reasons still feel uneasy feeding it to their dog. This study offers them peer-reviewed reassurance that plant-based dog food can meet a dog's needs, as long as iodine and vitamin B are topped up.

There is a wider consumer lesson too. Across the board, pet foods fell short of their own labels. "Nutritionally complete" means less than most buyers assume, whatever the food is made from.

## Key facts

- The study analysed 25 commercial dry dog foods: 19 with meat and 6 plant-based (2 vegetarian, 4 vegan).
- None of the 25 met all official nutrition guidelines, despite being labelled "nutritionally complete".
- All of them had acceptable levels of protein, fatty acids and essential amino acids.
- Most of the foods, including 5 of the 6 plant-based ones, were low in iodine. Seaweed could easily make up the gap.
- Plant-based foods were especially short of **vitamin B**, which dogs need for healthy skin, nerves and digestion. Supplements are needed.
- The European Pet Food Industry Federation and the Association of American Feed Control Officials require standard testing, but Gardner says most pet foods worldwide never go through independent nutrition testing.
- Published in PLOS One (2025), DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0328506.

## Voices in the piece

Rebecca Brociek, the lead author at Nottingham, says the source of the nutrients matters less than getting them: "As long as they're getting all of the essential nutrients from those ingredients, then the dogs are going to thrive." Her colleague David Gardner adds: "They're true omnivores. As most Labrador owners, myself included, will tell you, they'll eat anything."

Two researchers not involved in the study also comment. Andrew Knight of Murdoch University in Australia focuses on the labelling problem: "Consumers are expecting that if products are labelled as being nutritionally sound, that will be true, but clearly it's not true." Lucia Casini of the University of Pisa says: "I believe a vegetarian diet can be adequate if carefully supplemented with nutrients lacking in plants, as is the case for humans who choose a vegetarian diet." She advises owners not to make their own plant-based pet food.

## Limits of the study

The study covers dogs only. Cats are obligate carnivores with very different needs: they require taurine, arachidonic acid and ready-made vitamin A, among other things, usually from animal sources. These results should not be applied to cats without separate evidence.

The sample is 25 commercial brands, a snapshot rather than a full market survey, and New Scientist does not name the brands tested. Only dry kibble was tested, not wet food, raw diets or home-made meals. Casini's warning matters here: the good results apply to commercial foods with controlled supplements, not to owners mixing their own.
