# Plant-based dog foods provide almost all the nutrients pets need — Brociek et al., PLOS One (2025)

**Source:** New Scientist, Christa Lesté-Lasserre, 3 September 2025 (updated 17 September 2025)
**Journal reference:** *PLOS One* (2025), Rebecca Brociek et al., DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0328506

## What it is

A peer-reviewed analysis of **25 commercial dry dog foods** (19 meat-based, 6 plant-based) by **Rebecca Brociek and David Gardner at the University of Nottingham**, covered by New Scientist. **The headline finding: none of the 25 foods — meat or plant — fully met official canine nutritional guidelines, despite being packaged as nutritionally complete. But plant-based options compared well with meat-based ones on protein, fat and essential amino acids, with only two specific gaps: iodine (easily fixed with seaweed) and vitamin B.**

## Key facts

- **25 commercial dry dog foods analysed**: 19 with meat, 6 plant-based (2 vegetarian, 4 vegan)
- **None of the 25 foods met all official nutritional guidelines** despite "nutritionally complete" labelling
- **All foods had acceptable concentrations** of protein, fatty acids and essential amino acids
- **Most foods — including 5 of 6 plant-based ones — were low in iodine**, which seaweed could easily supplement
- **Vitamin B was particularly short in plant-based foods**; deficiency affects skin, nerves and digestive systems — supplementation needed
- **Governance context**: the European Pet Food Industry Federation and the Association of American Feed Control Officials require standardised testing, but **most pet foods worldwide do not go through independent nutritional testing**, per Gardner
- Published in **PLOS One (2025)**, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0328506

## Notable quotes

- **Rebecca Brociek** (Nottingham, lead author): *"As long as they're getting all of the essential nutrients from those ingredients, then the dogs are going to thrive."*
- **David Gardner** (Nottingham): *"They're true omnivores. As most Labrador owners — myself included — will tell you, they'll eat anything."*
- **Andrew Knight** (Murdoch University, Australia, external comment): *"Consumers are expecting that if products are labelled as being nutritionally sound, that will be true, but clearly it's not true."*
- **Lucia Casini** (University of Pisa, external comment): *"I believe a vegetarian diet can be adequate if carefully supplemented with nutrients lacking in plants — as is the case for humans who choose a vegetarian diet."* Owners should avoid making their own plant-based pet food.

## Why it matters

Three things:

1. **Pet food is a non-trivial slice of agricultural footprint.** Globally, pets consume meat at a scale that would make the pet-food industry a significant contributor to livestock emissions if measured as a country-equivalent. Plant-based options that keep dogs healthy are therefore a real climate lever, not a marginal one.
2. **The ethical case many vegan/vegetarian owners feel is reconciled.** Owners who avoid meat for ethical or climate reasons but feed meat to their pets are now backed by peer-reviewed evidence that plant-based alternatives can meet nutritional requirements — with the caveat that supplementation (iodine, vitamin B) is needed.
3. **A rare finding that applies equally to all pet food.** The paper's broader claim is that the pet-food category as a whole underperforms its own labelling. "Nutritionally complete" is a looser claim than consumers assume. That is a consumer-protection story as much as a climate one.

## Caveats

- **Dogs only.** The study did not cover cats, which are **obligate carnivores** and have very different nutritional requirements (cats require taurine, arachidonic acid, pre-formed vitamin A, among others, typically from animal sources). The findings should not be extrapolated to feline diets without separate evidence.
- **25-food sample.** The analysis is a snapshot of 25 commercial brands, not an exhaustive market review. Specific brands tested are not named in the New Scientist piece.
- **Dry kibbles only.** Wet food, raw-food and home-prepared diets were not covered.
- **Casini warns against home-prepared plant-based pet food** — the "balanced" claim rests on commercial preparations with controlled supplementation, not on owners doing it themselves.

## Tone and angle

Straight science reporting; three independent academic voices quoted (Brociek, Gardner, Knight, Casini, three universities on three continents). The piece is balanced about limits ("none met all official guidelines") while giving plant-based dog food a clean bill of nutritional health subject to supplementation.
