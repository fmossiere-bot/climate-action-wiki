# Big pharma accused of 'free-riding on nature': Rebecca Ratcliffe in the Guardian

**Source:** Guardian, Rebecca Ratcliffe, 22 October 2026. https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/oct/22/cop17-cali-conservation-fund-pharmaceutical-freeriding-nature-aoe
The fund's figures come from the UNDP Multi-Partner Trust Fund Office: https://mptf.undp.org/fund/cal00

## What it is

The Cali Fund is a UN biodiversity fund set up at COP16 in 2024. Its job is to collect money from companies that profit from nature's genetic information, much of which now sits in public databases as **digital sequence information (DSI)**. Drug makers are among the biggest users. The fund's target is $1 billion a year.

Some 18 months after its launch in February 2025, it holds $7,085 in deposits and $10,656 in pledges. That is roughly 0.0007% of one year's target. The Guardian reports this as COP17 gets under way, and finds that no major pharmaceutical company has paid anything.

Only three small firms have contributed: Prozomix (UK, $5,000), TierraViva AI (UK, $1,000) and E-Biom (Belgium, $1,085). Zero Carbon Analytics worked out that the 15 biggest drug companies could have paid about $756 million at the fund's suggested rate of 1% of profit or 0.1% of revenue. The dividends those same companies paid their shareholders in 2025 were 175 times that amount.

## Why it matters

The gap between $7,085 raised and a $1,000,000,000 target is a clear test of whether voluntary nature finance works. It lands at COP17 just as the way the world funds biodiversity is up for review. African countries, led by Egypt and Senegal, are pushing for contributions to be mandatory, and this result strengthens their case.

The mechanism itself is new. Under the Nagoya Protocol, companies that took physical samples of plants or animals from a country were meant to share the benefits. Once genetic data could simply be downloaded from a database, that rule could be sidestepped. The Cali Fund is the first attempt to close that gap, so how it is designed matters even if voluntary payments fail.

The dividend comparison also helps put the scale in perspective. Shareholders received 175 times what the fund asked for. It sits alongside the UN finding, covered in the Earthshot report, that for every £1 spent protecting nature, about £30 goes to activities that harm it.

## Key facts

- The Cali Fund's target is **$1 billion (£750m) a year**.
- As of 19 October 2026, it had $10,656 committed and $7,085 deposited, more than 18 months after its February 2025 launch.
- The only contributors so far are Prozomix (UK biotech, $5,000), TierraViva AI (UK, $1,000) and E-Biom (Belgium, $1,085). No major pharmaceutical company has paid in.
- Zero Carbon Analytics: the 15 biggest drug companies made about $161bn in profits in 2025. At the suggested rate (1% of profit or 0.1% of revenue) they could have paid $756m.
- Those 15 companies' shareholder dividends in 2025 were 175 times what they could have given the fund.
- At least 50% of Cali Fund money is set aside for Indigenous peoples and local communities.
- The wider context is a $700bn gap in global funding for nature.

## Voices in the piece

June Rubis, of Macquarie University, represents Indigenous peoples on the fund's steering committee. She says: "Look at how much profit these pharmaceutical companies are getting, and we're only asking for a tiny percentage. Definitely, they're free-riding" on nature.

Brian O'Donnell of Campaign for Nature notes that "More money has been spent on meetings talking about the Cali Fund than there is in the Cali Fund's coffers." He thinks companies should be required to pay rather than asked. Victoria Kalyvas of Zero Carbon Analytics argues that "The future of health is written in nature's DNA. The pharmaceutical industry stands to lose a great deal from its destruction, as do patients."

The industry pushes back. Guilherme Cintra of IFPMA, the drug makers' trade body, says the industry supports biodiversity but has "ongoing questions about the design and implementation". He argues the mechanism "overlaps with existing law, relies on inaccurate assumptions about how data is used in the discovery of medicines, and asks companies to hand over a share of their total revenue, whether DSI was used to develop a medicine or not".

GSK says it will decide on a contribution "when there is more information about how the Cali Fund will work alongside other national and multilateral access and benefit-sharing mechanisms". A Cali Fund spokesperson calls the result "disappointing but not entirely surprising", pointing out that similar funds took 2-3 years to grow.
