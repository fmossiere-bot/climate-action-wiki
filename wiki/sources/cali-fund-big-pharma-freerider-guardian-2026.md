# Big pharma accused of 'free-riding on nature' — Guardian, Rebecca Ratcliffe

**Source:** Guardian, Rebecca Ratcliffe, 22 October 2026 — https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/oct/22/cop17-cali-conservation-fund-pharmaceutical-freeriding-nature-aoe
**Primary data source:** UNDP Multi-Partner Trust Fund Office — https://mptf.undp.org/fund/cal00

## What it is

Guardian coverage of the **Cali Fund scandal at COP17**: a UN biodiversity fund set up at COP16 in 2024 to raise **$1 billion a year** from companies that profit from genetic resources (digital sequence information, DSI) sits at **$7,085 deposited and $10,656 committed** some 18 months after its February 2025 launch. **The specific finding: no major pharmaceutical company has contributed anything, despite the industry's reliance on genetic information for drug development.** Zero Carbon Analytics calculated that the 15 biggest pharma companies could have paid roughly **$756 million** at the fund's suggested rate (1% of profit or 0.1% of revenue); their shareholder dividends in 2025 were **175 times** that amount. The three contributions so far: Prozomix (UK, $5,000), TierraViva AI (UK, $1,000), E-Biom (Belgium, $1,085).

## Key facts

- **Cali Fund target: $1 billion (£750m) a year**
- **As of 19 October 2026: $10,656 committed, $7,085 deposited** — more than 18 months after the February 2025 launch
- **The three contributors so far**: Prozomix (UK biotech, $5,000); TierraViva AI (UK, $1,000); E-Biom (Belgium, $1,085). **No major pharmaceutical company has contributed**
- **Zero Carbon Analytics calculation**: 15 biggest pharma companies made ~$161bn in profits in 2025; at the Cali Fund's suggested rate (1% profit or 0.1% revenue) they could have paid **$756m**
- **Shareholder dividends from the same 15 companies in 2025 were 175× what they could have contributed to Cali**
- **At least 50%** of Cali Fund money is earmarked for Indigenous peoples and local communities
- Wider context: the **$700bn global nature funding gap**
- Industry objection (IFPMA, Guilherme Cintra): the mechanism "overlaps with existing law, relies on inaccurate assumptions about how data is used in the discovery of medicines, and asks companies to hand over a share of their total revenue, whether DSI was used to develop a medicine or not"

## Notable quotes

- **June Rubis** (Macquarie University, Indigenous rep on steering committee): *"Look at how much profit these pharmaceutical companies are getting — and we're only asking for a tiny percentage. Definitely, they're free-riding"* on nature
- **Brian O'Donnell** (Campaign for Nature): *"More money has been spent on meetings talking about the Cali Fund than there is in the Cali Fund's coffers."* Thinks companies should be compelled to pay, not just asked
- **Victoria Kalyvas** (Zero Carbon Analytics): *"The future of health is written in nature's DNA. The pharmaceutical industry stands to lose a great deal from its destruction, as do patients"*
- **Guilherme Cintra** (IFPMA, industry body): committed to biodiversity but has "ongoing questions about the design and implementation"
- **Cali Fund spokesperson**: result is *"disappointing but not entirely surprising"*; comparable instruments took 2-3 years to scale
- **GSK**: will decide on contribution "when there is more information about how the Cali Fund will work alongside other national and multilateral access and benefit-sharing mechanisms"

## Why it matters

Three things:

1. **A textbook voluntary-finance failure.** The ratio — $7,085 raised vs $1,000,000,000 target — is the sharpest single data point in the "voluntary biodiversity finance doesn't work" argument. It arrives at COP17 at precisely the moment the global biodiversity finance mechanism is up for review, strengthening the hand of African countries (led by Egypt and Senegal) pushing for mandatory contributions.
2. **The DSI mechanism itself is novel and worth watching.** Digital sequence information — storing genetic data from organisms in public databases — bypasses the physical-sample access-and-benefit-sharing rules that were the original Nagoya Protocol compromise. The Cali Fund is the first attempt to close that loophole. Its design will matter even if the voluntary contribution model fails.
3. **175× dividends vs potential contribution is a shareable fact.** When trying to explain the biodiversity-finance scandal to non-experts, that ratio is as evocative as the £30:£1 UN "degrading vs protecting" ratio from the Earthshot report.
