# Lofty ambitions: can puffer jackets be made more sustainable?

**Source:** The Guardian (Australia), Petra Stock, 25 July 2026. https://www.theguardian.com/australia-news/2026/jul/25/lofty-ambitions-can-puffer-jackets-be-made-more-sustainable

Part of the Guardian's "Change by degrees" series on household emissions.

## What it is

A practical guide to how sustainable puffer jackets are. It looks at what they are made of, how the different fillings (down, wool, synthetic and plant-based) compare for the climate and for animal welfare, and what you can do: buy less, buy secondhand, repair, and wear the jacket for years.

## Why it matters

Puffers show the fashion industry's wider problem in a single garment. New polyester is so cheap that recycling old jackets does not pay, so making more and throwing them away remains the norm. The EU's ban on destroying unsold clothes (see the [EU unsold clothes ban source](eu-unsold-clothes-ban-dw-2026.md)) tackles the retailer side of that story. This piece speaks to the rest of us, the people buying and wearing the jackets.

The most useful figure for anyone wanting to make a difference is about repair. Only about 1 in 10 items get mended, yet mending adds an average of **2.5 years** to a garment's life.

## Key facts

- Making the materials accounts for about 83% of a puffer jacket's carbon and water footprint ([2023 lifecycle study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/24723444231172216)).
- The average down jacket lasts 7 years. The next biggest part of its footprint is the water and energy used while it is worn and washed.
- About 100 million puffer jackets are made in China every year.
- An Adelaide University study of about 600 people found that 89% of garments were bought new. Padded jackets are among the most expensive things people own but rarely wear, so there is a good supply of secondhand puffers.
- Across Australia, about 53% of unwanted clothes end up in landfill, about 38% are reused or exported and about 9% are recycled.
- Australians buy 1.5 billion new items of clothing a year and send 220,000 tonnes to landfill ([Seamless environmental impact report 2024](https://www.seamlessaustralia.com/news/seamless-environmental-impact-report-2024)).
- Polyester puffers labelled "100% recycled" are often made from PET bottles or textile waste. Recycling textiles takes a lot of time, labour and infrastructure, and new polyester is so cheap that "it's cheaper just to make a whole new jacket than it is to recover and repurpose".

## What a puffer is made of

A puffer has an inner and outer shell, a filling, and small parts known as notions (zips, snaps and thread).

- **Shells** are usually nylon or polyester, both made from fossil fuels, sometimes blended with cotton or wool.
- **Fillings** are duck or goose feathers (down), wool or synthetic fibre. Some brands, such as Pangaia, are moving to a plant-based fluff made from wildflowers, biopolymers and aerogel.

## Animal welfare

Down is mostly a by-product of the meat industry, but about 1-2% is still thought to come from plucking live birds. The **Responsible Down Standard** requires higher welfare and bans live plucking.

For wool, the biggest welfare concern is mulesing: cutting skin from around a lamb's tail without anaesthetic. It is still legal in Australia, though banned in New Zealand and elsewhere. Look for ZQ Merino or the Responsible Wool Standard.

Polyester avoids these problems but comes from fossil fuels, and it sheds microplastics into rivers and seas with every wash.

## What you can do

- Look secondhand first. The supply is there because so many people buy more than they need.
- Buy good quality once, and look for repair services or take-back schemes, such as Upparel.
- Wash less, and wash cold. Heat damages synthetic fabrics, and airing a jacket out is often enough.
- Reapply a water-repellent finish rather than replacing the jacket.
