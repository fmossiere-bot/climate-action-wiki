# UBS Private Prisons complaint — Swiss NCP Follow-up Statement

**Source:** BankTrack News, August 2026 — https://www.banktrack.org/article/swiss_ncp_followup_statement_on_ubs_private_prisons_case_finds_two_of_three_recommendations_not_met_banktrack_response
**Original document:** [Swiss NCP Follow-up Statement on UBS Private Prisons Case (PDF)](https://www.seco.admin.ch/dam/en/sd-web/5otaY9I73zGK/Follow%20up%20Statement%20Swiss%20NCP%20UBS_coalition%20of%20NGO_forPublication.pdf)

## What it is

The **Swiss National Contact Point (NCP)** for Responsible Business Conduct has released its Follow-up Statement on a **two-and-a-half-year complaint** raised by **Worth Rises and BankTrack** against **UBS** for its investments in US private prison operators **CoreCivic and GEO Group**. The Follow-up assesses whether UBS acted on the recommendations in the NCP's Final Statement over the six months it was given. **The NCP found UBS only "partially addressed" two of three recommendations. Complainants argue that even "partial" overstates what happened — UBS took no new action.**

## Key facts

- Complaint filed by **Worth Rises** (a US private-prisons abolition organisation) and **BankTrack**
- Target investments: **CoreCivic and GEO Group** — two US private prison companies with well-documented human rights abuse allegations
- NCP's three recommendations to UBS:
  1. Include passive investments in overall risk screening for Responsible Business Conduct
  2. Actively advocate for a multi-stakeholder approach to consider RBC in passive investment
  3. (Third recommendation, described as met)
- Follow-up finding: UBS **"only partially addressed"** recommendations 1 and 2
- **UBS refused to engage in the mediation process** offered by the NCP — a first among European commercial banks
- UBS **continues to hold investments** in CoreCivic and GEO Group, despite never denying the human rights concerns
- **Parallel complaints against Barclays and HSBC** remain outstanding in the UK

## Notable quotes

- **Ryan Brightwell** (BankTrack human rights campaign lead): *"This is a textbook example of corporate impunity. The rules are clear, the risks are clear, and yet a major bank can simply refuse to act without facing any real consequence."*
- **Bianca Tylek** (Executive Director, Worth Rises): *"It's hard to have faith in the OECD process with so little recourse for violations of the organization's business ethics guidelines. UBS has done nothing to resolve our initial complaint, which was affirmed by the Swiss NCP, and yet the process wraps today."*

## Why it matters

Two broader points about the state of financial accountability:

1. **The NCP process has weak teeth.** Even when the NCP formally affirms a complaint, the bank can refuse mediation, refuse to act on recommendations, and face no sanctions. That's the specific failure this case illustrates: voluntary guidelines without enforcement mechanisms leave banks free to keep controversial investments while continuing to declare RBC commitments.
2. **Passive investment portfolios as an accountability gap.** Banks argue that passive holdings in index funds don't reflect active investment decisions, so shouldn't be subject to the same due diligence. The NCP explicitly rejected that argument — but if there are no sanctions, the principled ruling doesn't translate into changed behaviour.

While this specific case isn't about climate — it's about human rights in US private prisons — the pattern maps directly onto the climate accountability gap documented in [fossil fuel banking](../climate-finance/fossil-fuel-banking.md) and the [Ariana mine](../climate-finance/ariana-mine-santander.md) case. When voluntary frameworks (Net-Zero Banking Alliance, OECD Guidelines, TNFD) have no enforcement, banks can keep problematic investments while declaring compliance.

## Tone and angle

BankTrack advocacy piece with direct quotes from complainants. Underlying document (Swiss NCP Follow-up Statement) is available publicly. The framing is that this outcome demonstrates the need for legally binding human rights due diligence for banks — not stronger voluntary guidelines.
