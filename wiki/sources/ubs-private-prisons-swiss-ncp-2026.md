# UBS private prisons complaint: the Swiss NCP's follow-up statement

**Source:** BankTrack News, August 2026. https://www.banktrack.org/article/swiss_ncp_followup_statement_on_ubs_private_prisons_case_finds_two_of_three_recommendations_not_met_banktrack_response

**Original document:** [Swiss NCP Follow-up Statement on UBS Private Prisons Case (PDF)](https://www.seco.admin.ch/dam/en/sd-web/5otaY9I73zGK/Follow%20up%20Statement%20Swiss%20NCP%20UBS_coalition%20of%20NGO_forPublication.pdf)

## What it is

Under the OECD's guidelines for responsible business, each country has a National Contact Point (NCP) that handles complaints against companies. The Swiss NCP has published its follow-up statement on a complaint that ran for two and a half years. Worth Rises and BankTrack brought it against **UBS** over the bank's investments in two US private prison companies, CoreCivic and GEO Group.

The follow-up checks whether UBS acted on the NCP's recommendations in the six months it was given. The NCP found UBS had only "partially addressed" two of the three. The groups who complained say even "partial" is generous, because UBS took no new action at all.

## Why it matters

The case shows how weak this kind of process is. The NCP agreed with the complaint, yet the bank could refuse mediation, ignore the recommendations and face no penalty. Voluntary guidelines with no enforcement leave banks free to keep controversial investments while still claiming to behave responsibly.

It also exposes a gap around passive investments, the holdings a bank keeps through index funds that track a whole market. Banks argue these are not active choices, so they should not need the same checks. The NCP rejected that argument, but without sanctions its ruling has not changed what UBS does.

This case is about human rights in US prisons, not climate. But the pattern is the same one seen in [fossil fuel banking](../climate-finance/fossil-fuel-banking.md) and in the [Ariana mine](../climate-finance/ariana-mine-santander.md) case. When voluntary frameworks such as the Net-Zero Banking Alliance, the OECD Guidelines or TNFD have no enforcement, banks can keep problem investments while declaring that they comply. BankTrack draws the conclusion that banks need legally binding human rights checks, not stronger voluntary guidelines.

## Key facts

- The complaint was filed by Worth Rises, a US organisation campaigning to abolish private prisons, and BankTrack.
- It concerned UBS investments in CoreCivic and GEO Group, two US private prison companies facing well-documented allegations of human rights abuse.
- The NCP made three recommendations to UBS:
  1. Include passive investments in its overall risk screening for responsible business conduct.
  2. Actively push for a multi-stakeholder approach to responsible business conduct in passive investment.
  3. A third recommendation, described as met.
- In the follow-up, the NCP found UBS had "only partially addressed" recommendations 1 and 2.
- UBS **refused the mediation** offered by the NCP, a first among European commercial banks.
- UBS still holds investments in CoreCivic and GEO Group, and has never denied the human rights concerns.
- Similar complaints against Barclays and HSBC are still open in the UK.

## Voices in the piece

Ryan Brightwell, who leads BankTrack's human rights campaign, says: "This is a textbook example of corporate impunity. The rules are clear, the risks are clear, and yet a major bank can simply refuse to act without facing any real consequence."

Bianca Tylek, executive director of Worth Rises, questions the whole system: "It's hard to have faith in the OECD process with so little recourse for violations of the organization's business ethics guidelines. UBS has done nothing to resolve our initial complaint, which was affirmed by the Swiss NCP, and yet the process wraps today."
