# How to unlock unlimited geothermal energy, anywhere we want

**Source:** New Scientist, Katharine Sanderson, 21 July 2026 (updated 23 July 2026)

## What it is

A long New Scientist explainer on three new ways to get energy from the Earth's heat almost anywhere. Geothermal power has always depended on luck: it works well where hot rock sits close to the surface, as in Iceland, New Zealand, Kenya and Indonesia. The article argues that ten years of progress in drilling could free it from that limit.

The three routes are:

- **Enhanced geothermal systems (EGS)**, which crack open hot, solid rock so water can flow through it.
- **Advanced geothermal systems (AGS)**, a sealed loop of pipe that carries fluid down and back up without touching the rock.
- **Supercritical or "superhot rock" geothermal**, which drills deep enough to reach water so hot and pressurised it behaves as neither liquid nor gas.

## Why it matters

Geothermal power is low-carbon and can be switched on whenever it is needed. That makes it a good partner for wind and solar, which come and go with the weather. In principle the heat under our feet is almost limitless.

The three new routes tackle the geography problem in different ways, each with its own costs and risks. The Pohang earthquake in South Korea is the biggest warning. Iceland's slow-cooling method is the best answer so far.

If companies such as Quaise reach superhot rock more than 10 km down, drilling methods first developed for oil and gas could tap a resource around 150 times today's global electricity demand. It would be clean, available on demand and mostly local.

## Key facts

- The IEA estimates geothermal's technical potential at **around 150 times** current global electricity demand, if heat can be used down to about 8 km.
- Traditional geothermal plants only use heat from less than 2 km underground.
- Iceland now gets over 25% of its electricity from geothermal. The industry took off there after the 1970s oil crisis.
- EGS targets hot but solid rock 2–10 km deep, pumping water in at high pressure to open cracks. It is the most developed of the new methods.
- Eavor's closed-loop plant at Geretsried in Germany began construction in 2022 and started producing electricity in 2025.
- China Huaneng Group has built the first demonstration plant that uses supercritical CO2 instead of water to carry the heat, in Zhengzhou, running since May 2026.
- The Iceland Deep Drilling Project's second well (2017) reached 4.7 km. It was the first geothermal well in the world where supercritical conditions were confirmed. A third well is planned for next year.

## Drilling deeper

Water becomes supercritical at 374°C (705°F) and above, under enough pressure. Those conditions are usually found 5-20 km+ down.

Quaise Energy, based in Houston, Texas, plans to drill the upper part of a well in the usual way, then use high-powered millimetre waves for the deeper, harder rock. Nothing touches the rock: it is turned to ash. GA Drilling in Slovakia is testing laser and plasma systems, with first field tests in 2023.

Quaise wants its first superhot geothermal plant running in Oregon by 2030, reaching 400°C (752°F) at 3-5 km. Co-founder Matt Houde says: "by 2035, we want the first superhot well producing energy at a depth greater than 10 km".

## The serious risks

The biggest is earthquakes. In November 2017 an EGS project near Pohang, South Korea, set off a **magnitude-5.5 earthquake**. Over 80 people were injured and the damage came to about 300 billion Korean won (about £148 million). It was the most destructive earthquake in South Korea's history, and a government investigation concluded the EGS project caused it.

The mechanism is simple. Pumping water into deep rock raises the pressure inside it, which pushes the two sides of a fault slightly apart and reduces the friction holding them. If a fault is already close to slipping, that can be enough.

Iceland has a gentler approach. Rather than forcing the rock open all at once, engineers at HS Orka pump cold water in slowly, at low pressure, over months. The rock cools, shrinks and cracks on its own.

There are other problems too. Fluid leaking from EGS wells could contaminate groundwater. At supercritical temperatures, the steel lining of a well can expand, bend and fail, so Iceland's engineers are testing flexible joints between sections. Closed-loop systems may be safer, but rock debris can clog them (Eavor scaled back its planned wells), and it is not yet clear they can move fluid and draw heat fast enough to pay their way.

## Voices in the piece

Matt Houde, co-founder of Quaise Energy, is ambitious: "Ultimately, the whole world is the goal." He adds: "If you measure in terms of the energy stored within the Earth's crust, geothermal heat exceeds by orders of magnitude all hydrocarbons."

Iain Staffell of Imperial College London sees geothermal as "really well placed to be the glue that will make low-carbon electricity systems work."

Bill Ellsworth of Stanford explains that at Pohang the drilling linked two wells through faults that were ready to slip. On the deepest wells he is cautious: "We know that very deep geothermal energy would be fabulous if we could reach it. We just don't yet know if it's possible."

Sanjeev Kumar of the European Geothermal Energy Council puts the cost in perspective: "It's very, very costly. But once you've drilled that hole in the ground, that lasts for over 100 years."
