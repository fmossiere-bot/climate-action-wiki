# Extreme weather in 2025 drove record wildfire emissions in Europe

**Source:** New Scientist, Alec Luhn, 29 April 2026 (updated 15 May 2026)

## What it is

A New Scientist report by Alec Luhn on Europe's weather in 2025. Europe is the fastest-warming continent in the world, heating up twice as fast as the global average since 1980. In 2025 that showed up as record wildfires and heatwaves, including a three-week hot spell that reached 30°C inside the Arctic circle.

European wildfires released **47 million tonnes of carbon** in 2025, a record. Spain, the UK, the Netherlands, Germany and Cyprus each broke their own previous fire-emission records.

## Why it matters

For people in southern Europe, 2025 meant evacuations, closed roads and smoke. At least 3 people died in the fires in Portugal and Spain. Climate change made the hot, dry, windy conditions behind those fires at least 40 times more likely.

The pattern behind the fires is worth understanding. A wet spring made plants grow fast, then record summer heat dried them out, leaving a landscape ready to burn. Scientists expect this kind of swing, sometimes called "volatile weather", to get worse. Fires also feed back into the problem: the carbon they release adds to the warming that makes the next fire season worse.

## Key facts

- 2025 was the hottest year on record in the UK, Iceland and Norway, and one of the three hottest in Europe overall.
- More than 95% of Europe had above-average temperatures for the year.
- Scandinavia, Finland and north-western Russia had their worst heatwave ever: 21 days of temperatures reaching 30°C at the Arctic circle.
- Climate change made the August wildfires in Portugal and Spain at least **40 times more likely**.
- Fires came close to Madrid, sections of the Camino de Santiago pilgrimage route were shut, and smoke reached the UK.
- More than 10,000 sq km burned in Portugal and Spain, and at least 3 people were killed.
- Soils were the driest in 33 years of observations, and over a third of Europe suffered extreme agricultural drought.

## The seas, and some good news

The ocean around Europe set a record for annual sea surface temperature for the fourth year running. 86% of those seas went through strong, severe or extreme marine heatwaves. In the Mediterranean, 100% of the sea's area was hit by heatwaves for the third year in a row, and waters off Italy and Spain reached 30°C.

There was progress on the energy side. Solar produced a record 12.5% of Europe's electricity in 2025, and renewables as a whole reached 46%.
