# The environmental footprint of data centres in the United States

**Source:** Environmental Research Letters, Md Abu Bakar Siddik, Arman Shehabi and Landon Marston, 21 May 2021. https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/abfba1

## What it is

A research paper that estimates both the water footprint and the carbon footprint of data centres across the United States, one facility at a time. It was the first study to do this below the national level. The authors linked each data centre to the power plants that supply its electricity, the water utilities that supply its water, and the treatment plants that take its wastewater. The data are for 2018.

## Why it matters

Data centres are often discussed in terms of electricity alone. This study shows that where a data centre is built matters as much as how efficient it is. The same computing work can use far more water, or produce far more carbon, depending on the local grid and how dry the region is.

Most of the water a data centre uses is not the cooling water on site. It is the water used by the power stations that make its electricity. That makes the choice of location, and of energy supply, one of the biggest levers the industry has.

## Key facts

- In 2018, US data centres used about **1.8% of US electricity** and produced about 0.5% of total US greenhouse gas emissions.
- Their total yearly operational water footprint in 2018 was 5.13 × 10⁸ m³, or about 513 million cubic metres.
- Three-quarters of that water footprint is indirect, used in generating the electricity, rather than direct cooling water.
- 70% of the water scarcity footprint comes from data centres in the West and Southwest of the US. These regions host about 20% of servers but draw heavily on watersheds already under water stress.
- Hyperscale data centres (the very large ones run by big cloud companies) are about 6× more water-efficient per workload than smaller in-house or shared "colocation" centres: 2.10 against 12.15 m³ per workload.
- The carbon intensity of a data centre ranges from 0.02 to 1 tonne of CO₂-equivalent per MWh depending on where it is, a range of about 50×.
- Building future data centres in places with cleaner power and more water could cut the industry's water scarcity footprint by up to 90% and its carbon footprint by up to 55%.
