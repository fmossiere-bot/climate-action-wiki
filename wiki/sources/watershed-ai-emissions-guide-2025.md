# AI Emissions: A Practical Guide for Corporate Sustainability Leaders

**Source:** Watershed, Eric Nevalsky (Sustainability Advisor) and Steven Watson (PM, Product Footprints), 2025. https://watershed.com

## What it is

A 42-slide guide from Watershed, a company that sells carbon accounting software, written for sustainability managers in businesses. It aims to help them understand, measure and reduce the emissions that come from their organisation's use of AI.

It explains where AI emissions come from (mostly the electricity that powers the chips, not making them), four big forces shaping AI's climate impact, how to judge AI suppliers on sustainability, how to measure AI emissions when there is no agreed standard, and how AI can itself help cut emissions elsewhere.

## Why it matters

Many people now use AI tools every day at work, and companies are starting to ask what that does to their carbon footprint. The guide offers a sense of proportion. One query has a tiny footprint, smaller than you might fear. The bigger concern is growth: AI is getting more efficient per query, but total use is rising so fast that overall energy demand keeps climbing. Economists call this the **Jevons paradox**, when efficiency makes something cheaper and so people use more of it.

Where the computing happens matters a great deal. The same AI task can produce far less CO2 in a region whose electricity is mostly clean. Keep in mind that this is a guide from a company in the carbon accounting business, aimed at its own customers.

## Key facts

- Using AI models (called inference) accounts for **over 90%** of AI's lifetime emissions. Training the models accounts for less than 10%.
- AI emissions break down as about 80.9% from electricity for the chips (GPUs), about 17.8% from other data centre electricity, and about 1.3% from making the GPUs.
- A single query to a large language model uses about 0.24 Wh, roughly 0.1–0.3 g CO₂e on the average US grid. A heavy user running 100 queries a day produces about 3 kg CO₂e a year, less than the footprint of half a kilogram of beef.
- Google's Gemini cut the energy used per query 33 times over between 2024 and 2025, but total AI energy demand is expected to triple by 2030.
- In 2025, 1,891 energy projects (266 GW) were cancelled in the US in a single year, and 93% of them were clean energy.
- Goldman Sachs expects 60% of new data centre power this decade to come from natural gas rather than renewables.
- Choosing a data centre region with cleaner electricity can cut the emissions from running AI by 30–80% straight away.

## How to measure it

There is no globally accepted standard for measuring AI emissions yet. The guide ranks the data that matters most: the region or location where the computing runs (critical), which model is used (critical), and how many tokens, the chunks of text a model processes, are used (medium to high value).
