# How turning electricity directly into food could help save the planet — Michael Le Page, New Scientist, September 2026

**Source:** New Scientist, Michael Le Page (Columnist), 22 September 2026

## What it is

New Scientist commentary from Michael Le Page on **"solar foods" and "electro-agriculture"** — a cluster of emerging technologies that make food directly from renewable electricity, water, CO2 and nitrogen, rather than from sunlight and farmland. **The core argument: because plants capture less than 1% of the solar energy that reaches them, while solar panels convert more than 20%, we could grow around 10 times more food per hectare if we fed microbes (and eventually genetically-modified plants) with electricity-derived chemicals instead of relying on photosynthesis.** The piece reviews microbial-protein ("gas fermentation") companies like Finland's Solar Foods, acetate-based electro-ag research at UC Riverside and Copenhagen, and alternative routes like Savor's syngas-to-butter chemistry.

## Key facts

- **Plants capture less than 1% of solar energy** reaching a crop as edible food
- **Standard solar panels: >20% efficiency**; some experimental panels achieve over double this
- **Vertical farming is a non-starter**: every hectare of indoor growing area needs **~13 hectares of solar panels** to power lights
- **Solar Foods** (Finland): grows hydrogen-eating bacteria in vats on H2 + CO2 + ammonia, producing **Solein** (up to 80% protein); already approved in **US and Singapore**, awaiting EU approval (5+ years); second factory planned at **6,400 tonnes/year**; demonstrator **5× more productive** than expected (CEO Pasi Vainikka)
- **Norferm** (Norway, 1999-2005): produced **110,000 tonnes of microbial protein from methane** — proof that gas fermentation scales
- **Calysta**: 20,000 tonnes/year factory in China; **Unibio**: 50,000 tonnes/year plant in Saudi Arabia (both currently use fossil methane as feedstock)
- **UC Riverside (Robert Jinkerson, 2022):** solar → acetate → yeast is **~18× more efficient than standard farming**; nearly 4× for algae; a 2024 follow-up projects a **10× boost** with further improvements
- **Nolux**: Jinkerson spin-out working to genetically modify plants to grow on acetate in the dark
- **Acetate Consortium (U. Copenhagen, Remko Boom)**: focus on microbes already approved for food use (yeast) to avoid slow novel-food approvals
- **Savor** (US start-up): converts syngas (H2 + CO) into **edible fats / butter-like product**

## Notable quotes

- **Michael Le Page (framing):** "farming is the most destructive of all human activities, driving the two major crises we face, global heating and biodiversity loss. And yes, I do mean all farming, including organic."
- **Pasi Vainikka (Solar Foods CEO):** "It has scaled really well. It's almost five times more productive than we thought early on."
- **Remko Boom (U. Copenhagen, Acetate Consortium):** "Hydrogen hardly dissolves at all in water, so it is very difficult for microorganisms to get hold of." → the case for using acetate (highly soluble) instead
- **Boom on scalability:** "In principle, we can go to quite large scales. So, it could be a significant source of proteins over the world, and that would mean that we can decouple this type of food production from the use of land."
- **Boom:** "We don't expect a shortage of carbohydrates, while we do expect a shortage in protein."

## Why it matters

Three things:

1. **A proof-of-concept for decoupling food from land.** The Le Page/Jinkerson efficiency numbers (~18× for yeast, nearly 4× for algae) are not marginal gains. If a decent proportion of our food could be made this way, Boom's "decouple food production from land use" claim becomes genuinely plausible — and that is one of the single biggest levers for reducing agricultural pressure on biodiversity, water and climate.
2. **The gas-fermentation base case is already commercial.** Norferm proved the scale in 1999-2005; Calysta and Unibio are back doing it now at 20,000-50,000 tonne scale. The sticking point is that they are using **fossil methane**, which puts the fossil carbon back into the atmosphere. Switching to green hydrogen / biogas changes the climate maths — but costs more.
3. **Multiple approaches, no single winner.** Solar Foods (microbial protein from H2), the Acetate Consortium (microbes on acetate), Nolux (acetate-eating GM plants) and Savor (chemical synthesis of fats) are all live tracks. Boom: "We will probably not rely on one particular route."

## Caveats

- **No "replaces conventional farming anytime soon".** Le Page is explicit — this would require far more renewable electricity than we currently generate, and consumer acceptance of novel foods is uncertain.
- **Regulatory approval is slow.** Solar Foods has been waiting 5 years for EU approval of Solein. The Acetate Consortium chose to work with already-approved microbes (yeast) specifically to avoid that bottleneck.
- **Energy accounting matters.** Vertical farming shows what happens when you don't do the math (13 ha of solar per hectare of grow area). Solar-food claims need to be checked against the full electricity-to-calorie efficiency, not just the headline efficiency of one step.
- **Culture-medium footprint.** Plant-cell growing (chocolate etc.) currently uses sugars from conventionally-grown crops, so the environmental footprint is not yet zero.

## Tone and angle

New Scientist columnist piece — Le Page is personally advocating, flags that explicitly ("For me, there's one clear answer"), but packs the piece with concrete company names, specific efficiency numbers and the honest caveats about fossil-methane feedstock, regulatory delay and renewable-electricity volume. Written on New Scientist's 70th anniversary, framed as "the breakthrough I'm most looking forward to in the next 70 years".
