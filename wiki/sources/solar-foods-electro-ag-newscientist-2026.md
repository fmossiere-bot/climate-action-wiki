# How turning electricity directly into food could help save the planet: Michael Le Page, New Scientist, September 2026

**Source:** New Scientist, Michael Le Page (columnist), 22 September 2026

## What it is

A comment piece by New Scientist columnist Michael Le Page on "solar foods" and **electro-agriculture**. These are new technologies that make food from renewable electricity, water, CO2 and nitrogen, instead of from sunlight falling on fields.

His reasoning rests on efficiency. Plants turn less than 1% of the sunlight that reaches them into food, while solar panels turn more than 20% of it into electricity. If that electricity were used to make chemicals that feed microbes (and, one day, genetically modified plants), Le Page argues we could grow around 10 times more food per hectare than by relying on photosynthesis.

He looks at companies making protein from microbes fed on gas, such as Finland's Solar Foods, at research using acetate (a simple compound made from CO2 and electricity) at UC Riverside and in Copenhagen, and at other routes such as Savor's process for turning gas into butter. The column was written for New Scientist's 70th anniversary, as the breakthrough Le Page is most looking forward to in the next 70 years. He is open about being an advocate ("For me, there's one clear answer"), but backs the case with company names and figures.

## Why it matters

Farming takes up a huge share of the world's land, and that land is under pressure from both climate change and wildlife loss. If even a fair share of our food could be made without farmland, it would ease that pressure on nature, water and the climate. The efficiency figures here (about 18 times better than farming for yeast, nearly 4 times for algae) are big enough to make that idea worth taking seriously.

Part of this already works at industrial scale. Norferm proved it between 1999 and 2005, and Calysta and Unibio run plants of 20,000 to 50,000 tonnes a year today. The catch is that they feed their microbes **fossil methane**, which puts fossil carbon back into the air. Switching to green hydrogen or biogas would change the climate picture, but costs more.

No single method has won yet. Solar Foods (protein from hydrogen), the Acetate Consortium (microbes on acetate), Nolux (plants modified to grow on acetate) and Savor (fats made by chemistry) are all being developed. As Remko Boom puts it, "We will probably not rely on one particular route."

## Key facts

- Plants turn less than 1% of the sunlight reaching a crop into edible food.
- Standard solar panels are more than 20% efficient, and some experimental panels reach over double that.
- Vertical farming does not add up on energy: every hectare of indoor growing needs about **13 hectares of solar panels** to power the lights.
- Solar Foods (Finland) grows bacteria that feed on hydrogen, CO2 and ammonia in vats. The result, Solein, is up to 80% protein. It is approved in the US and Singapore and has waited more than 5 years for EU approval. A second factory is planned at 6,400 tonnes a year, and the demonstration plant is 5 times more productive than expected, according to CEO Pasi Vainikka.
- Norferm (Norway, 1999-2005) produced 110,000 tonnes of microbial protein from methane, showing that gas fermentation can work at scale.
- Calysta has a 20,000 tonnes a year factory in China, and Unibio a 50,000 tonnes a year plant in Saudi Arabia. Both currently use fossil methane.
- Research led by Robert Jinkerson at UC Riverside (2022) found that going from solar power to acetate to yeast is about 18 times more efficient than standard farming, and nearly 4 times for algae. A 2024 follow-up projects a further 10 times boost with improvements.

## The companies and research groups named

- **Nolux**: a spin-out from Jinkerson's lab, working on genetically modified plants that grow on acetate in the dark.
- **Acetate Consortium** (University of Copenhagen, Remko Boom): works with microbes already approved for food, such as yeast, to avoid slow approval for new foods.
- **Savor** (US start-up): turns syngas, a mix of hydrogen and carbon monoxide (H2 + CO), into edible fats and a butter-like product.
- Other microbial protein companies mentioned: Jooules, Air Protein and Aerbio.

## Voices in the piece

Le Page sets out his starting point plainly: "farming is the most destructive of all human activities, driving the two major crises we face, global heating and biodiversity loss. And yes, I do mean all farming, including organic."

Pasi Vainikka, CEO of Solar Foods, is pleased with how production has grown: "It has scaled really well. It's almost five times more productive than we thought early on."

Remko Boom of the University of Copenhagen explains why his team prefers acetate, which dissolves easily in water: "Hydrogen hardly dissolves at all in water, so it is very difficult for microorganisms to get hold of." He sees large potential: "In principle, we can go to quite large scales. So, it could be a significant source of proteins over the world, and that would mean that we can decouple this type of food production from the use of land." And he explains the focus on protein: "We don't expect a shortage of carbohydrates, while we do expect a shortage in protein."

## Limits of the figures

Le Page is clear that none of this will replace conventional farming any time soon. It would need far more renewable electricity than the world currently generates, and nobody knows how readily people will eat these new foods.

Approval is slow. Solar Foods has been waiting 5 years for EU approval of Solein, which is why the Acetate Consortium chose microbes that are already approved.

The energy sums matter. Vertical farming shows what happens when they are not done (13 ha of solar for every hectare of growing space), so claims about solar foods need checking across the whole chain from electricity to calories, rather than at a single efficient step. Growing plant cells, for products such as chocolate, also still relies on sugars from conventional crops, so its footprint is not yet zero.
