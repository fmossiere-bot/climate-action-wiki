# Shifts in the jet stream are behind Europe's long heatwaves

**Source:** New Scientist, Alec Luhn, 13 July 2026
**Peer-reviewed study:** *Environmental Research Letters* — DOI: 10.1088/1748-9326/ae7f34 (Duncan Pappert and Olivia Martius, University of Bern)

## What it is

A summary of new research from **Duncan Pappert and Olivia Martius at the University of Bern**, published in *Environmental Research Letters*, on the atmospheric patterns behind **Europe's long heatwaves — the ones that last two weeks or more**. Because there are only about **20 well-observed long heatwaves in the daily atmospheric record**, the team simulated **1,900 plausible hot spells with a state-of-the-art climate model** and looked for common patterns. They found that persistent European heat domes are driven by **two distinct types of jet stream configuration**, both involving **Rossby waves** — chains of weather systems that alter the polar jet stream around 60° north.

## Key facts cited

- **Rossby waves** — atmospheric wave chains that alter the polar jet stream at roughly 60° N — set up long-lasting European heat domes
- **Type I pattern**: jet stream becomes wavier, kinking north around the UK and Scandinavia and then veering south to form a shape like the Greek letter **omega**; a **persistent high-pressure ridge** forms within the omega, and Atlantic storms coming across the ocean **reinforce rather than break** the low-pressure cyclonic circulation at the beginning of the omega, pulling hot subtropical air northwards
- **Type II pattern**: jet stream shifts **polewards** in a straighter line, running north of the UK; a ridge forms over Europe, storm tracks are diverted north of the continent, and warm subtropical air floods in from the south
- **Both types** produce a high-pressure ridge over western Europe. At Earth's surface, air flows away from the ridge to lower-pressure areas; dry air descending to replace it is compressed and heats up, sunshine is unbroken, land dries and evaporation drops — creating a **positive feedback loop**
- **June 2026 European heatwave**: killed an estimated **20,000 people** in Europe and started as a Type II before morphing into a Type I; the current heatwave is largely Type I with a low-pressure trough to the west of a high-pressure ridge over Europe
- **Long heatwaves are deadlier per day** than short ones because bodies are under stress for longer, hydroelectric dams and agriculture come under prolonged pressure, and health services can be overwhelmed

## Notable quotes

- **Duncan Pappert** (University of Bern): *"The models can be improved if we understand these events better."* Also: *"It's always the ridge that's creating the heat — but how the atmosphere is setting up the situation for the ridge to be maintained, that's the difference."*
- **Tim Woollings** (University of Oxford): *"These patterns should help forecasters. This really clearly lays out the structure and evolution of these events, so people know what to look for."* On the trend: *"It is a concern that we don't know. We don't have a good enough understanding of these events really to know how their duration is changing."*
- Woollings also notes the jet stream is **shifting northwards in general**, which could bring drier conditions to southern Europe

## Caveats / open questions

- The two types are **averaged tendencies, not hard categories** — real events can shift between them
- Research is **inconclusive on the trend**: some studies find blocking patterns will become more frequent and more persistent over Europe under warming; other modelling shows no change
- Woollings: if Type I and II patterns did become more frequent or longer-lasting, "**it would be extremely damaging to economies and human health**"

## Why it matters

Two things distinguish this from previous heatwave coverage:

1. It gives forecasters and grid operators a **pattern-recognition tool** for anticipating long heatwaves earlier — enough advance warning matters, because prolonged heat is more deadly than a short spike, and because grid operators need lead time to prepare for air-conditioning demand
2. It sharpens the honest gap in the science: we know heatwaves are getting hotter, more frequent and longer-lasting due to rising background temperatures. We do not yet know whether the **specific jet-stream patterns that create long ones are getting more common**. That is exactly the kind of unresolved question that makes near-term adaptation planning difficult.

## Tone and angle

Straight climate-science reporting. New Scientist frames the paper as a **forecasting improvement** rather than an attribution study, and is careful to include both the trend uncertainty and the practical adaptation implication (grid operators, hydro dams, agriculture, air conditioning demand).
