# Shifts in the jet stream are behind Europe's long heatwaves

**Source:** New Scientist, Alec Luhn, 13 July 2026.

**Peer-reviewed study:** *Environmental Research Letters*, DOI: 10.1088/1748-9326/ae7f34 (Duncan Pappert and Olivia Martius, University of Bern).

## What it is

A New Scientist report on research by Duncan Pappert and Olivia Martius at the University of Bern, published in *Environmental Research Letters*. It looks at the weather patterns behind Europe's long heatwaves, the ones that last two weeks or more.

There are only about 20 well-observed long heatwaves in the daily weather record, too few to study on their own. So the team used a state-of-the-art climate model to simulate 1,900 realistic hot spells and looked for what they had in common. They found that lasting heat domes over Europe come from **two distinct jet stream patterns**. Both involve Rossby waves, chains of weather systems that bend the polar jet stream, the band of fast wind that circles the globe around 60° north.

## Why it matters

The findings give forecasters and grid operators a way to spot long heatwaves coming earlier. That warning time counts. Prolonged heat is more deadly than a short spike, and electricity grids need time to prepare for the rise in demand for air conditioning.

The research also shows plainly what scientists do not yet know. We know heatwaves are getting hotter, more frequent and longer because the background temperature is rising. We do not yet know whether the particular jet stream patterns that create long heatwaves are becoming more common. That uncertainty makes it harder for countries and cities to plan how to adapt over the next few years.

## Key facts

- Rossby waves, which bend the polar jet stream at roughly 60° N, set up long-lasting heat domes over Europe.
- **Type I pattern**: the jet stream becomes wavier. It kinks north around the UK and Scandinavia, then veers south, making a shape like the Greek letter omega. A lasting high-pressure ridge forms inside the omega. Atlantic storms arriving from the west strengthen the low-pressure swirl at the start of the omega instead of breaking it up, and that pulls hot subtropical air northwards.
- **Type II pattern**: the jet stream shifts towards the pole in a straighter line, running north of the UK. A ridge forms over Europe, storms are pushed north of the continent, and warm subtropical air floods in from the south.
- Both types produce a high-pressure ridge over western Europe. At ground level, air flows out from the ridge towards lower pressure. Dry air sinks to replace it, is squeezed and heats up. The sun shines without a break, the land dries out and less water evaporates to cool things down. Each step feeds the next.
- The June 2026 European heatwave killed an estimated **20,000 people** in Europe. It began as Type II and turned into Type I. The heatwave under way when the article was written is mostly Type I, with a low-pressure trough to the west of a high-pressure ridge over Europe.
- Long heatwaves are deadlier per day than short ones. Bodies are under strain for longer, hydroelectric dams and farms are under pressure for longer, and health services can be overwhelmed.

## Voices in the piece

Duncan Pappert, one of the two Bern researchers, hopes the work will feed into better forecasting: *"The models can be improved if we understand these events better."* He explains what separates the two types: *"It's always the ridge that's creating the heat, but how the atmosphere is setting up the situation for the ridge to be maintained, that's the difference."*

Tim Woollings, a climate scientist at the University of Oxford, sees a practical use: *"These patterns should help forecasters. This really clearly lays out the structure and evolution of these events, so people know what to look for."* He is frank about the gaps: *"It is a concern that we don't know. We don't have a good enough understanding of these events really to know how their duration is changing."* He also notes that the jet stream is shifting northwards in general, which could bring drier conditions to southern Europe.

## Limits of the findings

The two types are average tendencies rather than strict categories, and a real heatwave can move from one to the other. The research is also split on the trend. Some studies find these "blocking" patterns will become more frequent and last longer over Europe as the climate warms, while other modelling shows no change. Woollings warns that if Type I and II patterns did become more frequent or longer-lasting, "it would be extremely damaging to economies and human health".
