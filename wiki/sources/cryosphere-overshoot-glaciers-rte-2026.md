# 'Irreversible' damage to glaciers if 1.5C breached

**Source:** RTÉ News, Colman O'Sullivan, 2 November 2026. https://www.rte.ie/news/world/2026/1102/1597677-un-climate-ice/

## What is it

RTÉ reports on two scientific reports released just before COP31 about the **cryosphere**, the name for all the frozen parts of the Earth: mountain glaciers, ice sheets, sea ice, snow and permafrost (permanently frozen ground). The first, *Overshoot and the Cryosphere*, was led by glaciologist James Kirkham and written with 80 scientists. The second is *State of the Cryosphere 2026: The Cost of Overshoot*.

Both reach the same conclusion. Going past 1.5°C of warming "will bring large-scale and in many cases irreversible impacts" on the world's ice. One report puts it bluntly: "We cannot negotiate with the melting point of ice." Even if warming were held below 1.5°C, sea levels could rise by between two and nine metres over the long term.

## Why it matters

The world is already at about 1.37°C of warming, and Copernicus expects 1.5°C to be passed around 2030. The reports say today's level is already "unsafe" for the Arctic, Antarctic, mountain glaciers and polar oceans. A truly safe temperature for the Earth's ice, they argue, is closer to 1°C or even lower over the coming centuries.

Melting ice is far from a remote polar problem. It means more flooding, then water shortages for the people who depend on mountain rivers. Nepali glacier researcher Gunjan Silwal described the mountain and glacier collapse in August, which killed more than 1,400 people and left more than 5,000 missing, as "only a foretaste". The report warns that, on current trends, landslides, floods and extreme snow and rain could become "so frequent as to become unmanageable", followed by longer droughts as shrinking snowpack feeds less water downstream. It expects this to "drive large-scale downstream population displacement".

## Key facts

- Switzerland has already lost around 20% of its glacier mass.
- Warming stands at about 1.37°C above pre-industrial levels. Copernicus expects 1.5°C to be crossed around 2030.
- Even below 1.5°C, long-term sea level rise could reach **2 to 9 metres**.
- If warming exceeds 2.8°C into the next century, sea levels could rise by up to 5 centimetres a year by 2150, compared with about 0.45 centimetres a year today.
- In that scenario, thawing permafrost could release as much carbon dioxide each year as the United States, the world's second-largest emitter.
- At 3°C, "severe and possibly abrupt circulation disruptions become extremely likely", including a weakening of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) with "drastic impacts on temperatures in northern Europe".
- A worst-case but not improbable path sees the world heading for 2.6°C by 2100 even if countries meet their climate commitments.

## Can the ice come back?

Partly, and slowly. Bringing warming back below 1.5°C, or towards 1°C, could let some glaciers partly regrow and help sea ice recover. But oceanographer Matthew England of the University of New South Wales says recovery could take "decades to centuries", and some tipping points, once crossed, cannot be undone on any human timescale.

Kirkham's message is that the Paris goals still matter. "Temporarily exceeding 1.5C does not mean that the goals of the Paris Agreement are irrelevant but instead makes them more urgent," he said. Returning to 1.5°C or below by 2100 remains possible, he argues, by speeding up the move away from fossil fuels to reach net zero by mid-century, and "such urgent action can prevent and even reverse some cryosphere loss."

## Limits of the figures

The sea level and permafrost numbers are long-term projections under specific warming scenarios, not forecasts for this decade. The 2 to 9 metre range plays out over centuries. The 1.37°C figure is a long-term average and differs from single-year values, such as the roughly 1.52°C expected for 2026.
