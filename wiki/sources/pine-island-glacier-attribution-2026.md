# Human activity has driven retreat of Antarctica's fastest melting glacier

**Source:** Phys.org press release based on King's College London and British Antarctic Survey research, 28 June 2026. https://phys.org/news/2026-06-human-driven-retreat-antarctica-fastest.html

The peer-reviewed study was published in *The Cryosphere* (2026), led by Dr Alex Bradley (King's College London) and Mira Adhikari (British Antarctic Survey).

## What it is

A press release on the first study to directly link the retreat of a major Antarctic glacier to human activity. The team modelled the **Pine Island Glacier**, which drains a large part of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet into the Amundsen Sea. It is one of the biggest single contributors to global sea-level rise.

The researchers ran the glacier's history twice: once with the ocean warming caused by human greenhouse gases, and once without it. The difference between the two shows how much of the retreat is down to us.

## Why it matters

Scientists now routinely work out how much climate change made a heatwave, a flood or a mountain glacier's melt worse. Doing the same for an Antarctic glacier has been much harder, because the ice responds slowly and the ocean underneath is hard to measure. This is the first time anyone has put a number on how much of a major Antarctic ice loss can be laid at the door of human emissions.

The answer is moderate in size, but it changes the story. Ice loss from West Antarctica is no longer a purely natural process, and the emissions we produce today will keep shaping sea levels, and the lives of people on coasts around the world, for centuries.

## Key facts

- Ocean warming driven by greenhouse gases increased Pine Island Glacier's retreat by 18 to 20% since the 1940s.
- By 2015, simulations without human influence showed about 4 km less retreat of the grounding line (the point where the glacier lifts off the seabed and starts to float). That is about one-fifth of the total retreat observed.
- Pine Island began retreating fast in the 1940s, as stronger flows of warm ocean water reached under its floating ice shelf.
- Human-driven ocean warming in the region is thought to have started in the 1960s and has reinforced the retreat since then.
- The glacier may briefly stabilise later this century, when it reaches a ridge in the bedrock beneath it.
- If warming continues, that pause will be temporary, and human influence is projected to be the main driver of retreat in the 22nd century.

## Voices in the piece

Dr Alex Bradley, the lead author, puts the finding simply: "Without sustained warming of the surrounding ocean since the mid-20th century, the glacier would not have retreated as far as it has." He adds a warning about timescales: "Ice sheets respond slowly. The impacts of today's emissions will continue to shape Antarctic ice loss for centuries."

Mira Adhikari of the British Antarctic Survey places the result in a wider pattern: "Our results add to growing evidence that human-driven climate change is likely affecting even the most remote regions of the planet."
