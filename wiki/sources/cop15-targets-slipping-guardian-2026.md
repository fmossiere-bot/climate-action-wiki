# Four years after COP15, our biodiversity targets are slipping away (Patrick Greenfield, Guardian)

**Source:** Guardian, Patrick Greenfield, 16 October 2026. https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/oct/14/four-years-after-the-promises-of-cop15-our-biodiversity-targets-are-slipping-away

## What it is

Patrick Greenfield, the Guardian's biodiversity reporter, wrote this comment piece days before COP17 opened in Yerevan, Armenia. He was in the hall in Montreal in December 2022 when countries agreed the **Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework** at COP15, and he remembers the excitement.

Four years on, that optimism has faded. Progress reports ahead of COP17 show the world off track on all 23 targets. They use a traffic-light system that, in Greenfield's words, "is awash with yellow, orange and red". At the same time, international cooperation has weakened, and the US pulled back its funding under Trump's second term just when nature needed it most.

## Why it matters

Greenfield has followed these talks for years, so his warning carries weight. It comes before delegates arrive in Yerevan, not in the rush of the opening week.

The **12.1%** figure shows how big the US retreat is. Between 2015 and 2022, USAID and its partners provided an average of 12.1% of the world's biodiversity funding, about an eighth of the total over seven years. Almost all of it disappeared between 2024 and 2026. Behind that number are real programmes, such as rangers protecting rhinos in East Africa and patrols against illegal fishing in the Galápagos.

The EU's experience is a warning too. The EU did try to put COP15 into practice at home with its Nature Restoration Law, but the law became "a culture-wars issue". That lowers expectations of what COP17 can achieve.

## Key facts

- COP15 in Montreal, in December 2022, adopted the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework. It has 23 targets, including protecting **30% of the planet** by 2030 (known as 30×30), restoring 30% of degraded ecosystems and tackling $500bn of subsidies that harm nature.
- Nearly every country signed. The exceptions were the US, which has not ratified the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), and the Vatican.
- The progress report before COP17 found gaps on all 23 targets, and no target "presents a fully positive picture".
- According to the OECD, USAID and partners provided an average of 12.1% of global biodiversity funding between 2015 and 2022. Under Trump's second term, "almost all of that has gone".
- The cuts hit anti-rhino-poaching work in East Africa and efforts against illegal fishing in the Galápagos.
- There are some bright spots. Kazakhstan has used the agreement to bring back the Caspian tiger, and the EU passed its Nature Restoration Law, though it turned into "a culture-wars issue".

## Voices in the piece

The official progress report published before COP17 is blunt: *"Unless collective implementation accelerates rapidly, the 2030 targets and mission will not be achieved. Parties and other actors have taken action to address all 23 targets; however, no target presents a fully positive picture."*

Zac Goldsmith was the UK's environment minister when the COP15 deal was reached in 2022. He says: *"There's a massive gap between countries agreeing to do something and actually doing it. That's where we are today."*

Greenfield himself writes: *"The world has lost its way on nature once again... this time, humans are the asteroid."*

## Limits of the figures

This is a Guardian comment piece, not peer-reviewed research. The 12.1% USAID share comes from the OECD and is credible, but "almost all of that has gone" is the reporter's own assessment. The Galápagos and East African programmes are given as examples, not a full list. The piece also focuses on the US and says little about what BRICS countries, oil-producing states or Indigenous-led initiatives are doing to fill the gap.
