# COP17 key outcomes: Carbon Brief's round-up from Yerevan

**Source:** Carbon Brief, Carbon Brief Staff, 31 October 2026, with a correction published 3 November 2026. https://www.carbonbrief.org/cop17-key-outcomes-agreed-at-the-un-biodiversity-conference-in-yerevan

Two clipped copies of this article were ingested. They are the same piece: the later one corrects the name of a country that steered the talks on genetic data (Norway, not Canada). This page follows the corrected version.

## What it is

Carbon Brief's team followed the two weeks of the UN biodiversity talks (COP17) in Yerevan, Armenia, and wrote up every major decision. The conference held the first **global review** of progress on the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, the 2022 deal often called the "Paris Agreement for nature", which set 23 targets for 2030. The review "notes with deep concern" that unless action is "rapidly accelerated", those targets "will not be achieved". A UN report published just before the talks found the world falling behind on 22 of the 23.

Countries signed off all the main texts after a final plenary of nearly 12 hours. That contrasts with COP16 in Cali in 2024, which ran so far over time that it lost its quorum (the minimum number of countries needed to take decisions) and had to resume in Rome months later. Quito, Ecuador, will host COP18 in 2028.

## Why it matters

Most of the fight in Yerevan was about money. Rich countries missed their promise to provide "at least $20bn a year by 2025" for nature, and delegates pointed to a 27% fall in environmental funding for 2026-30. Instead of agreeing how to close that gap, the talks were dominated by a push from developed countries to add emerging economies to the list of donors. The list, set under Article 20 of the 1992 Convention on Biological Diversity, was last changed in 2006.

The compromise lets countries that want to join the donor list "signal interest" in confidential talks before COP18. No country can be added without its explicit consent. Countries also agreed criteria for a permanent nature-finance mechanism after 2030, under the COP's authority, but left the question of whether it will be a new fund until COP18.

## Key facts

- The global review "urges" faster action "including by increased ambition", while recognising that developing countries need more support to do so.
- The **Cali Fund**, which asks companies using nature's genetic data to pay 0.1% of revenue or 1% of profit, held $6,000 at the start of COP17. By the end, pledges totalled $10,565, with less than $9,000 actually deposited. No large company in the sectors that rely on this data has paid.
- Governments spend up to $1.8tn a year on subsidies that may harm nature. Target 18 aims to cut them by at least $500bn a year by 2030, but 84% of countries missed the 2025 deadline just to identify them.
- A Conservation International report put the "hidden environmental and social damage costs" of these subsidies at $11-24.7tn a year.
- Russia forced the text on marine biodiversity to only "take note" of the High Seas Treaty, which came into force in January 2026 and has been ratified by more than 100 countries.
- The climate and biodiversity text dropped a reference to the International Court of Justice's 2025 climate opinion, at Russia's insistence.
- More than 130 businesses and financial organisations signed an open letter before COP17 asking governments to reform nature-harming incentives.

## Genetic data and the Cali Fund

**Digital sequence information (DSI)** is the genetic data taken from plants, animals and microbes and stored in databases. Drug, cosmetics and seed companies use it to develop products. Most decisions on who should pay into the Cali Fund were pushed to talks before COP18. The final text, adopted at 5:25am on the last day, keeps the suggested rates and sets out how money will be shared after COP18. It allows payments "on an exceptional basis" if the fund reaches $150m. Half of the money will go to Indigenous peoples and local communities, 90% of that share to those in developing countries.

June Rubis, an Indigenous representative on the fund's steering committee, said wealthy nations "asked for a voluntary fund, got one, and business has spent the last two years stalling contributions". Siva Thambisetty, a law professor at the London School of Economics, called the fund "on life support". An industry-led alternative, the Biodiversity Foundation Initiative, was launched at the talks; Indigenous leaders said they were "actively lobbied" to back it.

## Subsidies, oceans and science

The paragraph on harmful subsidies survived, but softened from "urges" to "encourages", "in accordance with national circumstances". Saudi Arabia and Russia opposed it, and India proposed deleting it near the end. A new "friends of target 18" group, run by the International Institute for Sustainable Development and co-chaired by Colombia and Finland, will help countries share ways to reform subsidies. One of its advisers said governments are now taking the issue seriously "because they are out of money". Dozens of countries raised fossil-fuel subsidies in 2026 after the war involving Iran disrupted energy supplies.

On the ocean, countries set up an informal advisory group on marine areas beyond national borders. A Chilean delegate described the removal of agreed language on Indigenous peoples, women and youth as "erasing with our elbow what we have just written". On climate, a reference to the IPBES assessment on "transformative change" was dropped, while its "nexus" assessment on biodiversity, water, food and health stayed in. New voluntary guidelines on nature-based solutions were agreed.

## People, markets and the host

Countries agreed how the new permanent body for Indigenous peoples will work, under Article 8(j) of the convention. On markets, EU environment commissioner Jessika Roswall launched a "Friends of Credits for Nature and Biodiversity" group, and China announced a nature-market pilot in Sichuan. Peru, which said offsets show "no additionality" (no proof they protect nature that would not have been protected anyway), won a study on whether biodiversity credits and offsets deliver real gains.

Armenia's hosting was praised for its organisation, but activists against the Amulsar gold mine, which started operating a week before COP17, were stopped from entering the venue. A Mighty Earth report said Cyclone Senyar wiped out around 7% of the rare Tapanuli orangutan population in Sumatra.
