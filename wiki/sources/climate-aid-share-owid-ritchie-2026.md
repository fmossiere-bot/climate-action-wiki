# How much development aid is spent on climate change? Hannah Ritchie, Our World in Data

**Source:** Our World in Data, Hannah Ritchie and Pablo Arriagada, 26 October 2026. https://ourworldindata.org/how-much-development-aid-is-spent-on-climate-change

## What it is

Hannah Ritchie and Pablo Arriagada of Our World in Data look at how much of the world's development aid is now labelled "climate-related", and what that label means in practice. Their answer is that around one-fifth of all development aid now carries a climate code. In 2024, **$50 billion** of bilateral aid (money given directly from one country to another) went to climate-related projects, up from about $22 billion in 2010.

The data come from the OECD's RioMarkers database. It tags aid projects against the Rio Conventions: biodiversity, climate mitigation (cutting emissions), climate adaptation (coping with impacts) and desertification.

The catch is in the detail. More than half of climate-coded aid lists climate as a secondary objective. Hospitals, water systems and hydropower plants that would probably have been funded anyway now carry a climate tag. Count only the projects where climate is the main aim, and the share of allocable aid falls from 34% to 13%.

## Why it matters

When a government says it is funding climate finance, there is now a figure to check that claim against. The $50 billion total also gives a clear starting point for asking how much of it is new money and how much is old projects with a new label.

The drop from 34% to 13% under a stricter definition shows how much depends on the counting rules. It mirrors the Cali Fund story, where $7,085 arrived against hopes of $1bn: the official numbers can paint a rosier picture than reality.

There is also a human trade-off. Aid budgets are shrinking, and Our World in Data shows cuts among the top-10 donors in 2025. If climate takes a bigger slice of a smaller pie, other things get less. Ritchie is clear that she would rather see accurate accounting than pick a side.

## Key facts

- In 2024, total bilateral aid was about $210bn. 31% of it was "non-allocable" (refugee hosting, administration, debt relief), leaving **$145bn** in allocable project aid.
- About $50bn of 2024 bilateral aid was climate-coded. That is about 34% of allocable project aid, about 24% of all bilateral aid, and about 20% of all development aid once multilateral aid is included.
- The $50bn splits roughly evenly three ways: mitigation only, adaptation only, and projects coded as both.
- In 2010, about $22bn was climate-coded. This is probably an undercount, as about 1/3 of allocable aid was not screened for climate back then. The climate share of allocable aid rose from about 18% in 2010 to 34% in 2024.
- More than half of climate-coded funding lists climate as a secondary objective. Counting only primary-objective projects, the share falls from 34% to 13%.
- Donors differ a lot: Japan codes 50-80% of its aid as climate-related, South Korea sometimes more than 60%, France more than 40%, and the US and Canada less than 20%.
- Among recipients, climate-related aid dominates in Brazil and India, both middle-income countries. In low-income countries such as Uganda and Madagascar, its share has risen to nearly 50%.

## Three examples of what gets counted

Ritchie gives three real projects to show how slippery "climate-related" can be.

Japan funded the **$310m** Samawah Water Supply Improvement Project in Iraq. It is tagged as adaptation, because it brings climate-resilient water to a drought-prone area. But a basic water supply is a core development need and would probably have been funded anyway.

France funded the $122m Kakono hydroelectric power plant in Tanzania. It is tagged as mitigation, because it produces low-carbon electricity. Yet donors were building energy infrastructure in developing countries decades before anyone talked about climate.

South Korea funded a $163m hospital and training centre, also in Tanzania. It is tagged as mitigation, as a secondary objective, because the building uses an energy-efficient, low-carbon design. The hospital does not exist to tackle climate change, but the full $163m still counts as climate-related aid.

## Displacement or relabelling

Ritchie frames the piece around two worries that pull in opposite directions.

The first is displacement. Climate projects could push out traditional ones such as bed nets against malaria, nutrition programmes or antiretroviral treatment for HIV. If the aid budget stays the same and climate's share grows, something else shrinks.

The second is relabelling. Traditional projects simply get a climate code. Many donor countries promised climate finance on top of their aid. If that finance is just a new label on existing projects, the extra money is zero.

Her best guess is that both are happening. The fact that more than half of climate funding is secondary-objective points towards relabelling. But the data cannot say how much of each without a detailed, project-by-project look back through history, and Ritchie openly invites someone to do that work.

## Ritchie's own view

Ritchie writes more personally than usual: *"I care a lot about climate change. I also care a lot about basic health and development programs in the world's poorest countries... These two priorities could easily come into conflict."* It reflects a style Our World in Data has moved towards: careful with numbers, but personal in tone.

## Limits of the figures

Not every country screens all of its aid for climate objectives. In the early years, 2010-2014, a lot went unreported, so year-to-year figures before 2014 are rough. The database also covers bilateral aid only, which makes up about 90% of global development aid.
