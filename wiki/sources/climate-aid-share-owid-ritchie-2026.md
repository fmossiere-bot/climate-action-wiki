# How much development aid is spent on climate change? — Hannah Ritchie, Our World in Data

**Source:** Our World in Data, Hannah Ritchie and Pablo Arriagada, 26 October 2026 — https://ourworldindata.org/how-much-development-aid-is-spent-on-climate-change

## What it is

Our World in Data analysis by **Hannah Ritchie** and **Pablo Arriagada** on how much global development aid is now coded as "climate-related" and what that label actually means. **Headline statistic: around one-fifth of all development aid is now coded as climate-related, with $50 billion of bilateral aid going to climate-related projects in 2024, up from ~$22 billion in 2010.** The piece uses the **OECD RioMarkers database** (which tags aid projects against the Rio Conventions: biodiversity, climate mitigation, climate adaptation, desertification). **The key subtlety: more than half of climate-coded aid lists climate as a secondary objective — meaning projects (hospitals, water systems, hydro power) that would have been funded anyway now carry a climate tag.** If only projects with climate as the primary objective are counted, the share falls from 34% to 13%.

## Key facts

- **2024: ~$210bn total bilateral aid**; 31% was "non-allocable" (refugee hosting, admin, debt relief), leaving **$145bn in allocable project aid**
- **~$50bn of 2024 bilateral aid is climate-coded** = **~34% of allocable project aid, ~24% of all bilateral aid, ~20% of all development aid** (including multilateral)
- **The $50bn is split roughly evenly** between mitigation-only, adaptation-only, and projects coded as both
- **In 2010, ~$22bn was climate-coded** (likely under-stated, as ~1/3 of allocable aid wasn't screened then); by 2024, 34% of allocable aid is climate-related, up from ~18% in 2010
- **More than half of climate-coded funding lists climate as a secondary objective**. If only primary-objective projects were counted, climate's share would fall from 34% to **13%**
- **Country donor patterns**: Japan 50-80%, South Korea sometimes >60%, France >40%, US and Canada <20%
- **Recipient patterns**: climate-related aid dominates in Brazil and India (both middle-income); in low-income countries like Uganda and Madagascar it has risen to nearly 50% share

## The three concrete examples Ritchie gives

Each shows why "climate-related" is hard to define cleanly:

1. **Japan + Iraq: $310m Samawah Water Supply Improvement Project** — tagged as adaptation (climate-resilient water in a drought-prone area). But basic water supply is a core development need; probably would have been funded anyway.
2. **France + Tanzania: $122m Kakono hydroelectric power plant** — tagged as mitigation (low-carbon electricity). But energy infrastructure in developing countries predates the climate framing by decades.
3. **South Korea + Tanzania: $163m hospital and training centre** — tagged as mitigation (secondary objective) because the hospital was built with energy-efficient low-carbon design. The hospital doesn't exist to tackle climate change, but all $163m counts as climate-related aid.

## The two tensions

Ritchie frames the whole piece around two concerns pulling in opposite directions:

1. **Displacement**: climate projects could crowd out traditional ones — bed nets, nutrition programmes, HIV antiretroviral treatment. If total aid is fixed and climate's share rises, something else falls.
2. **Relabelling**: traditional projects get a climate code attached. Donor countries have often committed to climate finance **in addition** to aid, but if "climate finance" is just a label on existing projects, the net new money is zero.

Her guess: both are happening. More than half of climate funding being secondary-objective is partial evidence of relabelling. But the data cannot tell us how much of each is happening without a detailed historical project-by-project analysis — which is what Ritchie explicitly invites someone to do.

## Notable framings

- **Ritchie's own voice is unusually direct**: *"I care a lot about climate change. I also care a lot about basic health and development programs in the world's poorest countries... These two priorities could easily come into conflict."* This is OWiD's house style now — numerate but personal.
- **The RioMarkers database caveat is important**: not all countries screen all aid for climate objectives. In the earlier years (2010-2014) significant under-reporting. Year-to-year numbers before 2014 are rough.

## Why it matters

Three things:

1. **A clean numerical answer to a question that is usually ducked.** When politicians say "we're funding climate finance", the headline $50bn number is now auditable against a specific database. It is also the primary number against which the "additional vs relabelled" question can be posed.
2. **The 34% → 13% drop under a stricter definition is a sharable fact.** It is the climate-finance version of the Cali Fund "$7,085 vs $1bn" scandal: the official numbers overstate what is really happening.
3. **The displacement question is live.** As aid budgets shrink (per OWiD data showing cuts to top-10 donors in 2025), the climate-vs-health trade-off becomes real. Ritchie's piece is explicit that her own values would prioritise honest accounting over either side of the argument.
