# Excessive wealth, extreme emissions: Sam Wollaston at the Monaco Yacht Show

**Source:** Guardian, Sam Wollaston, 20 October 2026. https://www.theguardian.com/news/2026/oct/20/excessive-wealth-extreme-emissions-and-ecocide-what-i-learned-at-europes-big-superyacht-show

## What it is

Sam Wollaston spent a day at the Monaco Yacht Show, Europe's biggest superyacht trade fair, and wrote about what he saw. He boards a few of the 118 yachts on display, talks to a broker and to the French sociologist Grégory Salle, and sets the glamour against the numbers.

The central figure comes from a 2024 Oxfam study. It looked at 23 superyachts owned by 18 billionaires and found that each one produces, on average, about **5,672 tonnes of CO2 a year**. An average person on Earth would need 860 years to produce that much.

The fleet is also growing. Boat International counted 1,093 yachts over 24 metres under construction in 2026, against 755 in 2016.

## Why it matters

Most of us will never set foot on one of these boats, which is part of the point. A single yacht emits about 5,672 tonnes of CO2 a year, which would take an average person 860 years, roughly 10 generations, to produce. That gap is hard to ignore when people are asked to cut back on heating, driving or flying. It echoes a wider pattern in how money moves: the UN estimates that for every £1 spent protecting nature, about £30 goes to activities that damage it.

Salle calls the pattern "conspicuous seclusion". The yachts are huge and famous, so everyone can see them, yet their owners are hard to reach. Most yachts are registered in places with low or no tax, such as the Cayman Islands, the Marshall Islands, the Isle of Man and Malta, and many are held through trusts or shell companies. That makes them difficult to tax, regulate or sanction.

There is also a local cost that has nothing to do with carbon. When these boats drop anchor in the Mediterranean, their anchors and chains tear up **Posidonia seagrass**, a habitat that much of the region's sea life depends on. Mooring buoys, anchoring limits and marine protected areas are practical ways to reduce that damage.

## Key facts

- Boat International's 2026 Global Order Book lists **1,093 superyachts** over 24 metres under construction. That is 3.95% fewer than in 2025, but the new yachts are longer and heavier on average. In 2016 the figure was 755.
- The 2026 Monaco show had 118 superyachts with a combined length of 5,508 metres and a combined value of €4.5bn.
- Oxfam (2024): 23 yachts owned by 18 billionaires average about 5,672 tonnes of CO2 each per year, or 860 years of an average person's emissions.
- The Walton family (Walmart heirs) owns three superyachts. In one year they sailed 56,000 nautical miles and produced about 18,000 tonnes of CO2, roughly the footprint of 1,700 Walmart employees.
- The bigger yachts burn more than 500 litres of diesel an hour, releasing nitrogen oxides and sulphur dioxide as well as CO2. Recreational boating as a whole emits about 16 million tonnes of CO2 a year.
- Yachts under 500 gross tonnes avoid the International Maritime Organization's stricter safety and environmental rules, so many are built just below that size.
- Orders surged in 2022 and 2023, during and after Covid. Salle says a yacht became "the best way to practise social distancing in a very safe and very comfortable way" for the very rich.

## The yachts and owners named

- **Koru** (127m), Jeff Bezos. Cost about €444m. It is a sailing yacht, but a 75m support vessel follows it.
- **Launchpad** (118m), Mark Zuckerberg, with support vessels.
- **Nixie** (102m), Nik Storonsky, co-founder and CEO of Revolut. Hire costs €2.4m a week.
- **Leviathan** (111m) and its 93m support vessel **Draak**, Gabe Newell, co-founder of Valve. The fuel tank holds 494,000 litres and costs about €1m to fill.
- **Boardwalk V** (76.5m), Tilman Fertitta, Trump's ambassador to Italy. For sale at €135m.
- **Azzam** (180m), the world's longest superyacht, launched for Sheikh Khalifa bin Zayed Al Nahyan of Abu Dhabi.
- **Dilbar**, the largest by volume, formerly linked to Alisher Usmanov. A German court ruled that sanctions no longer apply, finding too little evidence that he controls it.
- **Solaris** (140m) and **Eclipse** (162m), Roman Abramovich. Now moored near Istanbul, as Turkey did not join the sanctions.
- **Scheherazade** (140m, €620m), which Navalny's activists link to Putin. Impounded in Italy.

## Voices in the piece

Salle sees the superyacht as "the symbol of excessive, unjustifiable wealth", adding that "it looks like provocation, especially when all you're hearing and reading about is crisis, austerity, poverty, including child poverty." He describes the owners as a "global super-bourgeoisie" or "transnational capitalist class".

A broker, quoted anonymously, admits: "I don't understand the clients. They just want something that's big and flashy and comfortable, they don't care about anything else."

Wollaston ends bluntly: "They drink fossil fuels like there's no tomorrow, which there won't be if they're allowed to carry on."

## Limits of the figures

The Oxfam average comes from 23 yachts, not the whole fleet, and real emissions vary a lot with size and use. Boat International's figure counts yachts being built, not those already at sea, so it shows where the fleet is heading rather than what it emits today.
