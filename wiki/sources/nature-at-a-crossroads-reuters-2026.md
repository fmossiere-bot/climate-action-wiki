# Nature at a Crossroads — Reuters Events (2026)

**Source:** Reuters Events Sustainable Business — "Nature at a Crossroads: The evolving architecture of nature disclosure"
**Author:** Catherine Early
**Format:** ~10-page industry report / long-form article, commissioned by Reuters Events
**Received:** July 2026

## What it is

A **Reuters Events long-form report on the evolving architecture of corporate nature and biodiversity disclosure**. It focuses on the controversy that broke out in **April 2024** when the **International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB)** announced it would not develop a **standalone mandatory standard on nature** (which would have been "IFRS S3"), and instead work on a **non-mandatory "practice statement"** attached to its existing IFRS S1. The report gives voice to both sides: the practicality argument from Chair Emmanuel Faber, and the backlash from Nature Finance, WWF, BirdLife International, the Nature Positive Initiative, Paul Polman, Johan Rockström and Pavan Sukhdev.

## Why it matters

Roughly **$44 trillion of annual economic output** is directly dependent on nature, per estimates cited in the report. PwC estimates that unchecked nature loss could **erode 12–17% of GDP within the next 15 years**, and a University of Sussex team estimates that even **partial collapses in fisheries, wild pollination and tropical timber would cost the global economy $2 trillion a year**. The Task Force for Nature-related Financial Disclosures (**TNFD**) has been building a voluntary framework since 2021; by end of 2025 more than **730 companies** had voluntarily adopted TNFD (46% year-on-year growth), representing over **$9 trillion in market capitalisation and $22 trillion in assets under management**. The question the ISSB now sits at the centre of is whether that voluntary momentum becomes **mandatory global reporting** — or whether nature is quietly re-classified as optional while climate remains mandatory.

## Timeline

- **2021** — TNFD is launched with G20 support
- **September 2023** — TNFD publishes its recommendations
- **April 2024** — ISSB announces it has commenced research on nature disclosure; TNFD pauses its own technical work to align
- **April 2025** — ISSB and TNFD sign a formal agreement to work together
- **November 2025** — ISSB begins standard-setting phase, undecided whether to develop a standalone standard, amend existing standards, or take another route
- **April 2026** — News leaks ahead of the ISSB Board meeting that the ISSB will **not** produce a mandatory standard; instead a non-mandatory **"practice statement"** to complement IFRS S1
- **October 2026** — ISSB plans an exposure-draft consultation on the practice statement, ahead of UN biodiversity negotiations (**COP17**)
- **15 August 2026** — China's new **Environmental and Ecological code** takes effect, harmonising climate, biodiversity and resource-use rules

## Statement or standard? The ISSB's choice

An ISSB staff paper considered four approaches: (1) fit nature within existing IFRS S1; (2) fit it within IFRS S2 (Climate); (3) develop a **standalone S3** on nature; or (4) publish a **non-mandatory practice statement**. **Just a month before the decision, the majority of the 16 regulatory members of the IFRS Sustainability Standards Advisory Forum had recommended a dedicated S3.** The ISSB Board went with the practice statement.

The **practicality argument** (Chair Emmanuel Faber): more than **40 jurisdictions are already in the process of putting ISSB Standards into law**; changing S1 or S2 or introducing a mandatory S3 now "would heavily disrupt that process." IFRS S1 already requires disclosure of all material sustainability-related risks, and for companies reliant on water, land or agricultural inputs, nature is already implicitly captured. Signe Norberg (IEMA) welcomes the move as still "a step in the right direction." Thomas Viegas (Aviva) argues that firms will need to look at nature under S1 anyway.

## The backlash

The pushback has been robust and comes from actors including WWF, BirdLife International, business leader Paul Polman, **Johan Rockström** (Potsdam Institute), Pavan Sukhdev (GIST Impact / TEEB), Norges Bank Investment Management-linked signatories, and the Nature Positive Initiative Secretariat. The core arguments are three:

1. **Materiality contradiction.** **Julie McCarthy** of Nature Finance calls the decision **"a hall pass for the destruction of half the global economy,"** noting that $44 trillion of annual output depends on nature. Declining to require disclosure of risks touching more than half of global GDP is not a procedural judgment — "it is a determination about materiality that the evidence flatly contradicts."
2. **Undermines the climate standard.** Carbon sinks like forests, wetlands and ocean systems are collapsing. Requiring companies to report **climate risks under IFRS S2 while assuming those ecosystems remain intact is scientifically false**, and this is exactly what the ISSB is now doing. McCarthy: *"The ISSB is not simply declining to address nature. It is actively maintaining a disclosure framework that instructs companies to report climate risk against a baseline that the board's own scientific advisors know to be false."*
3. **A practice statement is not a standard.** Gavin Edwards, executive director of the Nature Positive Initiative Secretariat, is blunt: *"The very purpose of ISSB's work is to bring standardisation, and that requires some level of specificity. Working halfway towards the standard through a practice statement does not make logical sense."* Marco Lambertini adds: *"A practice statement asks companies, politely, to consider looking. A standard requires them to report what they find. The decade-long history of voluntary sustainability disclosure makes one thing crystal clear: companies do not disclose what they are not required to disclose."*

The most quoted line in the report: **"Nature is optional and climate is mandatory. That's the gist that nobody can deny."** (Lambertini)

## Fragmentation vs consolidation

While the US and EU are seeing political pushback against environmental regulation, other jurisdictions are moving forward. **Japan** is the largest single-country contingent of TNFD adopters (~130 companies and financial institutions). **China's Environmental and Ecological code**, effective 15 August 2026, unifies climate, biodiversity and resource-use rules into one system that applies to all companies operating in China, including foreign joint ventures. **86% of Asia-Pacific firms** surveyed by TNFD already use or plan to use nature-related disclosure; **45% of financial-institution adopters are in Asia**. Lambertini argues the ISSB's approach was shaped by "political pushback against environmental regulation in the US and EU," and that other jurisdictions are quietly overtaking the ISSB's own standard.

McCarthy warns the disruption argument cuts both ways: without a unified ISSB standard, companies face a **patchwork of jurisdictional rules on nature** — including the EU's mandatory nature standard and the ISO's biodiversity framework — that a unified S3 would have closed.

## What is being called for

The critics converge on similar asks:

- If the practice statement goes ahead, it must **fill the gap on scope and measurement**, in a way similar to how climate is assessed under IFRS S2 and the GHG Protocol, using TNFD's impact metrics and the State of Nature metrics being finalised by the Nature Positive Initiative
- Companies should be required to assess **impacts on nature, its state, and dependencies of business and investment** — the full LEAP approach — rather than only material financial risk
- Guidance should extend from **own operations down to supply chains**, again mirroring climate reporting
- The ISSB has committed to an **October 2026 exposure-draft consultation** on the practice statement, ahead of **COP17** biodiversity negotiations — the critics are pointing towards that as the moment to widen the ask

Business for Nature CEO Eva Zabey urges wait-and-see: *"The detail will really matter, and we're looking forward to reviewing the exposure draft."*

## New tools and adoption

The report signposts several **new nature-related tools published in early 2026**:

- **Placing Nature on the Board Agenda** — Institute of Sustainability and Environmental Professionals and the Aldersgate Group
- **Asking Better Questions on Nature** — TNFD and Accounting for Sustainability (A4S)
- **Natcap** launched a new module in its data platform to help companies compare and prioritise nature-related opportunities
- **TNFD 2025 Status Report:** ~620 organisations across 50+ countries, with ~$20 trillion in AUM, have committed to adopting the TNFD framework
