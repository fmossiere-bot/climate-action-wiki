# Nature at a Crossroads (Reuters Events, 2026)

**Source:** Reuters Events Sustainable Business, Catherine Early. "Nature at a Crossroads: The evolving architecture of nature disclosure". An industry report of about 10 pages, commissioned by Reuters Events and received in July 2026.

## What it is

This long-form report looks at how companies are, or are not, required to report on their impact on nature and biodiversity. It centres on a row that began in April 2024, when the **International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB)** started work on nature disclosure. The ISSB sets global rules for how companies report on sustainability. In April 2026 it decided not to write a separate, mandatory standard on nature (which would have been called "IFRS S3"). Instead it chose a voluntary "practice statement" attached to its existing general standard, IFRS S1.

The report hears from both sides. ISSB Chair Emmanuel Faber makes the case for being practical. Nature Finance, WWF, BirdLife International, the Nature Positive Initiative, Paul Polman, Johan Rockström and Pavan Sukhdev lead the criticism.

## Why it matters

Estimates cited in the report suggest that about **$44 trillion** of yearly economic output depends directly on nature. PwC estimates that unchecked nature loss could wipe out 12–17% of GDP within the next 15 years. A team at the University of Sussex found that even partial collapses in fisheries, wild pollination and tropical timber would cost the global economy $2 trillion a year.

The Task Force on Nature-related Financial Disclosures (TNFD) has been building a voluntary reporting framework since 2021. By the end of 2025, more than 730 companies had taken it up, 46% more than a year earlier. Together they represent over $9 trillion in market value and $22 trillion in assets under management. The open question is whether that voluntary progress becomes mandatory reporting around the world, or whether nature quietly becomes optional while climate reporting stays compulsory.

## Key facts

- The ISSB chose a **non-mandatory practice statement** over a mandatory IFRS S3 on nature. The decision leaked in April 2026, and chair Emmanuel Faber defends it as pragmatic.
- PwC: unchecked nature loss could erode 12–17% of GDP within 15 years. University of Sussex: even partial collapses in fisheries, pollination and tropical timber would cost $2 trillion a year.
- About $44 trillion of yearly economic output depends directly on nature (figure cited by Nature Finance).
- 730+ companies had voluntarily adopted the TNFD framework by the end of 2025 (46% growth on the year before), representing more than $9tn in market value and more than $22tn in assets under management.
- Critics, including WWF, BirdLife International, Johan Rockström, Paul Polman, Pavan Sukhdev, Marco Lambertini and Julie McCarthy, argue the practice statement weakens IFRS S2, the climate standard, because it ignores the collapse of natural carbon sinks.
- China's Environmental and Ecological code takes effect on 15 August 2026, bringing climate and nature rules together for all companies operating in China.
- In October 2026 the ISSB holds a consultation on a draft of the practice statement, ahead of the COP17 biodiversity talks.

## Timeline

- **2021**: TNFD launches with G20 support.
- **September 2023**: TNFD publishes its recommendations.
- **April 2024**: ISSB announces it has started research on nature disclosure. TNFD pauses its own technical work to stay in step.
- **April 2025**: ISSB and TNFD sign a formal agreement to work together.
- **November 2025**: ISSB begins setting a standard, still undecided whether to write a standalone standard, change existing ones or take another route.
- **April 2026**: News leaks ahead of the ISSB Board meeting that there will be no mandatory standard, only a non-mandatory "practice statement" to go alongside IFRS S1.
- **15 August 2026**: China's new Environmental and Ecological code takes effect, bringing climate, biodiversity and resource-use rules together.
- **October 2026**: ISSB plans a public consultation on a draft of the practice statement (an "exposure draft"), ahead of the UN biodiversity talks at COP17.

## Statement or standard? The ISSB's choice

An ISSB staff paper looked at four options: fit nature into the existing IFRS S1; fit it into IFRS S2 (the climate standard); write a standalone S3 on nature; or publish a non-mandatory practice statement. Only a month before the decision, most of the **16 regulatory members** of the IFRS Sustainability Standards Advisory Forum had recommended a dedicated S3. The ISSB Board chose the practice statement anyway.

Faber's case is about timing. More than 40 jurisdictions are already turning ISSB Standards into law, and changing S1 or S2, or adding a mandatory S3, now "would heavily disrupt that process." He adds that IFRS S1 already asks companies to report all material sustainability risks, so for firms that rely on water, land or farm inputs, nature is already covered in practice. Signe Norberg of IEMA, a professional body for environmental practitioners, sees the move as still "a step in the right direction." Thomas Viegas of Aviva argues that companies will have to look at nature under S1 anyway.

## The backlash

The criticism has been strong. It comes from WWF, BirdLife International, business leader Paul Polman, Johan Rockström of the Potsdam Institute, Pavan Sukhdev of GIST Impact and TEEB, signatories linked to Norges Bank Investment Management, and the Nature Positive Initiative Secretariat.

Their first point is about what counts as material. Julie McCarthy of Nature Finance calls the decision **"a hall pass for the destruction of half the global economy,"** since $44 trillion of yearly output depends on nature. In her view, choosing not to require disclosure of risks that touch more than half of global GDP is more than a procedural call: "it is a determination about materiality that the evidence flatly contradicts."

Their second point is that the decision weakens the climate standard itself. Forests, wetlands and oceans, which soak up carbon, are in decline. Asking companies to report climate risks under IFRS S2 while assuming those ecosystems stay healthy is scientifically wrong, critics say, and that is what the ISSB is now doing. McCarthy puts it this way: *"The ISSB is not simply declining to address nature. It is actively maintaining a disclosure framework that instructs companies to report climate risk against a baseline that the board's own scientific advisors know to be false."*

Their third point is that a practice statement cannot do the job of a standard. Gavin Edwards, executive director of the Nature Positive Initiative Secretariat, says: *"The very purpose of ISSB's work is to bring standardisation, and that requires some level of specificity. Working halfway towards the standard through a practice statement does not make logical sense."* Marco Lambertini adds: *"A practice statement asks companies, politely, to consider looking. A standard requires them to report what they find. The decade-long history of voluntary sustainability disclosure makes one thing crystal clear: companies do not disclose what they are not required to disclose."*

The line most often quoted from the report is Lambertini's: "Nature is optional and climate is mandatory. That's the gist that nobody can deny."

## Fragmentation or consolidation

The US and EU are seeing political pushback against environmental rules, but other places are moving ahead. Japan has the largest group of TNFD adopters of any single country, with about 130 companies and financial institutions. China's Environmental and Ecological code, in force from 15 August 2026, brings climate, biodiversity and resource-use rules into one system for every company operating in China, including foreign joint ventures. Of the Asia-Pacific firms surveyed by TNFD, **86%** already use or plan to use nature-related disclosure, and 45% of financial institutions that have adopted TNFD are in Asia. Lambertini argues that the ISSB's choice was shaped by "political pushback against environmental regulation in the US and EU," and that other countries are quietly moving ahead of the ISSB.

McCarthy warns that the disruption argument works both ways. Without one ISSB standard, companies face a patchwork of national rules on nature, including the EU's mandatory nature standard and the ISO's biodiversity framework, a gap that a single S3 would have closed.

## What critics are asking for

- If the practice statement goes ahead, it should spell out scope and measurement, much as climate is handled under IFRS S2 and the GHG Protocol. It should use TNFD's impact measures and the State of Nature measures that the Nature Positive Initiative is finalising.
- Companies should have to assess their impacts on nature, the state of nature, and how their business and investments depend on it (TNFD's full LEAP approach), rather than only the financial risks that are material to them.
- Guidance should reach beyond a company's own operations into its supply chains, as climate reporting does.
- The ISSB has committed to an October 2026 consultation on the draft practice statement, ahead of the COP17 biodiversity talks. Critics see that as the moment to push for more.

Eva Zabey, chief executive of Business for Nature, is waiting to see the detail: *"The detail will really matter, and we're looking forward to reviewing the exposure draft."*

## New tools and adoption

The report points to several new nature tools published in early 2026:

- **Placing Nature on the Board Agenda**, from the Institute of Sustainability and Environmental Professionals and the Aldersgate Group
- **Asking Better Questions on Nature**, from TNFD and Accounting for Sustainability (A4S)
- **Natcap** added a module to its data platform to help companies compare and rank nature-related opportunities
- **TNFD 2025 Status Report**: about 620 organisations in 50+ countries, with about $20 trillion in assets under management, have committed to adopting the TNFD framework
