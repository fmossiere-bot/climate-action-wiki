# Turning food waste into jet fuel with simpler refining

**Source:** TechXplore, Marianne Stein, 22 June 2026. https://techxplore.com/news/2026-06-food-jet-fuel-simpler-refining.html
The peer-reviewed study is Buchun Si et al., Nature Sustainability, 2026 (DOI: 10.1038/s41893-026-01848-1), from the Department of Agricultural and Biological Engineering at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign.

## What it is

Researchers at the University of Illinois have found a simpler and cheaper way to make **sustainable aviation fuel (SAF)** from food waste. First, a process called hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) uses heat and pressure to turn the waste into a kind of crude oil. It copies the way nature makes crude oil, but takes a fraction of the time. The oil is then refined mainly by distillation, rather than the heavier chemical processing with catalysts used in their earlier work.

The trade-off is quality. The simpler method costs less but gives a lower-grade fuel, so it has to be mixed with ordinary jet fuel.

## Why it matters

Flying is one of the hardest parts of our lives to decarbonise, and there is not much sustainable fuel to go around. Food waste is a large and steady supply that would otherwise rot in landfill. A cheaper route from rubbish bin to fuel tank makes that supply more useful.

The lead researcher, Prof Yuanhui Zhang, compares the idea to biodiesel for cars, which is also sold as a blend. Even a fuel that is only 10-20% SAF would be practical, given how little is available.

## Key facts

- Food waste is turned into crude oil by HTL, then refined by distillation.
- The method is simpler and cheaper than the team's previous one, but the fuel is lower quality and has to be blended.
- Tested as a **50-50 blend** with conventional jet fuel, it meets ASTM and FAA standards for jet fuel.
- Blends with 10-20% SAF are also feasible and practical, given limited supply.
- HTL can also use treated wastewater as a feedstock, so wet organic waste can be used.
- The biggest bottleneck is logistics: collecting food waste and moving it from landfills and wastewater plants to where it can be processed.
- A life-cycle analysis found that both the baseline and the improved treatment scenarios achieve negative carbon emissions, meaning they remove more carbon than they release.

## The leftover water

HTL leaves behind a toxic liquid called the HTL aqueous phase (HTL-AP). It can be cleaned up with electrochemical (EC) treatment, which also recovers useful acids and nutrients. That treatment nearly triples the cost per gallon compared with the baseline, because the equipment and running costs are higher. The researchers expect the cost to fall as the technology improves.
