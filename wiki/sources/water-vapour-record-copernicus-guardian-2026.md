# Global atmospheric water vapour record in August 2026: Copernicus C3S

**Source:** Guardian, Graham Readfearn, 17 September 2026. https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/sep/18/planets-atmosphere-breaks-record-for-water-vapour-levels-increasing-risk-of-extreme-heat-and-rainfall

**Primary source:** Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S), ERA5 monthly bulletin, August 2026

## What it is

Guardian reporting on a new global record. According to Europe's Copernicus Climate Change Service, the air held more water vapour in August 2026 than in any month on record.

Averaged over the globe, the water vapour in the air reached **27.35 kg per square metre** that month. That is 0.04 kg per square metre above the previous record, set in July 2024. It sounds tiny, but spread over the whole planet it comes to roughly 20 trillion litres of extra water in the air: about 41 Sydney Harbours, or "20 cubes of water, each a km wide, high and deep". The Sydney Harbour comparison comes from Jason Evans of UNSW.

Heat is one cause. August was the joint-hottest month in the ERA5 record, which goes back to 1940, and the hottest in NOAA's record, which goes back to 1850. The other is the current El Niño in the tropical Pacific, expected to be the strongest on record, which is pushing huge amounts of moisture into the air from warm, evaporating seas.

## Why it matters

Much of what people feel as climate change comes through water vapour. Heatwaves where nights stay hot, storms that drop 100mm of rain in an hour, cyclones that strengthen faster than before: all of these are fed by extra moisture in the air. Because warmer air holds about 7% more water vapour per degree of warming, every temperature record tends to bring humidity and rainfall records with it.

This is also a direct observation of a feedback loop, not a forecast. As carbon dioxide and methane warm the air, it holds more water vapour, and that water vapour traps more heat. Each record like this makes the loop harder to ignore.

Andrew Dessler of Texas A&M says it is "unimaginable" that any month in the last few thousand years, perhaps 100,000 years, held more water vapour than August 2026. In other words, the atmosphere is now in a state with no clear match in either measurements or the deep climate record.

## Key facts

- August 2026: an average of 27.35 kg of water vapour per square metre worldwide, the highest monthly value in the ERA5 record (which starts in 1979).
- That beat the previous record (July 2024) by 0.04 kg per square metre, about 20 trillion litres, or 41 Sydney Harbours.
- August was also the joint-hottest month in ERA5 (since 1940) and the hottest month in NOAA's record (since 1850).
- Warmer air can hold about **7% more water vapour** for each degree of global warming. Scientists call this the Clausius-Clapeyron relationship.
- The current El Niño is forecast to be the strongest on record. A first analysis by Australian scientists found global heating made the record Pacific ocean temperatures 6 times more likely.
- Water vapour levels are estimated by combining humidity readings from satellites, weather balloons and other sources with a weather model.

## The feedback loop

Water vapour is itself a greenhouse gas. More warming leads to more evaporation, which puts more water vapour in the air, which causes more warming. Dr Kimberley Reid of the University of Melbourne explains: "Water vapour is also part of the climate feedback loop – water vapour is a greenhouse gas and so the atmosphere will warm even further, and then take up even more water vapour. The root cause is the extra CO2 and methane."

## What extra water in the air does

- It makes rainfall heavier, including from cyclones.
- It gives storms more energy. Julien Nicolas of C3S says "heat is released when water vapour condenses into cloud droplets, potentially helping to intensify them".
- It carries heat towards the poles. Prof Steven Sherwood of UNSW says "when you move this water vapour to higher latitudes, it puts heat into the air, and so a lot of extreme heat that's experienced there is indirectly from the water vapour".
- It stops the air cooling at night. Sherwood again: "we know high humidity has effects during heatwaves as it stops night-time cooling, so it gets harder for people to recover from very hot days".

## Voices in the piece

Dr Andrew Dessler of Texas A&M said it was "unimaginable that any month in the last few thousand years, maybe 100,000 years" had had more water vapour in the air than August 2026. He added: "We are, as the proverb says, living in interesting times."

Dr Julien Nicolas, senior scientist at C3S, called the record "consistent with the record high global temperature in August". He said "the exceptionally high sea surface temperatures seen across these regions in August, partly linked to the presence and strengthening of El Niño conditions, increased evaporation and were likely an important contributor."

Prof Steven Sherwood of UNSW explained why a small number matters: "An extra 0.04 kg m² of water vapour across the planet does not sound like a lot … when this rise is continuous, it starts to have effects. A lot of the impacts of climate change actually happen because of water vapour."

The article quotes six scientists in all, from Australia and the USA, and links water vapour, El Niño and record heat clearly without overstating the case.
