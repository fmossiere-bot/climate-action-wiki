# Less is more, so why does more feel greener? The sustainable additive bias

**Source:** Anthropocene Magazine, Sarah DeWeerdt, October 2026. https://www.anthropocenemagazine.org/2026/10/less-is-more-so-why-does-more-feel-greener/

## What is it

Companies hand out a lot of eco-friendly freebies: cloth tote bags, reusable bottles, recycled notebooks, "sustainable" hotel toiletries. Often the greener move would be to hand out less, or nothing. A team led by Linda Lemarié, associate professor of marketing at Audencia Business School in France, ran nine studies to see how people judge these choices. They named what they found the **sustainable additive bias**: people rate a company as greener when it adds eco-friendly items than when it removes unnecessary ones.

The studies included a field experiment with hotel guests rating their room, a lab experiment where business students chose trade show gifts, and seven online studies with more than 2,900 participants in total. People judged hotel rooms, hair care sets, festival welcome bags and gift boxes.

## Why it matters

Cutting back is often the most effective way to reduce waste, but it leaves nothing to see. Lemarié puts it this way: "reduction leaves nothing visible, even though it's often the best strategy from an environmental standpoint." So a company that quietly removes packaging gets less credit than one that adds a bamboo toothbrush. That rewards more stuff and more waste, and it helps explain why branded "green" giveaways keep multiplying.

It is useful for readers too. When something looks greener because it comes with more eco items, it is worth asking whether fewer items would have been better still.

## Key facts

- Nine studies, including seven online studies with more than 2,900 participants.
- A hotel room with a large selection of eco-friendly toiletries was rated more sustainable than one with a modest set.
- A tea box with eco-friendly tea filters added was rated more sustainable than one with them removed.
- When items were not labelled as eco-friendly, people correctly judged bundles with more items as less sustainable. The bias appears once sustainability is mentioned: people read the number of visible items as a sign of effort.
- Using highly recognisable green items as signals (a refillable bottle rather than biodegradable glitter) weakened the bias. Explaining that items had been removed for environmental reasons may also help.

## The open question

The researchers did not find a simple fix. Lemarié says the question left open is how to show that offering less is a real environmental effort "without appearing hypocritical or making people skeptical."
