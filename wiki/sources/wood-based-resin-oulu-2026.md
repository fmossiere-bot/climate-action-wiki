# New wood-based resin beats fossil resin strength by 76%

**Source:** New Atlas, Etiido Uko (with Juha Heiskanen, University of Oulu), 8 March 2026. https://newatlas.com/materials/bio-based-wooden-plastic-resins/

**Peer-reviewed study:** Composites Part B: Engineering. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1359836825011722

## What it is

New Atlas reports on research from the University of Oulu in Finland. The team has made plant-based versions of the two resins most used in strong composite materials: polyester and epoxy. Composites are materials such as fibreglass, where glass or carbon fibres are held together by a resin.

The researchers, including doctoral researcher Mikko Salonen and team member Juha Heiskanen, made their resins from **furfural**, a chemical produced from woody plant waste such as sawdust, straw and other leftovers from forestry and farming. In tests, the new resins were stronger and tougher than their oil-based equivalents, and they can also be broken down chemically and recycled.

## Why it matters

Resins like these are everywhere. Epoxy and polyester composites form the body of wind turbine blades, boat hulls, car body panels, roofing sheets, aircraft parts, high-end sports gear and reinforcements in civil engineering.

Two problems have held plant-based alternatives back: they were not as strong, and they were hard to deal with at the end of their life. This work reports improvements on both at once. Its chemistry allows the whole composite to be recycled in a closed loop, rather than only recovering the fibres. If it can be scaled up, it could replace an awkward fossil-based ingredient in the wind and boating industries and shrink the carbon and waste footprint of resins more widely.

The article is careful about timing, noting that it "may be a while before we see yachts and turbines made from renewable, recyclable composites". The work is still at the testing stage. The article also presents it as a way for Europe to depend less on imported oil, as well as a climate measure.

## Key facts

- The plant-based polyester resin showed up to **76% higher tensile strength** (resistance to being pulled apart) than a commercial fossil-based polyester.
- The plant-based epoxy composites were tougher and had much higher tensile and flexural (bending) strength than conventional DGEBA (diglycidyl ether of bisphenol A) resins.
- The furfural-based diepoxide has a chemical structure that can be broken down, so composites can be taken apart and their components recovered as raw materials. This is the first reported case of a plant-based epoxy composite that is both high-performing and chemically recyclable.
- The raw material is woody plant matter (lignocellulosic biomass), such as sawdust, straw and other farming and forestry leftovers.
- The resins can be made on existing chemical industry production lines without refitting factories, so no price premium is expected once the base chemicals are made at scale.
- In a 90-day weathering test in a harbour, panels withstood water, UV light, temperature changes and humidity.
- Three patents have been filed, and the team is looking for industrial partners for pilot-scale production.
- The EU holds less than 2% of global oil reserves, so replacing oil-based resins helps Europe become more self-sufficient in materials and supports the circular economy.
