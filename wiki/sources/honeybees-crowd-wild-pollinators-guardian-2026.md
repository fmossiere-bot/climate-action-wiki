# Honeybees outcompete wild pollinators at flowers: Anglia Ruskin study

**Source:** Guardian, Rebecca Ratcliffe, 17 October 2026. https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/oct/17/honeybees-are-barging-aside-wild-pollinators-in-hunt-for-pollen-and-nectar-study-finds

**Journal reference:** Buckle V. et al., *Ecological Entomology* (2026), DOI: 10.1111/een.70139

## What it is

The Guardian reports on a two-year field study in Cambridgeshire by Anglia Ruskin University. It was led by PhD researcher Victoria Buckle.

The study found that honeybees do more than quietly take a bigger share of nectar and pollen. They bump into wild pollinators, chase them and push them off flowers. The team recorded **300 competitive encounters** between insects feeding on bramble and ivy near honeybee hives. The other visitors were bumblebees, solitary bees, hoverflies and a mixed group that included butterflies, beetles and wasps. Honeybees won most encounters on both plants, and places with more honeybees had fewer wild pollinators.

## Why it matters

Many people took up beekeeping to help nature, and the number of registered UK beekeepers grew about 2.5 times in 15 years. Earlier research had already suggested that honeybees compete with wild insects for food. This study is the first to watch them pushing wild pollinators aside directly. The uncomfortable conclusion is that beekeeping, especially with many hives close together, can harm the wildlife it was meant to protect.

Climate change is likely to make this worse. In hotter summers nectar dries up and plants are stressed, so insects compete harder for what is left. UK summers could warm by up to 4°C by 2080, which brings pollinator competition into planning for a hotter climate.

The findings are practical. The number of hives and the number of flowers both matter, and conservation planners should count managed honeybees as one more pressure on wild pollinators, not as a simple benefit. For beekeepers who care about nature, that is useful guidance rather than a reason for guilt.

## Key facts

- The number of registered UK beekeepers rose from **19,000 in 2010 to 47,000 in 2025**, according to the National Bee Unit, driven by "save the bees" campaigns.
- The study recorded 300 competitive encounters over two years on bramble and ivy in Cambridgeshire. Honeybees won most of them on both plants.
- When a honeybee came out on top, it pushed away or chased the other insect in about 20% of cases.
- Other insects kept away from honeybees in 28.4% of encounters on bramble and 35.6% on ivy.
- The study shows "interference competition", meaning direct aggression. Before this, the known problem was "exploitation competition", where honeybees simply use up food first.
- Bumblebees sometimes knock others off too, but probably by accident because of their clumsy flight.
- Higher temperatures led to more competitive encounters, as nectar dried up and plants were stressed by heat. UK average summer temperatures could rise by up to 4°C by 2080.

## The one bee that fights back

The only pollinator in the study that held its own against honeybees was the ivy bee. It was first recorded in the UK in 2001 and feeds mainly on ivy nectar. The researchers saw it push and chase honeybees away.

## Voices in the piece

Victoria Buckle, the study's lead author, explains why the direct aggression matters: *"That's important because, of course, it means that the wild pollinators are facing a challenge from both those types of competition from honeybees."*

She describes how honeybees behave differently: *"With the honeybees, the aggression was more overt. You could tell that they're specifically trying to move the other one off the flower."* She links this to the way hives are organised: *"Some castes of bees can be guard bees, so they have that capacity within them to show threat and aggression, to guard their hives and to look after the brood."*

Bumblebees, by contrast, seem to collide by mistake: *"They bumble along the patch and then they'll mistakenly bump into another pollinator and it just falls off a flower"*.

Buckle is careful not to blame beekeepers: *"A lot of beekeepers are environmentally minded and they want to make sure that they're not impinging or having an impact on other pollinators. This research is important because it helps guide our planning and best practices for conservation."*

## Limits of the findings

The study covers one UK region (Cambridgeshire) and two plants (bramble and ivy), so the results may not apply everywhere or to other flowers. The ivy bee shows that some wild pollinators can cope with honeybees. The open question is whether most wild pollinators in UK landscapes are tough like the ivy bee or get pushed aside like the bumblebees and solitary bees in this study. The research also does not measure how much honeybee competition shrinks wild pollinator populations overall. It shows how the competition works and what happens in each encounter.
