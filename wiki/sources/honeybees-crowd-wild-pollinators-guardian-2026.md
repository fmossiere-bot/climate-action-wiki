# Honeybees outcompete wild pollinators at flowers — Anglia Ruskin study

**Source:** Guardian, Rebecca Ratcliffe, 17 October 2026 — https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/oct/17/honeybees-are-barging-aside-wild-pollinators-in-hunt-for-pollen-and-nectar-study-finds
**Journal reference:** Buckle V. et al., *Ecological Entomology* (2026), DOI: 10.1111/een.70139

## What it is

Guardian reporting on a **two-year Anglia Ruskin University field study in Cambridgeshire** by lead author **Victoria Buckle** (PhD researcher) and colleagues. **The headline finding: honeybees do not just quietly take more resources than wild pollinators — they actively bump into, chase and displace them from flowers.** The study documented **300 competitive interactions** between flower visitors (honeybees, bumblebees, solitary bees, hoverflies, and "other" including butterflies, beetles and wasps) foraging on bramble and ivy near honeybee colonies. Honeybees won most encounters across both plant species, and areas with higher honeybee concentrations had fewer wild pollinators.

## Key facts

- **UK registered beekeepers more than doubled**: from 19,000 (2010) to 47,000 (2025) per the National Bee Unit — driven by "save the bees" campaigns
- **300 competitive interactions documented** over two years on bramble and ivy in Cambridgeshire
- **Honeybees won most encounters** across both plant species
- **In honeybee-dominated interactions, the honeybee displaced or chased other flower visitors in ~20% of cases**
- **Other flower visitors actively avoided honeybees** in **28.4% of interactions on bramble, 35.6% on ivy**
- Study documents **"interference competition"** — direct aggression — rather than just the previously-known **"exploitation competition"** (using up resources first)
- **Bumblebees sometimes displace others too, but probably accidentally** (clumsy flight), per Buckle: *"They bumble along the patch and then they'll mistakenly bump into another pollinator and it just falls off a flower"*
- **The only pollinator that holds its own against honeybees in the study: the ivy bee** (first recorded in UK in 2001; specialises on ivy nectar) — it was observed displacing and chasing honeybees
- **Higher temperatures = more competitive interactions** (nectar dries up, plants heat-stressed). UK mean summer temperatures could rise by up to **4°C by 2080**, making this worse

## Notable quotes

- **Victoria Buckle** (Anglia Ruskin, lead author) on direct aggression: *"That's important because, of course, it means that the wild pollinators are facing a challenge from both those types of competition from honeybees."*
- **Buckle** on honeybee behaviour: *"With the honeybees, the aggression was more overt. You could tell that they're specifically trying to move the other one off the flower."*
- **Buckle** on why: *"Some castes of bees can be guard bees, so they have that capacity within them to show threat and aggression, to guard their hives and to look after the brood."*
- **Buckle** on beekeeper intent: *"A lot of beekeepers are environmentally minded and they want to make sure that they're not impinging or having an impact on other pollinators. This research is important because it helps guide our planning and best practices for conservation."*

## Why it matters

Three things:

1. **A "save the bees" paradox, now with mechanism.** The urban beekeeping boom (UK beekeepers 2.5× in 15 years) has been well-meaning, but prior research had already flagged indirect competition. This study provides direct, observed evidence that honeybees actively displace wild pollinators. The implication: **beekeeping, especially at high hive density, can harm the biodiversity it was meant to help**.
2. **Climate change makes this worse, not better.** Hotter summers drive more aggressive competition at flowers because nectar is scarcer. The 4°C-by-2080 projection folds pollinator competition into climate adaptation planning.
3. **Specific guidance for conservation planning.** The finding is practical, not just descriptive: hive density matters, flower density matters, and conservation planners should treat managed honeybees as one more pressure on wild pollinators rather than as an unambiguous benefit.

## Caveats

- **One UK region (Cambridgeshire), two plant species (bramble, ivy).** Findings may not generalise directly to other regions or forage types.
- **The ivy bee counter-example matters.** It shows that some wild pollinators can hold their own against honeybees. The policy question is whether most wild pollinators in UK landscapes resemble the ivy bee (resilient) or the bumblebees and solitary bees in this study (displaced).
- **The study does not quantify how much honeybee competition reduces wild pollinator populations overall** — just the mechanism and the per-interaction outcomes.

## Tone and angle

Straight environmental-science reporting. The researcher-quoted framing is diplomatic to beekeepers ("a lot of beekeepers are environmentally minded"), but the data is unambiguous. The Guardian piece links to prior reporting on the UK beekeeping boom and prior research on indirect competition.
