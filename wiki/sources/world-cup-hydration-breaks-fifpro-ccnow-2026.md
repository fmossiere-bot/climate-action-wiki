# The World Cup Story (Almost) No One Told

**Source:** Covering Climate Now, 23 July 2026. https://coveringclimatenow.org/from-us-story/the-world-cup-story-almost-no-one-told/

## What it is

Covering Climate Now, a collaboration of news outlets working to improve climate reporting, looks at how US media covered the new mid-half hydration breaks at the 2026 World Cup. It finds that most coverage never explained the climate science behind the breaks. The one exception it highlights is Vanessa Hauc, the environment reporter at the Spanish-language network Telemundo.

## Why it matters

The piece gives precise heat limits for football. The players' union FIFPRO proposed breaks at a **wet-bulb globe temperature** (WBGT, a measure that combines heat, humidity, sun and wind to show how hard it is for the body to cool down) of 26°C and stopping play at 28°C. FIFA wanted breaks only at 32°C. It also records the actual match-day heat at the 2026 World Cup.

It is a useful example of how to report the climate link well. Players, referees and fans are the ones who feel the heat. Telemundo showed that explaining why the breaks exist, as well as reporting that they happened, helps audiences connect what they see on the pitch with a warming world.

## Key facts

- 2026 was the first World Cup in 96 years to bring in compulsory hydration breaks in the middle of each half.
- FIFPRO commissioned a study on extreme heat and players' health and performance after the 2025 FIFA Club World Cup, where a referee fainted on the pitch. Argentina's Enzo Fernandez described lying on the ground "really dizzy" and called playing in that heat "very dangerous".
- FIFPRO recommended breaks at a WBGT of **26°C** (about 79°F) and suspending play at 28°C (about 82°F). FIFA wanted breaks only at 32°C (about 90°F). Hydration breaks were the eventual compromise.
- During Argentina against Cape Verde in Miami on 3 July, a broadcast noted that the game-time temperature of 89°F "can feel closer to 101°F" once humidity and sun are counted. Colombia against Ghana in Kansas City the same evening was played at 88°F and 70% humidity.
- Wet-bulb heat "makes it harder for the body to cool itself, causing core body temperature to rise and increasing the risk of exhaustion, dehydration, and even heat stroke".
- Other sports are adapting. Professional tennis allows cooling breaks above 30°C WBGT, and the 2026 LA Marathon let runners officially finish at mile 18 instead of 26 because of extreme heat.
- A Yale Program on Climate Change Communication survey (released around July 2026) found that 74% of Americans are interested in news stories about climate change. Latino audiences have long been more concerned about climate change than the US population as a whole.

## The money question

FIFA president Gianni Infantino denied that the hydration breaks were about money, saying there was "no additional revenue for FIFA" because TV deals had been signed in advance. Covering Climate Now calls this technically true but incomplete. The extra minutes of advertising make future World Cup TV contracts more valuable.

## Wider heat and smoke figures

The same Covering Climate Now briefing gathered other findings. Rapid attribution studies, which work out how much climate change contributed to a specific event, found that human-caused warming was responsible for **40-70% of the deaths** in recent major European heatwaves. Wildfire smoke is estimated to cause 1.5 million deaths a year worldwide. The 2023 Canadian wildfire season alone was linked to an estimated 80,000 smoke-related deaths, 20,000 of them in Europe.
