# Europe's supermarket shelves packed with 'misleading' claims about recycled plastic packaging

**Source:** The Guardian, Stefano Valentino, 27 January 2026. https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/jan/27/recycled-plastic-packaging-claims-misleading-say-experts

The article is part of an IJ4EU cross-border investigation coordinated by Ludovica Jona, with Voxeurop, Mediapart, Altreconomia, Público, Investigative Reporting Denmark, Deutsche Welle and others.

## What it is

This investigation looks at how brands such as Kraft's Heinz Beanz and Mondelēz's Philadelphia can call their plastic packaging "recycled" or "circular" when the material is still almost entirely made from fossil fuels.

It works through accounting rules that are legal within the industry. The main one is **mass-balance bookkeeping**, helped along by a second method called avoided emissions. Both sit on top of chemical recycling by pyrolysis, which heats plastic waste into an oil. That process yields only a small amount of recycled material, and it has to be mixed with fresh petroleum before it can be used. The EU is set to make the practice legal in 2026, and the UK in 2027.

## Why it matters

Rules that require a share of recycled content in packaging are one of Europe's main tools against plastic pollution and for cutting demand for new oil. If companies can meet those targets on paper through mass-balance accounting while the plastic stays almost entirely fossil-based, nothing changes in the real world. Meanwhile, as the IEA points out, oil companies are turning to plastic as their next big market.

For shoppers who pick a product because the label says "recycled", this is unfair. The piece traces the loophole step by step, from the chemistry to the accounting and the lobbying, and shows who gains at each stage.

## Key facts

- Saudi Aramco, through its petrochemical subsidiary Sabic, supplies the plastic used by many of the brands making these claims. Aramco is the world's largest corporate greenhouse gas emitter (over 70 million tonnes to 2023) and opposes production cuts under the UN plastic treaty.
- Pyrolysis oil can make up **at most 5%** of the feedstock going into a plant. It must be mixed with 95% virgin naphtha, a fossil oil product, or it damages the steam-cracking plants.
- Mass-balance bookkeeping lets a company assign the recycled input to chosen batches. So 5% pyrolysis oil across 100 tonnes can be used to certify 5 tonnes as "100% recycled" packaging, even though that packaging contains only fossil feedstock.
- Avoided emissions adds a credit for the emissions that would have been released if the same amount of waste had been burned instead.
- In 2022 Sabic used 2,600 tonnes of pyrolysis oil, against about 4 million tonnes of naphtha fed into its European cracking plants in the Netherlands. The recycled share is well under 5%.
- Sabic's own life cycle assessment (LCA) admits the process emits 6–8% more than making plastic from fossil fuel. Only the credit for avoided burning makes the result look positive, at about 2 kg less CO₂ per kg of recycled plastic.
- EU packaging rules (the PPWR) that allow mass balance take effect in 2026. UK rules allowing mass balance under the plastic packaging tax follow in 2027.

## How the system holds together

The LCAs were "critically reviewed" by parties that included the co-founder of Plastic Energy, Sabic's main feedstock supplier. Certificates are issued by the International Sustainability and Carbon Certification (ISCC) platform, which is run by the industry.

The petrochemical industry has stepped up its lobbying in the EU and is rushing to sign supply deals with pyrolysis oil producers. The IEA's World Energy Outlook 2025 expects plastic to become an important source of growth for oil companies as demand for oil as fuel falls.

Sabic and Plastic Energy both declined to share their full LCAs, and the brands named in the investigation did not respond.

## Voices in the piece

Helmut Maurer, a former senior environment expert at the European Commission, says: *"The whole process is labelled as plastic recycling, while fossil fuel use expands because virgin feedstock must be added."*

Lauriane Veillard of Zero Waste Europe puts it from the shopper's side: *"This is unfair to consumers: recycled content should be physically part of the final product."*

Peter Quicker, who studies emission control in waste management at Aachen University, doubts the assessments themselves: *"LCA documents serve no purpose other than advertising, because companies control the parameters to achieve desired results."*

Margaux Le Gallou, senior programme manager at the environmental group ECOS, says the overstated carbon savings "follow the downstream value chain, amplified by mass-balance credit, to packaged products, potentially making consumer brands' statements unreliable and misleading."
