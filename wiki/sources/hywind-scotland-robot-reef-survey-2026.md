# Robot survey finds 121 forms of life on Hywind Scotland's floating turbines

**Source:** EcoPortal, Kelly Lippke, 13 August 2026. https://www.ecoportal.net/en/robot-inspected-underwater-components/32017/
The article draws on a peer-reviewed study: Karlsson, R. et al., *Artificial hard-substrate colonization in the offshore Hywind Scotland Pilot Park*, Wind Energ. Sci., 7, 801–814, 2022. https://doi.org/10.5194/wes-7-801-2022

## What it is

Equinor's **Hywind Scotland Pilot Park** is a group of five floating wind turbines 12 miles (about 19 km) off the Scottish coast. Most offshore turbines stand on the seabed. These float on platforms held in place by mooring cables and suction anchors that reach hundreds of feet down.

During a routine maintenance check, a remote-controlled underwater robot inspected 41 underwater parts across the five turbines. Biologists logged every living thing they could see on the mooring lines, steel frames and anchors. They counted 121 different forms of sea life.

## Why it matters

Earlier evidence that wind farms can act as reefs came from turbines fixed to the seabed, such as the Formosa wind farm in Taiwan, where researchers found 86 new fish species (see [Taiwan offshore wind and reef fish](taiwan-offshore-wind-reef-fish-2026.md)). Floating turbines are how offshore wind will move into deeper water, so it matters whether they do the same.

This survey suggests they do, in a colder North Atlantic setting, and even with slow-growing corals. Because fishing boats cannot drag nets along the seabed inside the wind farm, the site has become an accidental refuge where marine life can settle undisturbed.

## Key facts

- Five floating turbines and 41 underwater components were surveyed, 12 miles off the Scottish coast.
- The robot recorded **121 distinct forms of sea life** across the installations.
- Near the surface there were dense clusters of orange starfish, spiny sea urchins and thick mats of plumose sea anemones.
- Deeper down, near the suction anchors, there were squat lobsters, brown crabs and large fish sheltering in gaps in the steel.
- Researchers found soft corals and possibly deep-sea stony corals growing on the frame. Both grow slowly and are easily damaged.
- Together the structures form an artificial reef reaching roughly 400 feet (about 120 metres) down.
- What grows depends on depth: fast-growing species that need light near the surface, and cold-water species suited to the dark further down.

## Limits of the findings

This is one site with five turbines. Studies over several years are still needed to show whether the platforms create new marine life or simply draw existing animals into one place.
