# 6 Pressing Questions About Beef and Climate Change, Answered (WRI)

**Source:** World Resources Institute (WRI), Richard Waite, Tim Searchinger, Janet Ranganathan and Jessica Zionts. First published 2019, updated March 2022. https://www.wri.org/insights/6-pressing-questions-about-beef-and-climate-change-answered

## What it is

An explainer from the World Resources Institute, set out as six questions and answers. It draws on WRI's *World Resources Report: Creating a Sustainable Food Future*.

It walks a non-expert through the climate case for eating less beef in countries that eat a lot of it, without asking anyone to become vegetarian or vegan. The piece is a few years old, but many of its numbers are still the standard ones used when people write about food and climate.

## Why it matters

When journalists need a clear, sourced answer to "how much does beef matter?", this is often where they go. Its target of about **50 calories of beef and lamb a day** is one of the few concrete diet goals that squares feeding 10 billion people with keeping deforestation under control.

It also calms a polarised argument. The question is often framed as "do we all have to go vegan?", which puts many people off. WRI shows that a moderate cut in the countries that eat the most is enough to make the numbers work, which leaves room for people to change at their own pace.

## Key facts

- Beef production, counting both the farming and the land cleared for it, caused about 3 billion tonnes of CO2-eq in 2010. That is roughly India's total emissions, and about 7% of global emissions that year (FAO 2017 estimate). The figure is conservative because land-use change is only partly counted.
- Global demand for ruminant meat (beef, lamb and goat) rose 25% between 2000 and 2019. Expanding pasture was the leading direct cause of deforestation in the first two decades of this century.
- Without action, pasture could expand by 400 million hectares, an area larger than India, between 2010 and 2050.
- Per gram of protein, beef needs about 20 times more land and emits about 20 times more greenhouse gas than beans.
- If people in high-consuming countries cut ruminant meat to about 50 calories a day (about 1.5 burgers per person a week), the world would need almost no extra farmland, even with 10 billion people by 2050. That is roughly half the US average and 25% below the European average.
- US beef eating per person has already fallen by one-third since the 1970s, but the decline needs to be 1.5 times faster across high-consuming regions to reach the 50-calorie target by 2050.
- At COP26 in 2021, leaders pledged to cut methane by 30% and end deforestation by 2030. Beef sits where both goals meet.

## Making cattle farming more efficient

WRI also points to ways of producing beef and milk with fewer emissions. In Colombia, silvopasture, which mixes trees with grazing land, can quadruple the number of cows per acre while cutting methane per pound of beef. In Kenya, dairy trials that fed cows napier grass and the high-protein shrub Calliandra cut methane per litre of milk by 8–60%. Feed additives such as 3-nitrooxypropanol (3-NOP) and seaweed can also reduce how much cattle burp.

## Limits of the figures

The article was last updated in March 2022 and uses data up to about 2019–2021. More recent sources, such as the FAO 2026 report already used elsewhere in this wiki, show that meat consumption has kept rising. Newer work, including from Terra.do, also places diet change nearer the top of the list of things individuals can do. The piece is still a sound reference for the beef-specific case.
