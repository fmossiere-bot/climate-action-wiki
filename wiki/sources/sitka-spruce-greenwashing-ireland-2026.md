# Sitka spruce industry book: greenwashing in Irish schools

**Source:** The Irish Times, Caroline O'Doherty and Órla Ryan, 22 April 2026. https://www.irishtimes.com/environment/2026/04/22/wildlife-experts-call-for-misleading-timber-industry-book-to-be-removed-from-schools/

## What it is

An Irish Times article about a children's book, *Sitka Spruce – the Amazing Timber Tree*, sent out to Irish primary schools. Commercial forestry groups produced it, and Michael Healy-Rae wrote the foreword while he was minister of state for forestry. The Irish Wildlife Trust (IWT) wants it withdrawn, saying it presents one-sided industry messaging to children as environmental education.

## Why it matters

Children tend to trust what they read in school. When a book paid for by an industry tells them that a commercial plantation is a thriving forest, that is **greenwashing in education**: marketing dressed up as fact.

The story also shows a gap in the system. Nothing currently stops industry groups from sending promotional material straight to Irish schools. And it reflects a long-running tension in Irish forestry between fast-growing commercial Sitka plantations and the goal of more native woodland and richer wildlife.

## Key facts

- The book was first produced by Morgan Sindall Construction in the UK and then adapted for Ireland.
- The Irish version is funded by the Society of Irish Foresters, the Irish Timber Council and SEEFA (Social, Economic, Environmental Forestry Association).
- The foreword is by Michael Healy-Rae, who had recently resigned as minister of state for forestry.
- The book shows Sitka spruce plantations full of wildlife, with trees taken out one at a time while the "woodland flourishes".
- In reality, Sitka plantations are widely described as **ecological dead zones**. They crowd out most other plants, support few animals, make soils and waterways more acidic, and are usually clear-felled.
- The book also presents Sitka as a carbon sink. That claim is disputed in many Irish cases, where the trees are planted on bogs that would store far more carbon if left intact.
- The Department of Education distanced itself from the book, saying it has no role in approving or endorsing material sent directly to schools.
- The IWT called for the book to be withdrawn straight away and its messaging revised.
- The Society of Irish Foresters defended the book. It says the criticism applies to older single-species plantations, and that new standards require 20% broadleaf trees and up to 15% of the area set aside for biodiversity.
