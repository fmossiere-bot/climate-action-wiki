# Planes flying over the Atlantic will be re-routed to avoid contrails

**Source:** New Scientist, Madeleine Cuff, 18 August 2026

## What it is

This is New Scientist's report on **Operation Blue Skies**, a trial that will steer planes away from the conditions that create contrails, the white trails that form behind aircraft at high altitude. The BBC covered the same trial on the same day (see the [BBC companion summary](contrails-operation-blue-skies-bbc-2026.md)). The New Scientist piece leans more on the research side.

Marc Shapiro of Contrails.org, a research organisation taking part in the project, explains the method. Forecasters predict where long-lasting contrails that warm the climate are likely to form, and planes are then re-routed around those patches of sky.

## Why it matters

Contrails are an easy part of aviation's impact to overlook, because they are not about fuel or CO2. Yet they add a real share of the warming that flying causes. If small changes in altitude can prevent many of them, that is a practical way to cut aviation's impact while cleaner fuels and planes are still years away.

The article backs up the BBC's figures and adds some technical detail, so the two reports can be cited together for the same facts.

## Key facts

- Contrails reflect sunlight during the day, which cools, but they also trap heat leaving the Earth, which warms. Overall the effect is **warming**, estimated at around a third of aviation's total climate impact.
- The trial starts in November 2026. Air traffic controllers in the Shanwick airspace over the North Atlantic will advise pilots to change altitude to avoid conditions where contrails form.
- The £5m project is described as the world's first contrail avoidance trial across a whole airspace. Earlier work adjusted the flight plans of individual airlines before take-off, but never covered an entire airspace where flights cannot opt out.
- Its figures match the BBC's: about 5% of global contrail warming comes from Shanwick, a typical altitude change is 2,000ft (600m), each re-routed plane burns about 100kg of extra fuel, and earlier trials with single airlines cut contrail formation by 50-60%.
- Researchers from the University of Cambridge and Imperial College London will analyse the data from the trial.
