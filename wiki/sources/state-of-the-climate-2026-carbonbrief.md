# State of the climate: 2026 likely to tie with 2024 as the warmest year on record

**Source:** Carbon Brief, Zeke Hausfather, 3 November 2026. https://www.carbonbrief.org/state-of-the-climate-2026-likely-to-tie-with-2024-as-the-warmest-year-on-record

## What it is

Carbon Brief's quarterly "state of the climate" update, written by climate scientist Zeke Hausfather, pulls together the latest global temperature records, ocean conditions and sea ice data. This edition covers the period to the end of September 2026.

Its main finding is that 2026 is virtually certain to be the warmest or second-warmest year on record. Carbon Brief's best estimate is **1.52°C above pre-industrial levels**, which would tie with 2024's record of 1.52°C. It gives 2026 a 67% chance of beating 2024 across the average of six global temperature datasets.

The reason for the late surge is a fast-strengthening **El Niño**, the warm phase of a natural cycle in the tropical Pacific that releases ocean heat into the air and temporarily pushes global temperatures up. This one is on course to be the strongest ever measured, and its biggest effect will come in 2027.

## Why it matters

Human greenhouse gas emissions cause effectively all of the long-term warming. El Niño and its cool counterpart La Niña decide whether a given year lands a little above or below that rising trend. A record El Niño on top of today's warming means 2027 is very likely to set a new record, at around 1.7°C. That is a reminder of where the long-term average is heading.

It also matters how the records are reported. In January, the agencies that publish global temperatures will probably disagree on whether 2026 beat 2024. That is not a sign that the science is shaky. The datasets fill gaps such as the poles in different ways, and the difference between first and second place is a few hundredths of a degree.

## Key facts

- August 2026 was the warmest August on record in all six major datasets, about 0.12°C above the previous record. September, at around 1.62°C, was the second-warmest, behind 2023.
- January to September 2026 averaged about 1.45°C above pre-industrial levels, second only to 2024 (about 0.06°C warmer).
- 2026 has a 93% chance of ending above 1.5°C, which would make it the second year above that line after 2024. A single year above 1.5°C is not a breach of the Paris goal, which refers to a long-term average.
- El Niño broke the daily record for the "Niño3.4" region of the Pacific in September. Forecasts (656 runs from 14 models) point to a peak of around 4°C above normal in December, more than 1°C above the previous record. Tree-ring and coral evidence suggests it may be the strongest in a millennium.
- Carbon Brief projects 2027 at around 1.74°C (range 1.54 to 1.95°C), with a 96% chance of being the warmest year on record. There is a 98% chance that 2026 or 2027 sets a new record.
- Around 22% of the Earth's surface had its warmest July to September on record, and about half had one of its five warmest. Only about 0.5% had one of its five coldest.
- Earth's energy imbalance, the gap between the energy the planet absorbs from the sun and what it sends back to space, rose from about 0.53 watts per square metre in 2000-2010 to about 1.25 in 2016-2026, a 2.4-fold increase.

## A record summer in Europe

<!-- tag: eu -->
Western Europe had its hottest summer on record. Copernicus, the EU's climate service, reported an average of 21.7°C, 2.5°C above the 1991-2020 average, beating the record set in 2003. By the end of the season a record 52% of the continent was exposed to "very strong" heat stress. Rivers across Europe had their lowest summer flows since the European Flood Awareness System's records began in 1992, and by early September the area burned in the EU was almost twice the 2006-25 average for the time of year. Europe as a whole had its third-warmest summer, behind 2024 and 2022.

<!-- tag: france, spain -->
France had its warmest July on record, 3.8°C above the 1991-2020 average, and about 85% of France and the Iberian peninsula had a record-warm July to September. Spain then had its warmest September on record, 3.4°C above average, beating the previous record from 1987 by 0.7°C. Switzerland had its hottest summer since records began, averaging 17.2°C.

## Elsewhere in the world

<!-- tag: us, india -->
The contiguous US had its hottest summer and warmest September on record, with about 59% of the country in drought. India's monsoon, which tends to weaken in El Niño years, ended with 12.6% less rain than normal, the driest in 11 years. Overall, 55 countries set national records for September temperatures, including Chile, Colombia, Italy, Kenya, Nigeria, the Philippines, Portugal, Spain, the UAE, the US and Venezuela.

El Niño also reshaped the hurricane season. It suppressed hurricanes in the Atlantic while contributing to a record number of tropical cyclones in the Pacific.

## Sea ice and the energy imbalance

Arctic sea ice reached its yearly minimum on 12 September at 4.60 million square kilometres, tied for 10th lowest in the nearly 48-year satellite record, after setting or tying daily record lows on 39 days earlier in the year. Antarctic sea ice peaked on 14 September at 17.59 million square kilometres, the third lowest on record behind 2023 and 2024, then fell to record daily lows from early October.

The growing energy imbalance means the planet is gathering heat faster, around 90% of it in the oceans, and that warming is speeding up. The highest yearly value so far was 1.79 watts per square metre in 2023, and the 12 months to August 2026 averaged 1.59. Several studies have found the imbalance rising faster than most climate models simulate, for reasons that are not yet clear.

## Limits of the figures

The 2026 and 2027 projections assume that this El Niño affects global temperatures the way past ones did. It is larger than anything in the record the model was trained on. If its effect turns out to be no bigger than the strongest past events, the chance of a 2026 record falls to about 50%. Odds also vary by dataset: from 97% in Berkeley Earth to 18% in Copernicus's ERA5. None of these datasets is "right"; they handle data gaps differently.
