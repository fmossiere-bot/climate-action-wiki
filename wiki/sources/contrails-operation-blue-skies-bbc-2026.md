# AI trial to reroute planes around climate-warming contrails

**Source:** BBC News, Shiona McCallum, 18 August 2026. https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c62em5lpvnjo
**Companion source:** New Scientist, Madeleine Cuff, 18 August 2026, "Planes flying over the Atlantic will be re-routed to avoid contrails"

## What it is

**Operation Blue Skies** is a £5m UK trial. It will test whether AI forecasts can predict where warming contrails are likely to form over the North Atlantic, so that air traffic control can steer planes around those patches of sky.

Contrails are the white lines planes leave behind. They form when hot exhaust meets very cold air high up and turns into ice crystals. Some fade quickly, but the ones that linger trap heat that would otherwise escape to space.

Earlier tests worked airline by airline. This is the world's first trial run across an entire airspace. The partners are Google, the UK government, the Met Office, NATS (the UK's air traffic control service), and researchers at the University of Cambridge and Imperial College London.

## Why it matters

When people think about flying and the climate, they think about fuel. But contrails are estimated to cause around a third of aviation's total warming effect, so they are a big part of the picture that rarely gets mentioned. The wiki's page on [air travel](../sectors/transport/Facts%20about%20Air%20Travel.md) already listed AI contrail avoidance as a possible fix in general terms. This trial turns it into a funded, dated test with real numbers attached, and it is also an example of AI being used to cut emissions rather than only adding to them.

## Key facts

- The trial covers **Shanwick Oceanic airspace**, the eastern North Atlantic corridor. It accounts for about 5% of global contrail warming, mostly in the November–February winter season.
- Contrails are estimated to cause around a third of aviation's total climate warming impact.
- The trial runs over two winters, 2026-27 and 2027-28, with roughly 20-40 test days each, mostly at night when there is less traffic.
- About 10,000 flights are expected to cross the airspace during trial hours each year. Hundreds will be re-routed, enough to give statistically meaningful results.
- Planes will usually be asked to climb or descend by up to 2,000 feet (600 metres), the same kind of routine change pilots already make to avoid turbulence.
- Each re-routed plane burns about 100kg of extra fuel. The added CO2 is "of less than 1%" of the contrail warming avoided, which can reach "tonnes or even tens of tonnes" per flight.
- Funding: £2.65m from the UK Department for Transport and £1.4m from Google (AI research, engineering and computing power).
- Earlier airline-level trials of Google's contrail forecasts cut contrail formation or warming by 50-70%.
- Because instructions come from air traffic control, they apply to every aircraft in the region, with no opt-out.
- The results are meant to be published in academic journals.
