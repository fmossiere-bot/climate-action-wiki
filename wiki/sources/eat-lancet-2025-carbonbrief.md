# EAT-Lancet 2025: what the new report says about climate and diet

**Source:** Carbon Brief Staff, 2 October 2025. https://www.carbonbrief.org/eat-lancet-report-three-key-takeaways-on-climate-and-diet-change/

**Underlying report:** *The EAT-Lancet Commission on healthy, sustainable and just food systems*, Rockström et al., The Lancet Commissions, DOI: 10.1016/S0140-6736(25)01201-2. https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(25)01201-2/abstract

## What it is

This is Carbon Brief's summary of the 2025 EAT-Lancet Commission report on "healthy, sustainable and just food systems". The commission is a group of experts in nutrition, climate, economics, health, social sciences and farming. It first met in 2019, when it proposed the **planetary health diet**: a way of eating meant to be good for people and for the planet.

The 2025 report updates that diet with better modelling of the food system. For the first time it also treats fairness as a goal in its own right. Carbon Brief picks out what it sees as the report's main messages. A plant-rich diet gives the best results for health and climate. Changing food systems could cut emissions and land use a great deal. And social justice has to sit at the centre of that change.

## Why it matters

Since 2019, the planetary health diet has been the usual reference point for what a healthy, climate-friendly diet looks like. The 2025 update builds on more recent data and puts numbers on what changing diets could achieve: a 15% cut in some farm emissions on its own, 20% with less waste and better yields, and 34% when combined with strong climate policy.

It also changes who the question is aimed at. The **richest 30%** of the world's population cause more than 70% of the environmental pressure from food. That makes this less about everyone eating the same way and more about how the wealthiest eat. Meanwhile, more than half the world's population struggles to access a healthy diet.

Carbon Brief also notes the politics. The 2019 report met a coordinated backlash from the meat and dairy industry, partly run by PR firms. Johan Rockström said the commission is "ready to meet that assault" this time.

## Key facts

- Food systems produce about 1/3 (one third) of the greenhouse gases caused by people.
- Even if fossil fuels were fully phased out, "food can on its own push us beyond the 1.5C limit", according to Professor Johan Rockström.
- Switching to the planetary health diet could cut non-CO2 farm emissions (methane and nitrous oxide, N2O) by **15% by 2050**.
- Widespread adoption would need fruit, vegetable and nut production to rise by two-thirds, and would allow livestock meat production to fall by one-third compared with 2020.
- The diet provides about 2,400 kcal a day, built around whole grains, plant oils, nuts and legumes. It still allows about 1 glass of milk a day, and 2 servings of meat and 2 eggs a week.
- Adopting it could prevent around 15 million "avoidable" deaths a year.
- Food systems are the largest contributor to five of the nine planetary boundaries (the safe limits for Earth's systems) that have been crossed.

## What the modelling shows

The report compares several futures to 2050:

- **Business as usual** (a scenario called SSP2-7.0, with 9.6bn people and 2C of warming): farm production rises 37%, non-CO2 farm emissions rise 33%, and farmland expands by an area the size of Mexico.
- **Full switch to the diet**, with food loss and waste halved and farm productivity up 7–10%: non-CO2 farm emissions fall 20% compared with 2020, and farmland shrinks by 3.4m km², an area the size of India.
- **Diet change plus strong climate policy** (carbon pricing, land-use rules, bioenergy, tree planting and renewables): non-CO2 farm emissions fall 34% compared with 2020, and the land saved roughly doubles.

## Limits of the figures

Carbon Brief points out that the diet was designed for health first and sustainability second. The scenarios are models of what could happen if people actually changed how they eat, which is a large assumption in itself.
