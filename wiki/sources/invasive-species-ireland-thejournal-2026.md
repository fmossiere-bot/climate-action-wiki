# Invasive species are spreading across Ireland, and the response is inadequate

**Source:** The Journal (Ireland), Orla ní Dhúill, 24 October 2026. https://www.thejournal.ie/readme/biodiversity-invasive-species-7194702-Oct2026/

## What it is

An investigative essay by Orla ní Dhúill, an environmentalist and journalist on Achill Island. She asks why Ireland's response to invasive species, one of the main drivers of biodiversity loss worldwide, is "so inadequate".

Her central finding is about a new body. The government announced an **Invasive Alien Species Bureau** in May 2026. By late October 2026 it still had no staff and no plan to hire any. That was confirmed in a parliamentary question (PQ 383) put by Social Democrats TD Jennifer Whitmore.

She then describes what happens when community groups try to remove invasive plants themselves. The licensing rules are unclear. Work must be done by a "qualified professional", but nobody defines what that means. There is no dedicated funding. And a licence to move the waste requires an approved disposal site, yet neither the NPWS nor the EPA will provide a list of them.

## Why it matters

Ireland signed up in Montreal (COP15, 2022) to tackle invasive species as part of the Kunming-Montreal biodiversity framework. The Bureau exists on paper. In practice nothing is happening, and volunteers who want to help have no clear way forward.

The "qualified professional" rule shows how a vague requirement can stop action. If the government insists on a qualification for every intervention but never says what counts, the effect is an unspoken instruction to do nothing. ní Dhúill used a parliamentary question and her own correspondence with the agencies to make that visible.

The missing list of waste sites is a specific failure that could be fixed quickly. The NPWS and the EPA each say they have no list and point to the other. A public, official list of approved facilities would unblock most community clearance work.

## Key facts

- Plants make up nearly half of the invasive species of concern in Ireland.
- Examples include rhododendron ponticum (damaging ancient woodland in Killarney), gunnera (choking watercourses on Achill), Asian hornets (a growing worry), Japanese knotweed, Spanish bluebells (still sold in Irish garden centres even though they are on the third schedule list), giant hogweed (dangerous to people) and box tree moth (destroying box hedges across Europe since 2007).
- Minister O'Sullivan announced the Invasive Alien Species Bureau in May 2026. By October 2026 it had **no staff and no hiring plan**, according to PQ 383, dated 14 July 2026.
- The Bureau appears to be a rebrand of existing projects run by the National Parks and Wildlife Service (NPWS) and the National Biodiversity Data Centre (NBDC).
- In February 2026 Collette O'Flynn, the NBDC's invasive species officer, told journalist Pádraic Fogarty that the NBDC's job is to collect and organise sightings, not to intervene, and that she was not clear who was responsible for removal.
- The legal basis is the EU regulation's third schedule list of species. Breeding, transporting, releasing or spreading them without a licence is an offence.
- Landowners can use the weedkiller glyphosate on private land without a licence. For publicly funded clearance, a professional pesticide licence is needed, which takes a short training course. This is the only "qualified" status that is clearly defined.
- There is no equivalent licence for removing plants by hand, without chemicals.
- The Local Environmental Action Fund (LEAF) could in theory pay up to €9,000 for a project, but only if a "qualified" professional sends the invoice. When ní Dhúill asked what "qualified" means, she got no firm answer.
- The NPWS licence to transport the waste requires an "approved" disposal site, such as a specialist landfill or incinerator. The NPWS has no list and told her to ask the EPA. The EPA has no list either. Applicants are told to get a letter from a facility saying it holds the right licences.

## Voices in the piece

ní Dhúill describes the frustration of trying to get help: "Attempts to seek out advice are met with 'do nothing' or that only 'qualified' persons can do the work of eradicating a given species even in areas of the country so badly affected that to do nothing holds just as high a risk."

She ends with a warning about time: "Invasive species will not wait for government departments to settle questions of responsibility, nor will they stop spreading while community groups struggle to establish which licences they need to weed them out."

## Limits of the evidence

This is one journalist's experience, based on her own correspondence with the NPWS and EPA licensing offices and on the parliamentary question. The departments' position may change, especially if the piece draws political attention. The parliamentary question dates from July 2026, so staffing at the Bureau could have changed by late October. ní Dhúill does not confirm the current numbers.
