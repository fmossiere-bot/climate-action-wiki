# Invasive species spreading across Ireland — the inadequate response

**Source:** The Journal (Ireland), Orla ní Dhúill, 24 October 2026 — https://www.thejournal.ie/readme/biodiversity-invasive-species-7194702-Oct2026/

## What it is

Investigative essay from Irish environmentalist and journalist **Orla ní Dhúill** (Achill Island) on why Ireland's response to invasive species — one of the key drivers of global biodiversity loss — is "so inadequate". **The headline finding: the government announced an Invasive Alien Species Bureau in May 2026; by the time of the piece in late October 2026, it still has no personnel and no hiring plan, confirmed via a parliamentary question (PQ 383) submitted by Social Democrats TD Jennifer Whitmore.** ní Dhúill then walks through the practical obstacles facing community groups who try to remove invasive species themselves: unclear licensing rules, "qualified professional" requirements with no defined meaning, no funding scheme, and a licensing system that requires an approved waste facility that neither the NPWS nor the EPA will provide a list of.

## Key facts

- **Plants make up nearly half** of Ireland's invasive species of concern
- Major examples: **rhododendron ponticum** (degrading ancient woodlands in Killarney), **gunnera** (congesting watercourses in Achill), **Asian hornets** (growing concern), **Japanese knotweed**, **Spanish bluebells** (still sold in Irish garden centres despite being on the third schedule list), **giant hogweed** (dangerous to human health), **box tree moth** (wrecking box hedges across Europe since 2007)
- **The Invasive Alien Species Bureau** was announced by **Minister O'Sullivan in May 2026**. By October 2026 it has **no staff and no hiring plan** (PQ 383, 14 July 2026)
- The Bureau appears to be a rebrand of existing projects between the **National Parks and Wildlife Service (NPWS)** and the **National Biodiversity Data Centre (NBDC)**
- **Collette O'Flynn** (NBDC invasive species officer) told journalist Pádraic Fogarty in February 2026 that the NBDC's role is to receive and organise sightings, not intervene, and that she was not clear who was responsible for removal
- The legal framework rests on the **EU regulation third-schedule species list** — breeding, transporting, releasing or dispersing without a licence is an offence
- **Glyphosate** can be used by landowners on private land without a licence; professional use (for public-funded clearance projects) requires a short-training professional pesticide licence — this is the only "qualified" status that is clearly defined
- **No equivalent licence exists for manual (non-chemical) removal**
- **Local Environmental Action Fund (LEAF)** could theoretically provide up to **€9,000** for a project — but only if a "qualified" professional invoices it. When ní Dhúill asked what "qualified" means, no firm answer was given
- The **NPWS waste-transport licence** requires an "approved" disposal site (specialist landfill or incinerator). **NPWS has no list of approved sites and told ní Dhúill to ask the EPA. The EPA also has no list.** Applicants are told to get a letter from a facility claiming it has the appropriate licences

## Notable quotes

- **ní Dhúill**: *"Attempts to seek out advice are met with 'do nothing' or that only 'qualified' persons can do the work of eradicating a given species even in areas of the country so badly affected that to do nothing holds just as high a risk."*
- *"Invasive species will not wait for government departments to settle questions of responsibility, nor will they stop spreading while community groups struggle to establish which licences they need to weed them out."*

## Why it matters

Three things:

1. **A specific policy-paralysis case study in Ireland.** Ireland committed in Montreal (COP15, 2022) to tackle invasive species as part of the Kunming-Montreal Framework. The Bureau exists on paper. In practice, there is no implementation and no clear path forward for the community groups trying to help.
2. **The "qualified professional" bureaucratic trap.** When government does not define a term but requires it for every intervention, it functions as an unspoken "do nothing" instruction. ní Dhúill's parliamentary question and freedom-of-information approach is the exact method needed to make this visible.
3. **The waste-facility list vacuum is a specific, fixable administrative failure.** Both the NPWS and the EPA deny having a list, each pointing to the other. For a reader who wants to help, this is actionable: a public, authoritative list of approved waste facilities would unblock most community action.

## Caveats

- **One journalist's experience.** ní Dhúill's piece is based on her own correspondence with NPWS and EPA licensing offices plus the parliamentary question. Official departmental response may evolve, especially if the piece gains policy traction.
- **The parliamentary question is from July 2026.** The staffing situation at the Bureau could have changed by late October; ní Dhúill does not confirm current personnel.
