# Forgotten indirect greenhouse gases cause 15% of warming

**Source:** New Scientist, Alec Luhn, 11 June 2026. https://appuk.newscientist.com/2026/06/11/2530049/content.html

**Peer-reviewed study:** Science, DOI: 10.1126/science.aee5790, co-authored by Ilissa Ocko (Spark Climate Solutions).

## What it is

A New Scientist report on new research about **indirect greenhouse gases**. These are gases such as carbon monoxide (CO) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that do not trap much heat themselves, but set off chemical reactions that do. The researchers argue they should be included in national climate targets and in the action plans countries submit under the Paris Agreement.

## Why it matters

These gases have been left out of most climate policy, yet they have done a real share of the warming. They also disappear quickly, within hours to a few years, whereas CO₂ lingers for centuries. That means cutting them would slow the pace of warming fast, which buys time while the slower work of cutting CO₂ goes on.

Many of the sources are close to home: gas boilers, older cars, burning fields and the solvents in paints and cleaning products. Tackling them would also clean up the air people breathe.

## Key facts

- Indirect greenhouse gases, mainly CO and VOCs, have caused about **15% of all warming** since pre-industrial times. That is double the contribution of nitrous oxide.
- Together, carbon monoxide, VOCs and black carbon (soot) have caused about 0.3°C of warming.
- They work in two ways. CO and VOCs react in the air to form ozone near the ground, which traps heat. They also use up hydroxyl radicals, natural "cleaning" molecules that would otherwise break down methane, so methane lasts longer.
- They break down within hours to a few years, unlike CO₂, which lasts centuries. Cutting them would quickly slow the rate of warming.
- Few countries include these gases in their emissions targets, and Paris Agreement action plans do not mention them.
- The main sources are incomplete burning of fossil fuels (gas boilers, older vehicles), burning grasslands and forests for farming, and paints and cleaning solvents.
- Nitrogen oxides (NOx) play a mixed role. Overall they cool the climate, but locally they warm it by producing ozone.

## A warning about hydrogen

The researchers flag a risk in the switch to hydrogen. If cutting emissions relies heavily on burning hydrogen rather than using batteries, hydrogen leaking and being vented could add 0.1°C of warming by 2100.

## Researchers quoted

The study's co-author Ilissa Ocko works at Spark Climate Solutions. The piece also draws on atmospheric chemists Alex Archibald of the University of Cambridge and Alastair Lewis of the University of York.
