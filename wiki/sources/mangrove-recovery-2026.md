# Mangrove forests are healing after decades of human destruction

**Source:** BBC News (Climate & Science), Matt McGrath and Esme Stallard, 5 June 2026. https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cn4pk07npvvo

## What it is

BBC News reports on a study published in 2026. It finds that since 2010 the world's mangrove forests have been gaining more area than they lose, after decades of decline.

According to the study, the recovery comes from forests growing back naturally once clearing slows down, stronger legal protection, and a change in how people see mangroves after disasters such as the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami.

## Why it matters

Mangroves are salt-tolerant trees that grow along tropical coasts. They shelter coastlines from storms and waves, give nursery grounds to fish, and **store up to five times more carbon** per hectare than forests on land.

For a long time the story was one of loss, as mangroves were cleared for fish farms, farmland and building. This study suggests the tide has turned in many places. The recovery is uneven, though. Some regions are still losing mangroves fast, and the communities living there bear the cost.

## Key facts

- From the 1980s to 2010, over 12,000 sq km of mangroves were cleared across Asia, Africa and the Americas.
- Since 2010, net losses have fallen to roughly 849 sq km, and the world is now gaining more mangrove area than it loses.
- Mangroves store up to five times more carbon per hectare than land-based forests.
- The share of closed-canopy mangroves, the densest and most carbon-rich kind, has grown by nearly 20% since the 1980s.
- Indonesia and Myanmar have seen the most recovery. West and Central Africa, especially the Niger Delta, remain hotspots of destruction.
- Tropical cyclones are still a major threat. In the Niger Delta, oil pollution from pipelines is devastating.
- New mapping with Landsat satellites found far more regrowth than earlier assessments had picked up.
