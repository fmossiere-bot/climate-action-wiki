# Europe's most critical wetlands mapped: a 10-metre Nature study

**Source:** Phys.org press release based on University of Copenhagen and Global Wetland Center research, 15 July 2026. https://phys.org/news/2026-07-europe-critical-wetlands-climate-action.html

**Peer-reviewed study:** *Nature* (2026), Kovács GM et al., *Highly fragmented European wetlands with uneven restoration needs*, DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-10760-9

**Public map viewer:** https://ee-gmkovacs.projects.earthengine.app/view/european-wetland-types

## What it is

A press summary of new research in *Nature* from the Global Wetland Center at the University of Copenhagen. The work was led by postdoctoral researcher Gyula Máté Kovács with Associate Professor Stéphanie Horion.

The team used satellite images sharp enough to show details 10 metres across, and machine learning to sort them. The result is the **first consistent, detailed map of Europe's wetlands**, covering six types of wetland in 38 European countries. It shows where restoring wetlands would do the most for carbon and for wildlife.

That makes it directly useful for the EU's Nature Restoration Law, which requires member states to restore at least 30% of wetlands that are not in good condition by 2030.

## Why it matters

Until now, countries have defined wetlands in their own ways. What counts as "peatland" in Denmark, Scotland or Poland has never matched, which made it hard to compare progress or add it up across borders. This map uses one set of definitions for the whole continent, so the EU can check national restoration reports on the same basis.

The study also puts a number on what has already been lost: up to about 5 billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent released from damaged wetlands. That bill keeps growing for every year a drained wetland stays drained. Restoring wetlands also brings back wildlife, which planting trees or building solar farms alone does not.

Countries writing their national restoration plans can use the map as a starting point for picking priority sites. The team's next step is a global version.

## Key facts

- Half of Europe's wetlands have already disappeared through drainage, farming and extraction of resources such as peat.
- The map covers six wetland types (inland marshes, peatbogs, salt marshes, salines, intertidal flats, and moors and heathlands) across 38 European countries.
- It works at a resolution of 10 metres, much finer than earlier continent-wide maps.
- 27–33% of wetlands sit in patches smaller than 25 hectares, and 7–11% in patches under 1 hectare. Coarser maps miss many of these small sites.
- Around one fifth of Europe's wetlands are heavily affected by human activity. Inland marshes are the most disturbed.
- **Peatlands** matter most for the climate. They store large amounts of carbon when healthy but can release a lot when damaged. Most are in Northern Europe.
- Up to 5 billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent may already have escaped from degraded European wetland soils, compared with if they had been left alone. That is roughly 1.5 years of the EU's total CO2 emissions.

## Limits of the figures

The authors are careful to separate wetland types. Coastal marshes matter, but they do not store as much carbon as peatlands. They also distinguish wetlands that are heavily affected from those still in acceptable condition. The 5 billion tonne figure is an upper estimate ("up to"), not a precise count.
