# Europe's Most Critical Wetlands Mapped — 10m Nature study

**Source:** Phys.org press release based on University of Copenhagen / Global Wetland Center research, 15 July 2026 — https://phys.org/news/2026-07-europe-critical-wetlands-climate-action.html
**Peer-reviewed study:** *Nature* (2026) — Kovács GM et al., *Highly fragmented European wetlands with uneven restoration needs*, DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-10760-9
**Public map viewer:** https://ee-gmkovacs.projects.earthengine.app/view/european-wetland-types

## What it is

A press summary of new *Nature* research from the **Global Wetland Center at the University of Copenhagen**, led by postdoctoral researcher **Gyula Máté Kovács** with **Associate Professor Stéphanie Horion**. Using **10-metre-resolution satellite imagery and machine learning**, the team has produced the **first harmonised, high-resolution map of Europe's wetlands** — covering **six categories across 38 European countries**. The map identifies where the greatest **carbon and biodiversity payoff** from restoration lies, and it directly supports the EU's **Nature Restoration Law**, which requires member states to restore **at least 30% of wetlands not in good condition by 2030**.

## Key facts cited

- **Half of Europe's wetlands** have already disappeared through drainage, cultivation and resource extraction
- New map covers **six wetland categories** — inland marshes, peatbogs, salt marshes, salines, intertidal flats, and moors & heathlands — across **38 European countries**
- Spatial resolution: **10 metres** — significantly finer than earlier continental mapping
- **27–33% of wetlands** occur in contiguous areas **smaller than 25 hectares**; **7–11%** are found in patches under 1 hectare — meaning many of the smallest wetlands are missed by coarser mapping efforts
- Around **one fifth of Europe's wetlands are highly affected by human activities**; **inland marshes** are the most heavily disturbed
- **Peatlands** are the most critical type to restore for climate outcomes — highly effective carbon stores but potentially significant carbon losers when disturbed; concentrated in Northern Europe
- Cumulative estimate: up to **5 billion metric tonnes of CO₂-equivalent** soil carbon may have already been released from degraded European wetlands compared to a scenario in which they had remained undisturbed — **roughly equivalent to 1.5 years of total EU CO₂ emissions**

## Why it matters

Two things make this a landmark. First, it is **the first cross-Europe wetland map that harmonises national definitions** — what counts as "peatland" in Denmark, Scotland or Poland has never been consistent, which has made restoration accounting and comparison across borders unreliable. Second, it puts a specific carbon number on inaction: **~5 billion tonnes CO₂-eq** is the price already paid, and it grows every year that degraded wetlands stay degraded. Restoration also generates biodiversity co-benefits that solar-only or forest-only offset strategies do not.

The map is genuinely useful for policy: countries submitting national restoration plans under the EU Nature Restoration Law can use it as a starting point for identifying priority sites, and the **EU can assess national reporting on a comparable basis** rather than relying on inconsistent national definitions.

## Tone and angle

Straight science press release, quoting the two lead authors. Careful to note the distinction between wetland types — coastal marshes matter but do not store as much CO₂ as peatlands — and to distinguish "highly affected" wetlands from those in acceptable condition. Also flags the work's next phase (a **global version of the map**).
