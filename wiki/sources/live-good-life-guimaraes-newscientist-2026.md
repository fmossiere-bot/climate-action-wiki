# Can everyone live a 'good life' without destroying the planet? New Scientist on Guimarães

**Source:** New Scientist, April Reese, 14 July 2026 (updated 15 July 2026)

## What it is

A long feature on Guimarães, a city in northern Portugal that is trying to live within the planet's limits, and on what that takes politically, financially and culturally. The city was named European Green Capital 2026 by the EU.

The writer uses Guimarães to open up a wider debate about planetary boundaries, about having "enough" (often called sufficiency), and about how societies change. She draws on Johan Rockström (planetary boundaries), Joel Millward-Hopkins (University of Lausanne), Sara Pires (University of Aveiro), Tim Lenton (University of Exeter) and the World Inequality Lab's Global Justice Report.

## Why it matters

Guimarães is an early, real-world test of a "consumption corridor" in a European city: a floor of decent living for everyone, and a ceiling on how much anyone consumes. The article puts numbers on its progress. Waste per person is down 18% since 2018, its waste circularity score is 2.5 times the national average, 95 hectares of green space have been restored, and it receives €5m in EU Green Capital funding.

One lesson other cities can copy is that Guimarães wrote its sustainability commitments into law. That makes them hard for a later council to undo.

The World Inequality Lab's report (June 2026) adds a fairness argument. Instead of asking everyone to give up the same amount, it asks those who caused most of the damage, the very rich, to pay most of the cost.

The piece is hopeful but does not gloss over the limits. Many Guimarães residents still choose to drive despite the new bus fleet, and the very rich barely change their habits when fuel prices rise.

## Key facts

- Humanity has now crossed **seven of the nine planetary boundaries**: climate change, ocean acidification, biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater-system change, novel entities and nutrient cycles. Ozone depletion and air pollution from aerosols are still within safe limits.
- The planetary boundaries idea was introduced in a 2009 paper by Johan Rockström at the Potsdam Institute.
- Total household water use has risen sixfold over the past 50 years, faster than water use in farming or industry.
- Over a similar period, the extraction of natural resources and energy use have tripled.
- A study by Sara Pires of the University of Aveiro, about a decade ago, found that if everyone lived like a Guimarães resident, we would need almost 2.3 Earths. The global average is 1.7 Earths.
- A 2025 study by Millward-Hopkins found consumption is growing four times faster in countries that already consume more than they need than in countries below decent living standards. At current rates, lower-income countries would not fully reach decent living standards until the end of the century.
- Guimarães has cut average waste per person by 18% since 2018, and its waste circularity index is 2.5 times the Portuguese average.
- The Landscape Laboratory and partners have restored 95 hectares of green space since 2012, and circular projects recover more than 20 tonnes of textiles a year.
- The Global Justice Report (World Inequality Lab, June 2026) proposes a global wealth tax on billionaires to pay for the transition. It calculates that average income per person in every country could rise to about €5,000 a month by the end of the century while staying within planetary limits.

## Ideas the article explains

- **Planetary boundaries, or the "safe operating space"** (Rockström): nine limits which, if crossed, push us out of the stable conditions of the Holocene that humanity has relied on for 12,000 years.
- **Decent living standards** (Millward-Hopkins): a basic floor for well-being, such as food and housing, plus a margin to tackle inequality.
- **Useless overconsumption**: wealth above the decent living level adds little to well-being but a lot to environmental damage.
- **Consumption corridor**: a floor of decent living and a ceiling on maximum consumption, agreed together.
- **Social tipping points** (Tim Lenton, University of Exeter): moments when shifts in attitudes and habits set off a much bigger, often fast and self-sustaining change across a society.
- **One Planet City**: a designation from Bioregional and WWF for cities that commit to shrinking their ecological footprint to a "one-Earth" level by 2050.

## What Guimarães is doing

- The **Landscape Laboratory**, a research and education centre in an old textile factory, run with the University of Minho and the University of Trás-os-Montes e Alto Douro.
- Pay-as-you-throw rubbish collection, charged by the kilo.
- Regular repair fairs to keep electronics and other goods in use for longer.
- A revived circular textile industry, including RDD Textiles and Zouri shoes, which use recycled materials and natural dyes.
- Floodwater retention basins that combine wetlands, meadows and retaining walls. They held back water during Portugal's deadly storms in January 2026, while nearby areas flooded.
- "Green brigades" of volunteers who organise clean-ups, plant trees and run wildlife surveys.
- Public consultation: a recent call for ideas drew 200 suggestions, and 150 are now being put into practice.
- Bairro C, a neighbourhood that is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, has become a testing ground for zero-carbon policies and technologies.
- The previous council wrote its sustainability commitments into law, so the new mayor cannot easily reverse them.

## The awkward finding about wealthy environmentalists

A recent study found that among the wealthiest 30% of people, those who cared most about the environment had an even bigger footprint than their peers. Millward-Hopkins explains why: great wealth shields people from the money pressures that might otherwise change their behaviour. When fuel prices go up, poorer people cut back. The very rich do not. This matches the [high-income carbon paradox](../concepts/behavior-change/high-income-carbon-paradox.md) already covered on the wiki.

## Voices in the piece

Carlos Ribeiro, a biologist who heads the Landscape Laboratory, describes the city's approach: "Social justice and the principle of leaving no one behind are truly central to our approach."

Joel Millward-Hopkins of the University of Lausanne argues that people in rich countries would gain more than they lose: "Any reductions in consumption that are required by regular people in Europe or North America could be more than compensated for by providing things that really improve people's lives: universal healthcare and education; healthy, affordable food; fulfilling work; and secure housing without the threat of eviction or mortgage spikes. All of these things can be provided to all without exceeding ecological limits, if we can direct our labour towards things that matter, not just what's profitable."

Sandra Freitas, a Guimarães resident who runs a vegetarian café, puts it in everyday terms: "We don't need too much food in our freezer or too much money in our pockets, and we don't need to have 10 cars."

Sara Pires of the University of Aveiro, on the city's legal commitments: "It's almost impossible for a new mayor to come and destroy that."

Cornelia Mohren, environmental coordinator at the World Inequality Lab, links climate and fairness: "The climate crisis and inequality are very much intertwined. We think that it's necessary to make those pay who caused the climate crisis, which are the global rich."
