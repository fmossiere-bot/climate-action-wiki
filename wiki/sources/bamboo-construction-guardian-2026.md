# Schools, airports, high-rise towers: architects urged to get 'bamboo-ready'

**Source:** The Guardian, Yassin El-Moudden, 22 January 2026. https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/jan/22/bamboo-architecture-construction-engineering-schools-airports-towers

## What it is

The Guardian reports on a new design manual from the **Institution of Structural Engineers**, a guide to the ISO 22156 standard for permanent bamboo structures. Its lead author is David Trujillo, assistant professor in humanitarian engineering at the University of Warwick.

The Institution wants architects to be "bamboo-ready". It presents engineered bamboo as a serious low-carbon alternative to steel and concrete in schools, airports, low-rise housing and some towers.

## Why it matters

Cement and steel are two of the hardest parts of the economy to clean up. Bamboo will not replace them everywhere. It is not suited to tall buildings, and in cooler countries it still needs supply chains, testing, insurance and trained builders.

What it does offer is unusual. It **grows in years, not decades**, it keeps carbon locked away while the building stands, and it has already proved itself in earthquake zones and poorer communities where safe housing matters most. Until now, engineers had no clear, standard document for designing permanent bamboo buildings, which made it hard to get projects approved. The new manual fills that gap.

## Key facts

- The construction industry produced about 1/3 of global carbon emissions in 2022. More than half of that came from cement and similar materials.
- Bamboo is ready to harvest in 3 to 6 years, compared with decades for timber.
- Terminal 2 of Kempegowda International Airport in Bengaluru, India, uses bamboo tubes for its ceiling and pillars.
- The Ninghai bamboo tower in north-east China is claimed as the world's first engineered-bamboo high-rise, at over 20 metres tall.
- The Green School in Bali has an arc-shaped bamboo gymnasium, a showcase project for the UK structural engineers Atelier One.
- Composite bamboo shear walls are now used in Colombia and the Philippines for housing that can withstand earthquakes and other disasters.
- In general, bamboo is not recommended for buildings above two storeys.

## Other points in the piece

Bamboo grows with little pesticide or fertiliser, and harvesting it can help soil recover after years of single-crop farming.

The manual also points to "knowledge gaps" in engineering. Part of the reason is history: colonial-era technical education pushed aside local know-how about building with bamboo.

Portugal is now growing larger bamboo varieties as a crop, as the Mediterranean climate shifts. That could open up a European supply chain for building with it.

## Voices in the piece

Neil Thomas, director of Atelier One, puts the case simply: "Everything you can do with timber, you can do with bamboo."

Trujillo sees bamboo as part of a wider move in how cities are built: "The idea that we can move people away from using carbon-intensive materials and towards low-carbon materials or, better still, carbon-fixing materials seems like a very wise way of minimising the emissions from urbanisation." He hopes lecturers will "incorporate it in their taught content so that we educate our next generation of engineers and architects to be bamboo-ready."
