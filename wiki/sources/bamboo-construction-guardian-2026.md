# Schools, airports, high-rise towers: architects urged to get 'bamboo-ready'

**Source:** The Guardian, Yassin El-Moudden, 22 January 2026
**URL:** https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/jan/22/bamboo-architecture-construction-engineering-schools-airports-towers

## What it is

Guardian reporting on a **new design manual from the Institution of Structural Engineers** (the ISO 22156 guide for permanent bamboo structures), whose lead author is **David Trujillo, assistant professor in humanitarian engineering at the University of Warwick**. The Institution is calling on architects to be "**bamboo-ready**," framing engineered bamboo as a serious low-carbon alternative to steel and concrete in schools, airports, low-rise housing and some tower structures.

## Key facts cited

- **Construction industry = ~1/3 of global carbon emissions** in 2022; more than half of that is from cement and cementitious materials
- **Bamboo growth cycle: 3–6 years**, compared to decades for timber
- **Terminal 2 of Kempegowda International Airport, Bengaluru** (India): bamboo tubes form the ceiling and pillars
- **Ninghai bamboo tower, north-east China**: claimed as the world's first engineered-bamboo high-rise, over 20 metres tall
- **Green School, Bali**: arc-shaped bamboo gymnasium — a showcase project for Atelier One (UK structural engineers)
- **Composite bamboo shear walls** now used in Colombia and the Philippines for **earthquake-resilient, disaster-resilient housing**
- **Bamboo is not recommended above two storeys** in general
- Grows with **little pesticide or fertiliser**; harvesting can support recovery of soil degraded by monoculture
- The Institution's manual points to **"knowledge gaps"** partly linked to colonial-era technical education that displaced local bamboo expertise
- **Portugal** is now growing larger bamboo varieties as a crop in response to Mediterranean climate shifts — potentially opening a European building supply chain

## Notable quotes

- **Neil Thomas** (director, Atelier One): *"Everything you can do with timber, you can do with bamboo."*
- **David Trujillo** (Warwick, lead author of the manual): *"The idea that we can move people away from using carbon-intensive materials and towards low-carbon materials or, better still, carbon-fixing materials seems like a very wise way of minimising the emissions from urbanisation."*
- Trujillo also hopes lecturers will "incorporate it in their taught content so that we educate our next generation of engineers and architects to be bamboo-ready."

## Why it matters

Cement and steel are two of the hardest sectors to decarbonise. Engineered bamboo is not a silver bullet — it is inappropriate for tall structures and needs new supply chains, testing, insurance and training in temperate zones — but it has three properties that no fossil-based alternative has: it **grows in years, not decades**, it **stores carbon during use**, and it **is already proven** in seismically active, low-income contexts where disaster-resilient housing is critical. The Institution's manual is important because it removes one of the biggest procurement blockers: no clear, standardised engineering document for permanent bamboo structures.

## Tone and angle

Optimistic but grounded. The Guardian names concrete examples (airports, towers, gymnasiums, disaster housing) rather than talking in the abstract, and the piece flags both the specific limits (two-storey rule) and the systemic issues (colonial-era knowledge gaps, teaching pipeline).
