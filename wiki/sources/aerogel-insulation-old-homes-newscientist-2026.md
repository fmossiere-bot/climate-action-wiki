# Super-insulating aerogels could help old homes reach net zero

**Source:** New Scientist, Madeleine Cuff, 28 October 2026 (updated 4 November 2026)

## What is it

**Aerogels** are synthetic, ultra-light solids that are mostly air. Wim Malfait of the Swiss Federal Laboratories for Materials Science and Technology (EMPA) describes them as "porous solids", where air molecules get trapped between tiny pores, so heat moves through them very slowly. They also resist fire and let moisture through, which matters in old buildings.

Until now aerogels have been expensive and polluting to make, so they were mostly used in industrial plants, aviation and space technology. Making one usually means creating a gel of silica nanoparticles and then drying it under carefully controlled conditions, called supercritical drying, which uses a lot of energy and can take days.

This New Scientist article reports on two start-ups, one British and one French, that say they can now make aerogels more cheaply and at a larger scale. Their first products are aimed at homes that are hard to insulate: insulating plaster, renders for outside walls, cavity wall fill and a reflective "cool roof" coating.

## Why it matters

The UK has around **8 million homes with solid walls**, most of them built before the 1920s. They are hard to insulate. Planning rules often stop owners adding cladding outside, and cladding inside eats into floor space and can cause mould and damp. So most of these homes stay largely uninsulated and burn a lot of gas each winter. Without better insulation, low-carbon heating is expensive to install and to run in them.

A thin, breathable, fireproof insulating plaster could give these households a realistic option. The catch is cost. Even with cheaper production, aerogel products will cost more than ordinary insulation, and Malfait warns that start-ups will have to get homeowners past "this initial price shock".

## Key facts

- Around 8 million UK homes have solid walls, the vast majority built before the 1920s.
- Thermulon (UK) says it has redesigned production around cheaper materials, lower energy use and standard equipment. With plaster-maker Best of Lime it is developing an aerogel lime plaster for historic solid-wall homes, likely its first product.
- Thermulon plans commercial production with a contract manufacturer in Northern Ireland from next year, able to make 100 tonnes of aerogel a year in 2027.
- Keey Aerogel (France) makes aerogel from silica waste from the construction industry, using a solvent that is continuously recycled. Founder Francisco Ruiz says its carbon footprint is three times smaller than that of traditional aerogels, at a competitive cost.
- Keey raised €18 million in venture capital in 2025, including French government backing. Its first plant, near Mulhouse in eastern France, starts production next year with capacity for 1,000 to 2,000 cubic metres of aerogel a year.
- Keey is developing an aerogel render for outside walls, a cool roof coating that reflects sunlight, and a cavity wall insulation to be sold in the UK and Ireland, so builders can meet tighter standards without shrinking rooms.

## Limits of the figures

The cost and carbon claims come from the companies themselves and have not been independently tested. The first version of the article gave Keey's plant capacity as 1,000 to 2,000 tonnes a year. New Scientist corrected this on 29 October 2026 to 1,000 to 2,000 cubic metres, which is a much smaller amount by weight, because aerogels are so light.
