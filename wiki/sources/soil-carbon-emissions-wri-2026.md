# Soils emit massive amounts of greenhouse gases

**Source:** World Resources Institute (WRI), David Gibbs, Erin Glen, Nancy Harris, Angela Isabella Scafidi and Serkan Isik, November 2026. https://www.wri.org/insights/soil-carbon-explained

*Two clipped copies of this article were ingested. They are the same text, so this page covers both.*

## What it is

An explainer from the World Resources Institute, built on new research by WRI and partners. Soils hold more carbon than all the world's trees and the atmosphere combined. The study finds that between 2016 and 2024, soils disturbed by people released about **3.9 billion tonnes of greenhouse gases a year**, close to the combined fossil fuel emissions of India and Saudi Arabia in 2024.

## Why it matters

Soil carbon is easy to forget because it is underground and hard to measure, so it is often missing from national emissions inventories. Yet losing it does three kinds of harm at once: it adds to climate change, it makes farmland less fertile and more fragile in droughts and floods, and it threatens farmers' incomes.

## Peat and other organic soils

Two-thirds of soil emissions come from **organic soils**, such as peat, which form in waterlogged places where dead plants rot very slowly. Peatlands cover only 3% of the world's land but store roughly 600 billion tonnes of carbon, about a third of all soil carbon, sometimes up to 10 metres deep. When they are drained, oxygen gets in and microbes start breaking down the peat, releasing CO2 year after year. About 80% of organic soil emissions come from drainage and the rest from fire, which drained peat makes more likely. Climate-driven drying adds to this: most emissions from a fire in Scotland in the very dry summer of 2025 came from peat bogs, including areas that used to be too wet to burn.

## Ordinary farm and grassland soils

The other third, about 1.3 billion tonnes of CO2 a year, comes from **mineral soils**, the ordinary soils under most farmland, grassland and forest. Losses here are slower and spread over a much larger area. Grasslands and land with annual crops together lost 840 million tonnes of CO2 a year between 2015 and 2020, and croplands alone 190 million tonnes. The US state of Iowa has lost half of its cropland soil carbon over 200 years, largely from continuous tilling, and erosion across the US Corn Belt has cut crop yields by 6%.

## Permafrost, the bigger future risk

Permafrost, ground frozen for thousands of years, holds roughly half the world's soil carbon and lies under about a quarter of the Northern Hemisphere's land. As it thaws, it could release around 7 to 15 billion tonnes of greenhouse gases a year by the end of the century, more than the United States emits today, though the range is very uncertain. This is a feedback loop: warming thaws permafrost, which releases gases that cause more warming. Thawing ground also buckles roads and buildings and has forced some Arctic communities to move.

## What can be done

The biggest opportunity is to **stop draining peatlands**. They produce only about 1% of the world's calories, yet their drainage releases roughly a third of farming's emissions. Rewetting can briefly increase methane, but it halts the much longer stream of CO2. On mineral soils, avoiding the conversion of forests and grasslands matters most, while practices such as agroforestry, no-till farming, cover crops and crop rotation can slowly rebuild carbon, often taking decades to show. There are hopeful cases: soil carbon has risen on China's Northeast Plain since 2006 with gentler tillage, and in the eastern US where forests have regrown on old farmland.

## Summary findings

- Disturbed soils released about 3.9 billion tonnes of greenhouse gases a year in 2016 to 2024: two-thirds from organic soils, about 1.3 billion tonnes of CO2 from mineral soils.
- 93% of organic soil emissions come from just 10 countries, with Indonesia, where peat was drained for logging, oil palm and pulpwood, the largest. Ten countries account for about three-quarters of mineral soil emissions.
- Peatlands: 3% of land, about 600 billion tonnes of carbon, about 1% of global calories, roughly a third of agricultural emissions when drained.
- Iowa has lost half its cropland soil carbon in 200 years. The US Corn Belt has lost nearly 1.5 billion tonnes of carbon from hillslopes, costing nearly $3 billion a year.
- Erosion costs US agriculture an estimated $44 billion and South Asian farmers about $10 billion a year, and cuts global food production by over 30 million tonnes a year. Land degradation has lowered yields where 1.7 billion people live.
- Permafrost thaw could release 7 to 15 billion tonnes of greenhouse gases a year by 2100.
