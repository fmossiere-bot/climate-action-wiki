## 1. Why the ocean matters

> The ocean covers 71% of the planet and plays a central role in regulating the climate. It absorbs around 25% of the CO2 emitted by human activities and around 90% of the excess heat caused by climate change, which slows the pace of global warming. 

> About half of the world's oxygen comes from marine phytoplankton. The ocean also holds 80% of the world's animal biomass, and around 3 billion people depend on marine resources for food and income.

## 2. Growing pressure

These functions are under strain. Sea surface temperature has risen by about 1°C since the industrial revolution, with the Mediterranean warming around 20% faster than the global average. Rising CO2 absorption is also making the ocean more acidic, weakening corals, molluscs and plankton. Sea level rose by around 15 centimetres over the twentieth century, and extreme weather events are becoming more frequent. In France, the village of Miquelon, in the Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon archipelago, is set to be relocated due to storm and flooding risks, a first for the country.

Pollution and overfishing add to the pressure. Between 9 and 14 million tonnes of plastic enter the ocean each year, and around 80% of pollutants come from land-based sources. An estimated 37.7% of fish stocks are currently overexploited.

## 3. International momentum: BBNJ and the first Ocean COP

> Ocean protection has gained more attention on the international stage in recent years, partly following the UN Ocean Conference (Unoc). A significant step is the BBNJ agreement, which covers the protection of biodiversity in the high seas, areas beyond national jurisdiction. This agreement enters into force in 2026.

Building on this, **the first Ocean COP is due to take place in early 2027**. This will be the first dedicated global conference of parties focused on ocean protection, similar in structure to the climate COPs. Experts in the field describe this as a historic step, while also noting that significant work remains to turn international commitments into effective protection on the water.

**One related international target is to protect 30% of the ocean through marine protected areas by 2030**. According to a 2025 study cited by ocean scientists, the effectiveness of these areas depends on three main factors: a large surface area, strict protection with no fishing or extraction allowed, and connections between protected zones so that species can move freely between them.

## 4. Credibility and limitations

Figures on ocean warming, sea level rise and biomass come from established scientific sources, including the IPCC and UN bodies, and are widely accepted. Some points, such as the exact effectiveness criteria for marine protected areas, rely on a single 2025 study referenced in the source interview, and have not been independently cross-checked here. The BBNJ entry-into-force date and the timing of the first Ocean COP reflect the state of international negotiations as reported in mid-2026, and could be subject to change.

## 5. Bottom line

The ocean provides essential, measurable services to the planet, from climate regulation to food and oxygen, but it is under growing pressure from warming, acidification, pollution and overfishing. The BBNJ agreement and the first Ocean COP in early 2027 mark a new phase of international attention to ocean protection, though translating these agreements into effective, on-the-water results remains an open challenge.
