## 1. Why the ocean matters

> The ocean covers 71% of the planet and plays a central role in regulating the climate. It absorbs around 25% of the CO2 released by human activity and around 90% of the extra heat caused by climate change, which slows down global warming.

> About half of the world's oxygen comes from marine phytoplankton, the tiny plants that drift near the surface. The ocean also holds 80% of the world's animal biomass, and around 3 billion people depend on marine resources for food and income.

## 2. Growing pressure

All of this is under strain. Sea surface temperature has risen by about **1°C** since the industrial revolution, and the Mediterranean is warming around 20% faster than the global average. As the ocean absorbs more CO2 it also becomes more acidic, which weakens corals, shellfish and plankton. Sea level rose by around 15 centimetres over the twentieth century, and extreme weather is becoming more frequent. In France, the village of Miquelon, in the Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon archipelago, is due to be moved because of the risk of storms and flooding, a first for the country.

Pollution and overfishing add to the pressure. Between **9 and 14 million tonnes of plastic** enter the ocean each year, and around 80% of pollutants come from land. An estimated 37.7% of fish stocks are currently overfished.

## 3. International momentum: BBNJ and the first Ocean COP

> Ocean protection has gained more attention internationally in recent years, partly after the UN Ocean Conference (Unoc). One big step is the BBNJ agreement, which protects biodiversity on the high seas, the areas beyond any country's control. It enters into force in 2026.

Building on this, **the first Ocean COP is due to take place in early 2027**. It will be the first global conference of parties dedicated to ocean protection, organised much like the climate COPs. Ocean experts call it a historic step, while noting that a lot of work remains to turn international promises into real protection at sea.

A related international target is to protect **30% of the ocean** through marine protected areas by 2030. According to a 2025 study cited by ocean scientists, how well these areas work depends mainly on their design. They need to be large, strictly protected with no fishing or extraction, and connected to each other so that species can move freely between them.

## 4. Credibility and limitations

The figures on ocean warming, sea level rise and biomass come from established scientific sources, including the IPCC and UN bodies, and are widely accepted. Some points, such as the exact conditions that make marine protected areas work, rest on a single 2025 study mentioned in the source interview and have not been checked against other sources here. The date the BBNJ agreement enters into force and the timing of the first Ocean COP reflect the state of negotiations as reported in mid-2026, and could change.

## 5. Summary

The ocean provides essential, measurable services to the planet, from regulating the climate to supplying food and oxygen. It is under growing pressure from warming, acidification, pollution and overfishing. The BBNJ agreement and the first Ocean COP in early 2027 open a new phase of international attention to the ocean, though turning these agreements into real results at sea is still an open challenge.
