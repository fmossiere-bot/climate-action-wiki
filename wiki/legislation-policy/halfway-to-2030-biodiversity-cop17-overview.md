# Halfway to 2030: what the biodiversity COP17 in Armenia is about

> COP17 is the first global review of progress on the 2030 nature targets agreed at COP15 in Montreal in 2022. Nearly 90% of countries now have targets, but fewer than half of those targets are on track; the hardest fight is money, with €30 flowing to activities that damage nature for every €1 spent protecting it; the Cali Fund asked for about €890m from big pharma and got €6,300; and Ireland pushed hard for the EU Nature Restoration Law but admits it is not on track at home.** The final COP17 deal is expected on 30 October 2026, four days from this write-up.

## What is a biodiversity COP?

Most people have heard of the climate COPs. There is a second series of UN summits, just for nature. It comes from the **Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD)**, signed at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro in **1992**. Under it, countries agreed to protect nature and to use what it gives us in a fair and lasting way.

- **196 parties** are part of the convention. The United States is not one of them.
- The meetings are numbered. **COP15** was in Montreal in 2022, **COP16** in Cali, Colombia, in 2024, and **COP17** is now in **Yerevan, Armenia, from 19 to 30 October 2026**.
- More than **10,000 delegates** came: ministers, officials, scientists, campaigners, Indigenous leaders, business people and bankers.
- It is the first time a former Soviet republic has hosted the summit. The theme is **"Taking action for nature"**.

Two smaller treaties meet at the same time: the **Cartagena Protocol** on biosafety (genetically modified organisms) and the **Nagoya Protocol** on sharing the benefits of nature's genetic resources.

## The promise of Montreal

In December 2022, in the early hours of the morning, governments agreed a deal at COP15 that many people called the "Paris Agreement for nature". Its full name is the **Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework**. It has **23 targets for 2030** and four goals for 2050. The big ones:

- **30x30**: protect at least **30% of land, inland waters and seas** by 2030.
- **Restore 30%** of damaged ecosystems.
- Cut pollution and invasive species, and slow down extinctions.
- Reduce subsidies that harm nature by at least **$500 billion (about €450 billion) a year**.
- Find **$200 billion (about €180 billion) a year** for nature from all sources, including **$30 billion (about €27 billion) a year** from rich countries to poorer ones.

This was not the first try. The previous set of targets, the **Aichi targets** for 2020, were almost all missed. Montreal was meant to be different.

## COP17 is the halfway check

Four years after Montreal, with four years left, COP17 is the **first global review** of how countries are doing. Just before the summit, on 15 October, UN Secretary-General **António Guterres** launched the first report on this, the **State of Biodiversity Action**.

**The good news**

- Nearly **90% of countries** now have national biodiversity targets.
- Close to **60%** have a full national strategy and action plan.
- Over **70%** have sent their national report.
- **83% of countries** have targets that match the global plan. At the end of the Aichi period in 2020, only about half did.
- Indigenous peoples, cities, regions, businesses and groups have registered about **5,000 commitments**.

**The bad news**

- **Fewer than half** of the national targets that were checked are on track for 2030.
- A draft of the report found the world is falling short on **22 of the 23 targets**.
- Countries were happy to set targets on protecting nature. They were much slower on the hard parts: what we consume, harmful subsidies, money, land rights and bringing nature into economic decisions.
- Only **27%** of countries have a target on cutting harmful subsidies, and only **5%** gave a number.
- The seas, rivers and coasts get less attention than forests.
- Guterres said that finance, capacity, cooperation and data are **"still missing in action"**. He also said **"Nature won't thrive on good intentions."**

The report is honest that four years is short to see big changes in nature itself. But the signs that can be measured are more negative than positive. WWF says wildlife populations it monitors have dropped by **73% in 50 years**, and the UN says more than **one million species** are at risk of extinction.

In the Guardian, environment reporter **Patrick Greenfield** wrote before the summit that the world "has lost its way on nature once again" and that hopes for COP17 were "very low". The excitement in the hall in Montreal has faded.

## Where 30x30 stands

- Today about **18 to 19% of land** and inland water is protected, two points more than in 2020.
- At sea, about **23%** of the waters countries control is protected, but only about **10% of the whole ocean**.
- On the **high seas**, the waters that belong to no country, only about **1.5%** is protected, according to Brian O'Donnell of Campaign for Nature.
- If every country did everything it has promised, land protection would reach only about **26 to 27.5%** by 2030. Short of 30%.
- Since 2022, about **1.3 million km² of land** and **6 million km² of ocean** have been newly protected. To picture it, that land alone is about **15 times the size of the island of Ireland**.
- Governments report **193 million hectares** under restoration.

One real step forward: the **High Seas Treaty** came into force in **January 2026**. It is the first legally binding set of rules to protect life in international waters.

Groups like Greenpeace warn that the number is not everything. A "protected" area on a map that is still fished, mined or drained does little. They want quality as well as size, and respect for the people who live there.

## The money problem

This is the hardest fight at COP17. WWF called it the most contentious part of the talks, with "many divergent views".

- For every **$1** spent protecting nature, about **$30** goes to activities that damage it, according to the UN Environment Programme.
- In 2023, **$7.3 trillion (about €6.5 trillion)** flowed into industries that harm nature.
- Money for nature is still **less than half** of the $200 billion a year promised for 2030.
- Rich countries were supposed to give **$20 billion (about €18 billion) a year** by 2025. They reached about **$16 billion (about €14 billion)**.
- The total gap in nature funding is put at about **$700 billion (about €625 billion) a year**.

Still, the UN's biodiversity chief **Astrid Schomaker** told delegates: **"If you hear there is no finance, please do not believe it."** The money exists. It is going to the wrong places.

The arguments in Yerevan:

- **Who pays?** The list of countries expected to give money was last updated in **2006**. France says big emerging economies and Gulf states should now pay too. The EU wants more donors, but on a voluntary basis. African countries ask rich countries to explain where the money will actually come from.
- **Who controls the fund?** Countries of the global south want a new fund run under the convention itself. Most rich countries prefer the current system, the Global Environment Facility. This row was so bad that COP16 in Cali was suspended in 2024 and had to finish in Rome in 2025.

## The Cali Fund: €6,300 instead of €890 million

This part of the story is almost hard to believe.

Scientists put the **DNA of plants, animals and microbes** into free online databases. This is called **digital sequence information (DSI)**. Companies use it to make medicines, vaccines, cosmetics and new crops. A lot of that genetic wealth comes from forests and seas in poorer countries, which get nothing back.

At COP16 in 2024, countries created the **Cali Fund** to fix this. Companies that make money from this data are asked to give **1% of their profits or 0.1% of their revenue**. At least **half** of the money is meant to go to Indigenous peoples and local communities.

- **Target**: **$1 billion (about €890 million) a year**.
- **What it got**, as of 19 October 2026, more than 18 months after its launch in February 2025: **$10,656 (about €9,500) promised** and **$7,085 (about €6,300) actually paid in**.
- That came from small firms only: **Prozomix** (UK), **TierraViva AI** (UK) and **E-Biom** (Belgium).
- **No big pharmaceutical company** has paid anything.

A study by **Zero Carbon Analytics** found that the 15 biggest drug companies made about **$161 billion (about €144 billion)** in profit in 2025. At the suggested rate, they could have paid about **$756 million (about €675 million)**. What they paid out to shareholders was **175 times** that amount.

- **June Rubis**, who sits on the fund's steering committee for Indigenous peoples in Asia: "Look at how much profit these pharmaceutical companies are getting, and we're only asking for a tiny percentage." On whether they are free-riding on nature: "Definitely."
- **Brian O'Donnell** of Campaign for Nature said more money had been spent on meetings about the fund than the fund holds. He thinks companies should be made to pay, not just asked.
- The industry body **IFPMA** says it supports biodiversity but has "ongoing questions" about how the fund is designed. **GSK** says it will decide once it knows how the fund fits with other rules. **Sanofi** says nature is a core part of its strategy.
- The fund itself called the result "disappointing but not entirely surprising", and says similar funds took two to three years to grow.

At COP17, African countries led by Egypt and Senegal are pushing back against a system that depends only on voluntary payments. Ethiopia is building a national database of its own genetic resources, so it can prove where they came from.

## Ireland's part in the story

<!-- tag: ireland -->
**Our record is poor.** An assessment last year found that **90% of Ireland's protected habitats**, and the plants and animals that depend on them, are in bad health. Ireland's Minister for Nature, **Christopher O'Sullivan**, who is in Yerevan, said plainly that Ireland is **"not on track"** to meet its 2030 targets, and that Ireland and the EU "need to do more".

**But Ireland has also led.**

- Ireland pushed hard for the **EU Nature Restoration Law**, passed in **2024**. Its aims mirror the Montreal targets.
- Ireland was the **first EU country** to send its national Nature Restoration Plan to Brussels.
- Ireland holds the **presidency of the Council of the EU** until the end of 2026, so Irish officials helped prepare the EU's position for COP17.
- The minister admits Ireland has not yet put money on the table. He says he is working on a **dedicated nature restoration fund** and hopes to share details "soon".

**Irish voices in Yerevan**

- **Fintan Kelly** of the Irish Environmental Network is there with people from **Fair Seas, the Irish Whale and Dolphin Group, BirdWatch Ireland, the Irish Wildlife Trust and Streamscapes**. He worries about the end of the EU's LIFE nature projects, too little nature money in the new Common Agricultural Policy, plans to weaken checks under the Birds and Habitats Directives, and cuts to environment funding in the next EU budget. There is still no EU or Irish fund to pay for the Nature Restoration Law.
- **Professor Jane Stout**, ecologist at Trinity College Dublin, called the first week "a mixed bag". In 1992 business was seen as the problem. Now there are many sessions on business and nature, because companies are realising that polluted water, tired soils, vanishing pollinators and collapsing fish stocks are a threat to them too.

## Europe's message

<!-- tag: eu -->
The EU wants Yerevan to be **"the COP of proof, not only of promises"**, in the words of Commission Vice-President **Teresa Ribera**.

- The EU says it is on track to double its international nature funding from **€3.5 billion to €7 billion**, as promised in 2021, with priority for the poorest countries.
- For its next budget (2028 to 2034), the Commission has proposed keeping **35%** for climate and environment action.
- On 27 October, Commissioner **Jessika Roswall** launches an initiative on **nature credits**, a way to bring private money into protecting nature.

## A host under the spotlight

Armenia wants COP17 to show it as a green leader, but its own record is being questioned.

- Just days before the summit, Armenia opened the **Amulsar gold mine**, despite years of environmental and human rights concerns. The Global Forest Coalition and Armenian groups condemned it.
- Mining is central to the economy, and Yale's 2026 Environmental Performance Index ranks Armenia **75th of 177** countries.
- Air pollution in Yerevan is about **five times** the World Health Organisation's recommended limit.

Before the talks, **Greenpeace** flew a hot air balloon over the village of Garni with the message **"Delay is Destruction"**.

## So far in the talks

- On **16 October**, a first ever Leaders' Summit adopted the **Yerevan Declaration**, linking nature, climate and land.
- On **23 October**, delegates agreed **14 decisions**, on things like invasive species, public awareness, gender and biosafety. Schomaker said "some of the most complex issues are still ahead of us."
- Still open: the money, the Cali Fund, the new body for Indigenous peoples, harmful subsidies, soil, pollution and connecting habitats.
- Ministers arrive on **27 and 28 October**. The aim is a package called the **Yerevan Pathway**, to get the targets back on track for the last four years.

## Why COP is still worth it

**Robert Kim** is 14 years old, runs the YouTube channel Children's Climate Championship, and is at his third biodiversity COP. He does not agree that COP17 is "dead in the water". He points to what these summits have already helped bring about:

- **Colombia** banned oil and gas on over **48 million hectares** of the Amazon.
- **Australia** created a **475,000 hectare** koala reserve.
- **Suriname** promised to protect **90%** of its rainforest.
- **193 million hectares** of damaged land are starting to recover.
- Salmon, green sea turtles, tuna, saiga antelopes, tigers, elephants, bison and tortoises are recovering in places.

He also points out that ending public subsidies for oil, gas and mining would free up an estimated **$1.7 to $3.2 trillion (about €1.5 to €2.9 trillion) a year**. That could cover the whole nature gap and still leave money for climate.

His message is simple: vote for nature at every level, talk about it, and ask for green spaces and waters to be protected. **"Our planet needs you. We need you."**

## The short version

- **COP17** in Yerevan is the halfway check on the 2030 plan to stop nature loss.
- Almost every country now has a plan, but **fewer than half of the targets are on track**.
- **30x30** is behind, especially at sea.
- The real fight is **money**: €30 goes to harming nature for every €1 spent to protect it.
- The **Cali Fund** asked big pharma for up to €890 million a year and got about **€6,300**, none of it from big pharma.
- **Ireland** helped win the EU Nature Restoration Law but admits it is **not on track** at home.
- The final deal comes on **30 October**.
