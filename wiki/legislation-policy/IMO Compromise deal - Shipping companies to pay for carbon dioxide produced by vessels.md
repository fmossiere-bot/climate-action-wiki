# IMO Compromise Deal - Shipping Companies to Pay for Carbon Dioxide Produced by Vessels

## What is it

In April 2025, a majority of countries meeting at the **International Maritime Organisation (IMO)** in London approved a compromise deal that will make all ships pay for the CO2 they emit, from 2028. Saudi Arabia, Russia, the United Arab Emirates and several other petrostates opposed the rules, but the agreement passed anyway.

International shipping produces around **3% of global greenhouse gas emissions**, yet it is not covered by the Paris Agreement. It is up to the IMO to decide how the industry cuts its emissions. In 2023 the IMO promised that shipping would reach net zero "by around 2050", and this framework is the first concrete plan for getting there.

Nishatabbas Rehmatulla of University College London, who researches shipping and energy, called the agreement a "momentous outcome" that sets the framework for the industry to deliver on its net-zero promise.

## Why it matters

This is the first time a whole global sector has been given a mandatory carbon price that applies to everyone in the same way. Aviation, the other big transport sector left outside the Paris Agreement, has only a voluntary emissions target. The IMO deal is mandatory and comes with a high price: up to **$380 per tonne** for excess emissions, described as the toughest approach yet to cutting shipping emissions.

## How the targets work

The framework is due to come into force in 2028. It sets two carbon-intensity targets for ships over 5,000 gross tonnes, measuring greenhouse gas emissions per unit of energy in the fuel used (grams of CO2-equivalent per megajoule, counted from well to wake):

- **The lower target** requires ships to cut the greenhouse gas intensity of their fuel by 4% by 2028 compared with 2008 levels, rising to 30% by 2035.
- **The tougher target** requires a 17% cut by 2028, rising to 43% by 2035.

Ships that miss the tougher target must pay $100 per tonne for the emissions beyond it, and $380 per tonne for emissions above the weaker target. Ships that do better than the stricter target receive a reward payment, meant to cover the extra cost of using low-carbon fuels. The framework still needed to pass a final vote in October 2025 before it could come into force.

## What the money is for, and why some say it falls short

The measure is expected to raise about **$10 billion (£7.6 billion) a year**, much less than the $60 billion a year that campaigners had hoped a simple carbon levy would bring in. The money is also likely to stay within shipping to pay for cleaner technology. A levy would instead have sent it to vulnerable countries hit by extreme weather.

The idea is to give ship owners a reason to cut emissions while raising money to develop new "e-fuels" such as green ammonia and methanol. These fuels are still expensive and scarce. Scaling them up means building new supply chains for renewable hydrogen and refitting ports and ships to run on them.

The deal is expected to cut emissions by only around 8% by 2030, well below the 20% cut set out in the IMO's own 2023 climate strategy. Emma Fenton, a senior director at the campaign group Opportunity Green, said: "The IMO has made a historic decision, yet ultimately one that fails climate-vulnerable countries and falls short of both the ambition the climate crisis demands, and that member states committed to just two years ago."

## The risk of leaning on biofuels

One concern is that the rules could accidentally trigger a rush towards biofuels, which can do their own environmental damage. This needs watching for at least the first five years. Over that period, biofuel is likely to be the cheapest way to comply for **85% of the global fleet**, which could mean tens of millions of tonnes of extra demand. To avoid this, the package should be designed to steer ship owners towards e-fuels instead, for example by paying more generously when they use them. Without that push, the e-fuel market may not grow, and the industry could reach 2035 or 2037 with very little e-fuel available.

## Key facts

- Shipping produces around 3% of global greenhouse gas emissions and sits outside the Paris Agreement.
- The IMO's 2023 climate strategy aims for net-zero shipping emissions by around 2050.
- From 2028, ships over 5,000 gross tonnes face carbon-intensity targets: a 4% cut (lower target) or a 17% cut (tougher target) against 2008 levels, rising to 30% and 43% by 2035.
- Ships that miss the targets pay $100 to $380 per tonne of excess emissions. Ships that beat them receive a reward payment.
- The scheme is expected to raise about $10 billion a year, against the $60 billion a year a simple carbon levy could have raised.
- Expected emissions cuts of about 8% by 2030 fall well short of the IMO's own 20% target.
- Biofuel is expected to be the cheapest way to comply for 85% of the fleet in the first five years, raising concerns about a surge in demand.
