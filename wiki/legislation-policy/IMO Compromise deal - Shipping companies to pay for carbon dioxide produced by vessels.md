# IMO Compromise Deal - Shipping Companies to Pay for Carbon Dioxide Produced by Vessels

## What is it

In April 2025, a majority of countries meeting at the **International Maritime Organisation (IMO)** in London approved a compromise deal that will require all ships to pay for the CO2 they emit, from 2028. Saudi Arabia, Russia, the United Arab Emirates and several other petrostates opposed the rules, but the agreement passed regardless. **International shipping accounts for around 3% of global greenhouse gas emissions**, but the sector is not covered by the Paris Agreement; instead, it falls to the IMO to decide how to decarbonise the industry. In 2023, the IMO promised that shipping would become a net-zero emissions sector "by around 2050", and this framework is the first concrete roadmap for getting there.

Nishatabbas Rehmatulla of University College London called the agreement a "momentous outcome" that sets the framework for the shipping industry to deliver on its net-zero promise.

## Why it matters

This is the first time a global sector has been given a mandatory, uniform carbon price. Aviation, the other major transport sector left outside the Paris Agreement, has only a voluntary emissions target. The IMO deal is different: it is mandatory and comes with a high carbon price attached, described as the most aggressive approach yet taken to cutting shipping emissions, with a carbon price of up to $380 per tonne for excess emissions.

## How the targets work

The framework, due to come into force in 2028, sets two carbon-intensity targets for ships over 5,000 gross tonnes, measuring greenhouse gas emissions per litre of fuel used:

- **The lower target** requires ships to cut their fuel's greenhouse gas intensity by 4% by 2028 compared with 2008 levels, rising to 30% by 2035.
- **The tougher target** requires a 17% reduction by 2028, rising to 43% by 2035.

Ships that fail to meet the stretch target must pay $100 per tonne of emissions beyond that point, rising to $380 per tonne for emissions above the weaker target. Ships that overperform against the stricter target receive a reward payment, intended to offset the added cost of using low-carbon fuels. The framework still needed to pass a final vote in October 2025 before entering force.

## What the money is for, and why some say it falls short

The measure is forecast to raise about $10 billion (£7.6 billion) a year, considerably less than the $60 billion a year that had been hoped for from a straightforward carbon levy. The revenue is also likely to stay within the shipping industry to help fund cleaner technologies, rather than being diverted to vulnerable countries suffering the effects of extreme weather, as a levy on the sector would have done. The underlying idea is to create an incentive for ship owners to cut emissions while raising money to support the development of new "e-fuels" such as green ammonia and methanol, options that remain expensive and scarce. Scaling them up will require building new supply chains for renewable hydrogen and retrofitting ports and ships to run on the new fuels.

The forecast emissions reduction from the deal is only around 8% by 2030, well below the 20% reduction called for in the IMO's own 2023 climate strategy. Emma Fenton, a senior director at the campaign group Opportunity Green, said: "The IMO has made a historic decision, yet ultimately one that fails climate-vulnerable countries and falls short of both the ambition the climate crisis demands, and that member states committed to just two years ago."

## The risk of leaning on biofuels

A significant concern is that the rules could unintentionally encourage a surge in biofuel use, which can itself be environmentally damaging. This is worth monitoring closely, at least for the first five years, since biofuel is likely to be the least-cost compliance option for 85% of the global fleet during that period, implying tens of millions of tonnes of additional demand. To avoid this outcome, the rules and structure of the package should be designed to incentivise ship owners to invest in e-fuels instead, for example through more generous payments for using them. This is seen as essential for growing the e-fuel market: without a deliberate push, the industry risks reaching 2035 or 2037 with very little e-fuel actually available.

## Key facts

- Shipping accounts for around 3% of global GHG emissions and sits outside the Paris Agreement.
- The IMO's 2023 climate strategy targets net-zero shipping emissions by around 2050.
- From 2028, ships over 5,000 gross tonnes face carbon-intensity targets: a 4% cut (lower target) or 17% cut (tougher target) versus 2008 levels, rising to 30% and 43% respectively by 2035.
- Non-compliant ships pay $100 to $380 per tonne of excess emissions; overperforming ships receive a reward payment.
- The scheme is forecast to raise about $10 billion a year, versus the $60 billion a year that a straightforward carbon levy could have raised.
- Projected emissions cuts of about 8% by 2030 fall well short of the IMO's own 20% target.
- Biofuel is expected to be the lowest-cost compliance option for 85% of the fleet in the first five years, raising concerns about a biofuel demand surge.
