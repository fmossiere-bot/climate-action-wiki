# What is CSRD? The EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive

## What is it

The **Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)** is an EU rule requiring companies to disclose more detailed information on their climate and environmental performance, put in place partly to fight "greenwashing". It entered into force on 5 January 2023, and is part of the European Green Deal's climate action objectives, intended to enhance company disclosure on climate and environmental data and tackle greenwashing directly. The **European Green Deal** itself has three key targets: for the EU to be the first climate-neutral continent by 2050, to cut net greenhouse gas emissions by at least 55% by 2030 compared with 1990 levels, and to plant three billion additional trees across the EU by 2030.

Under CSRD, mandatory reporting standards developed by the **European Financial Reporting Advisory Group (EFRAG)** were introduced. The directive modernises and strengthens the rules on the social and environmental information that companies have to report. A broader set of large companies, along with listed SMEs, are required to report on sustainability, approximately 50,000 companies in total as originally scoped. The first companies applied the new rules for the 2024 financial year, with reports published from 2025.

## Why it matters

The new rules are meant to ensure investors and other stakeholders have access to the information they need to assess investment risks arising from climate change and other sustainability issues, and to build a culture of transparency about the impact companies have on people and the environment. Over the medium to long term, harmonising the information companies must provide is also intended to reduce reporting costs, since businesses will not have to respond to as many bespoke investor and regulator requests.

In Ireland, companies have been notably underprepared: one in five Irish companies say they are worried about the new rules, 7% say they do not understand the rules, and 46% did not understand that they might be audited independently on their reporting.

## Reporting standards: ESRS and EFRAG

Companies subject to CSRD report according to the **European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS)**. The draft standards were developed by EFRAG, previously known as the European Financial Reporting Advisory Group, an independent body bringing together a range of different stakeholders. The standards are tailored to EU policy while building on and contributing to international standardisation initiatives; the European Commission adopted the first set of standards by mid-2023, based on draft standards EFRAG published in November 2022.

## Why CSRD replaced the NFRD

CSRD replaced the earlier **Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD)**, which had established important principles for certain large companies to report sustainability information annually, including introducing a **"double materiality" perspective**, meaning companies had to report both on how sustainability issues affect their business and on their own impact on people and the environment. In practice, there was ample evidence that the information companies reported under NFRD was not sufficient: reports often omitted information investors and other stakeholders considered important, reported information was hard to compare between companies, and users were often unsure whether they could trust it.

These quality problems had real knock-on effects. Investors lacked a reliable overview of the sustainability-related risks companies were exposed to, at exactly the point when they increasingly needed that information, partly to meet their own disclosure requirements under the Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR), and partly because a credible market for green investment depends on investors knowing the sustainability impact of the companies they invest in. Without reliable information, money cannot be reliably channelled towards environmentally friendly activities. Poor reporting quality also created an accountability gap: high-quality, reliable public reporting helps build a culture of greater public accountability.

## October 2025 update: scope narrowed under the omnibus deal

Following the EU's omnibus agreement in the European Parliament, CSRD's scope was significantly narrowed. It was originally intended to apply to approximately **11,000 companies**; under the omnibus deal, it will now affect just **4,700 businesses**. Under the new threshold, only organisations with more than **1,000 employees and at least €450 million in annual revenue** are subject to the enhanced reporting requirements, reached after months of tension and a political balancing act in which the European People's Party (EPP) compromised with left-wing lawmakers.

These changes reflect the EU's broader deregulatory agenda, aiming to reduce administrative obligations by 25% for large firms and 35% for SMEs; the addition of a revenue threshold was a key concession in the negotiations. Climate transition plans were also diluted: companies were previously required to develop, act upon and report on these plans, but are now only obliged to develop and publish them. The omission of language on double materiality, previously reported to be under threat, leaves its final status uncertain, and the obligation to link corporate fines to turnover was removed. The revised proposal still required approval from the Council of the EU and the European Parliament before taking effect.

Separately, the EU also agreed new waste reduction measures for textiles and food around the same time, requiring fashion brands to pay for collection and recycling through Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) schemes, and introducing EU-wide food waste reduction targets.

## Key facts

- CSRD entered into force on 5 January 2023, originally set to cover around 11,000 companies plus listed SMEs, roughly 50,000 companies in total.
- The EU Green Deal targets climate neutrality by 2050, a 55% cut in net GHG emissions by 2030 (versus 1990), and three billion additional trees by 2030.
- 20% of Irish companies say they are worried about CSRD; 7% don't understand the rules; 46% didn't realise they might be independently audited.
- Companies report under the European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS), developed by EFRAG; the first standards were adopted by the European Commission by mid-2023.
- CSRD replaced the NFRD, which introduced the "double materiality" reporting principle but produced information investors found unreliable and hard to compare.
- The 2025 omnibus reform narrowed CSRD's scope from about 11,000 to about 4,700 companies, raising the threshold to 1,000+ employees and €450m+ revenue, and softened climate transition plan and double materiality requirements.
