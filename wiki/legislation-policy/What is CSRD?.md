# What is CSRD? The EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive

## What is it

The **Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)** is an EU rule that requires companies to disclose more detailed information about their climate and environmental performance. One of its aims is to fight "greenwashing". It came into force on 5 January 2023 and is part of the European Green Deal's climate goals, designed to improve what companies report on climate and environmental data and to tackle greenwashing head on. The **European Green Deal** itself has three main targets: make the EU the first climate-neutral continent by 2050, cut net greenhouse gas emissions by at least 55% by 2030 compared with 1990, and plant three billion extra trees across the EU by 2030.

CSRD introduced mandatory reporting standards developed by the European Financial Reporting Advisory Group (EFRAG). The directive updates and strengthens the rules on the social and environmental information companies must report. A wider group of large companies, plus listed SMEs, have to report on sustainability: approximately 50,000 companies in total, as originally planned. The first companies applied the new rules to the 2024 financial year, with reports published from 2025.

## Why it matters

The rules are meant to give investors and others the information they need to judge the investment risks that come from climate change and other sustainability issues. They are also meant to build a habit of openness about the impact companies have on people and the environment. Over time, making companies report the same information in the same way should also lower reporting costs, because businesses will face fewer one-off requests from investors and regulators.

Irish companies have been noticeably underprepared. One in five say they are worried about the new rules, 7% say they do not understand them, and **46%** did not realise they might be independently audited on their reporting.

## Reporting standards: ESRS and EFRAG

Companies covered by CSRD report using the **European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS)**. The draft standards were written by EFRAG, previously known as the European Financial Reporting Advisory Group, an independent body that brings together a range of stakeholders. The standards are tailored to EU policy while building on, and feeding into, international standard-setting. The European Commission adopted the first set by mid-2023, based on drafts EFRAG published in November 2022.

## Why CSRD replaced the NFRD

CSRD replaced the earlier **Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD)**. The NFRD set out important principles for certain large companies to report sustainability information each year. It introduced the idea of "double materiality": companies had to report both on how sustainability issues affect their business and on their own impact on people and the environment. In practice, there was plenty of evidence that what companies reported under the NFRD was not good enough. Reports often left out information that investors and others considered important, the figures were hard to compare between companies, and readers were often unsure whether they could trust them.

These quality problems had real consequences. Investors had no reliable picture of the sustainability risks companies faced, just when they needed it most. Partly they needed it to meet their own disclosure duties under the Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR). Partly it was because a credible market for green investment depends on investors knowing the sustainability impact of the companies they back. Without reliable information, money cannot be steered towards environmentally friendly activities. Poor reporting also left a gap in accountability, because good, reliable public reporting is what helps hold companies to account.

## October 2025 update: scope narrowed under the omnibus deal

After the EU's omnibus agreement in the European Parliament, the scope of CSRD was cut back sharply. It was originally meant to apply to approximately 50,000 companies (the earlier Non-Financial Reporting Directive covered about 11,000). Under the omnibus deal it will now affect just **4,700 businesses**. Under the new threshold, only organisations with more than 1,000 employees and at least €450 million in annual revenue face the stricter reporting rules. The deal came after months of tension and a political balancing act in which the European People's Party (EPP) reached a compromise with left-wing lawmakers.

These changes reflect the EU's wider push to cut red tape, which aims to reduce administrative obligations by 25% for large firms and 35% for SMEs. Adding a revenue threshold was a key concession in the negotiations. Climate transition plans were also watered down. Companies previously had to develop these plans, act on them and report on them. Now they only have to develop and publish them. The text no longer mentions double materiality, which had already been reported to be under threat, so its final status is uncertain. The requirement to link corporate fines to turnover was also removed. The revised proposal still needed approval from the Council of the EU and the European Parliament before taking effect.

Around the same time, the EU also agreed new measures to reduce textile and food waste. Fashion brands will have to pay for collection and recycling through Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) schemes, and the EU introduced food waste reduction targets for all member states.

## Key facts

- CSRD came into force on 5 January 2023 and was originally set to cover roughly 50,000 companies in total, including listed SMEs, up from about 11,000 under the earlier Non-Financial Reporting Directive.
- The EU Green Deal targets climate neutrality by 2050, a 55% cut in net greenhouse gas emissions by 2030 (against 1990) and three billion extra trees by 2030.
- 20% of Irish companies say they are worried about CSRD, 7% do not understand the rules, and 46% did not realise they might be independently audited.
- Companies report under the European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS), developed by EFRAG. The European Commission adopted the first standards by mid-2023.
- CSRD replaced the NFRD, which introduced the "double materiality" principle but produced information investors found unreliable and hard to compare.
- The 2025 omnibus reform narrowed CSRD's scope from about 50,000 to about 4,700 companies, raised the threshold to more than 1,000 employees and €450m in revenue, and softened the rules on climate transition plans and double materiality.
