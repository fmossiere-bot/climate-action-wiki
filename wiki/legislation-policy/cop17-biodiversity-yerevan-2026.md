# COP17 Biodiversity (Yerevan, 2026): the first progress check on the global nature targets

> **Editorial summary:** Original synthesis by Fabien Mossière drawing on the Écologie 360 magazine dossier (Christophe Doré) and cross-checked against primary sources at the Convention on Biological Diversity, IPBES, UNDP and the EESC. **COP17 is the halfway point between the 2022 Montreal Framework and its 2030 targets.** It is not designed to produce a new grand agreement — its value lies in the first honest global review of how far behind the Kunming-Montreal targets we are, and whether that review translates into stronger national plans, real finance and clear action on the 500 billion-dollar harmful-subsidies target. The IPBES Nexus Assessment (published December 2024) gives negotiators a menu of 71 response options that work across biodiversity, water, food, health and climate — Yerevan is the first biodiversity COP where it can be used. Two source-side corrections from the original magazine piece are documented in section 8.

## 1. Key facts at a glance

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| What | 17th Conference of the Parties to the UN Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD COP17) |
| When | 19 to 30 October 2026 |
| Where | Yerevan, Armenia |
| Theme | 'Taking Action for Nature' |
| Who | 196 Parties to the Convention, plus observers (science, civil society, Indigenous Peoples, youth, business) |
| Why it matters | First global review of progress on the 2030 nature targets agreed in Montreal in 2022 |

## 2. What the biodiversity COPs are for

The biodiversity COPs get less media attention than the climate COPs, but they come from the same place. The Convention on Biological Diversity was opened for signature at the Rio Earth Summit in 1992, together with the climate and desertification conventions. It now has 196 Parties. The United States is the main country that has not ratified it.

The Parties usually meet every two years. The Convention covers three areas:

- protecting and restoring ecosystems
- using living resources in a sustainable way
- sharing fairly the benefits that come from genetic resources

Two other agreements meet in Yerevan at the same time: the Cartagena Protocol on biosafety and the Nagoya Protocol on access to genetic resources and benefit sharing.

## 3. The background: Montreal 2022 and Cali 2024

**COP15 (Montreal, 2022)** adopted the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework. It sets 23 targets for 2030. The best known are:

- protect 30% of land and sea ('30x30', Target 3)
- restore 30% of degraded ecosystems (Target 2)
- cut the risk from pesticides and the loss of excess nutrients by half (Target 7)
- reduce subsidies that harm nature by at least 500 billion US dollars per year (Target 18)
- mobilise 200 billion US dollars per year for nature from all sources (Target 19)

**COP16 (Cali, 2024, finished in Rome in February 2025)** created the Cali Fund, which collects payments from companies that use digital genetic data. It also set up a permanent body for Indigenous Peoples and local communities, and agreed a plan for finance and for monitoring. Many countries arrived in Cali without an updated national plan, which was already a warning sign.

## 4. What is new at COP17: the first global review

Yerevan is the halfway point between 2022 and 2030. For the first time, the Parties will check together if the world is really moving towards the Montreal targets.

The review is based on two things:

- **National plans** (National Biodiversity Strategies and Action Plans, or NBSAPs), which say what each country intends to do.
- **Seventh National Reports**, which say what each country has actually done and with what results. These were due on 28 February 2026. The CBD Secretariat reported that 125 of the 196 Parties submitted on time, which it described as a record.

Early analyses show that national plans are often less ambitious than the global agreement. So COP17 has two jobs: measure the collective delay, and decide what extra effort is needed before 2030.

## 5. The Nexus report: a toolbox for the negotiators

The Nexus Assessment was published in December 2024 by IPBES, the science body often described as the 'IPCC for biodiversity'. It came out just after COP16, so Yerevan is the first biodiversity COP where it can be used.

The report links five issues that are usually handled separately: biodiversity, water, food, health and climate. Its main message is that acting on one issue alone often creates problems for the others. It proposes 71 response options that help several goals at the same time, for example:

- restoring forests, soils and wetlands
- developing farming that depends less on pesticides
- reducing food waste
- shifting towards sustainable, healthy diets

For COP17, the interest is practical. The report gives governments a menu of measures to make their national plans stronger while keeping a joined-up view. It is a scientific reference, not a legal text. Countries do not make commitments 'under' the Nexus report. Their commitments sit under the Kunming-Montreal Framework.

## 6. Main topics to watch in Yerevan

1. **The result of the global review.** How honest is the assessment, and does it lead to a clear decision asking countries to raise their effort?
2. **Ambition of national plans.** Do countries agree to update weak plans before 2030?
3. **Finance.** Rich countries promised 20 billion US dollars per year in international nature finance by 2025, and 30 billion by 2030. Yerevan will show if the first promise was kept. Payments into the Cali Fund will also be looked at closely.
4. **Harmful subsidies.** Target 18 asks for a reduction of at least 500 billion US dollars per year by 2030. This covers farming, fishing, fossil fuels and other sectors. It is politically the hardest target.
5. **Pesticides, fertilisers and habitat loss.** Observers hope for precise commitments here, not only general statements.
6. **30x30.** Progress on the quantity of protected areas, but also on their quality and management.
7. **Links with climate and land.** 2026 has three Rio Convention COPs: biodiversity in Yerevan, climate COP31 in November, and desertification COP17. There is pressure to make the three processes work better together.

## 7. Why this matters for Ireland and Europe

The European Union negotiates as a bloc at the CBD, so Ireland's position is part of the EU position. Two files at home are directly linked to the Montreal targets:

- the **EU Nature Restoration Law**, which requires each member state to submit a national restoration plan in 2026
- Ireland's **4th National Biodiversity Action Plan (2023 to 2030)**

Agriculture is the sensitive point in both, as it is in the global talks on pesticides, nutrients and subsidies.

## 8. Credibility and limitations

- **Source quality.** The starting article is a one-page summary from the French magazine Écologie 360. It is accurate overall but simplified. Dates, targets and the review process have been checked against CBD, IPBES, UNDP and EESC material.
- **Two points corrected from the source.** The source suggests countries present commitments 'within the framework of the Nexus report'. In fact the Nexus report is a scientific input, and the framework is Kunming-Montreal. The source also links the 500 billion dollar figure to pesticide and fertiliser subsidies only. Target 18 covers all subsidies harmful to biodiversity.
- **No enforcement.** The CBD has no sanctions. The previous set of targets (Aichi, 2010 to 2020) was not met in full for any target. The global review is a 'name and encourage' exercise, not a compliance system.
- **Incomplete data.** About one third of Parties had not submitted their national report by the deadline, so the review will have gaps. Self-reported data can also be optimistic.
- **Written before the event.** This article is dated one month before COP17. Outcomes are not known and the agenda may shift.

## 9. Bottom line

COP17 is a decisive meeting, but not a magic one. It will not produce a new grand agreement. Its value is in telling the truth about where the world stands at the halfway point to 2030, and in turning that into stronger national plans, real money and clear action on subsidies, pesticides and habitat loss. The Nexus report gives governments a tested list of solutions. Whether they pick them up is a political choice, not a scientific one.
