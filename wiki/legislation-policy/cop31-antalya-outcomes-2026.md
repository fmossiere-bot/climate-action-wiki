# What COP31 decided: the outcomes of the Antalya climate talks

**COP31 ended on the evening of Sunday 22 November 2026 without its main political deal.** The overarching "cover decision", which the hosts called the Antalya Pact, was rejected on the floor by the EU, the UK, Norway, Panama, Vanuatu, the Marshall Islands and Belize, mainly because it did not name fossil fuels. Dozens of smaller technical decisions were adopted, including on a just transition mechanism and loss and damage. The hardest questions, on finance, adaptation, cutting emissions and fossil fuels, were pushed to the Bonn talks in June 2027 and to COP32 in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, in November 2027 (We Don't Have Time live blog).

It was also the longest COP in history, running roughly two days past its scheduled Friday close, and the largest, with just under 120,000 registrations (We Don't Have Time live blog).

## What COP31 was

COP31 was the 31st yearly meeting of the countries that signed the UN climate convention. It ran in Antalya, on Türkiye's Mediterranean coast, from 9 November and was scheduled to finish at 18:00 on Friday 20 November. For the first time, two countries shared the job. Türkiye hosted, held the formal presidency through its environment minister **Murat Kurum**, and ran the voluntary Action Agenda. Australia led the negotiations through its climate and energy minister **Chris Bowen**, and Fiji hosted the pre-COP meeting for the Pacific. The background to this arrangement and the presidency's ten priorities are on [COP31 in Antalya: dates, structure and main topics](cop31-antalya-2026.md).

The summit was billed as an "implementation COP", about delivering past promises rather than making new ones. Few people expected sweeping breakthroughs ([Kite Insights](../sources/kite-insights-cop31-preview-2026.md)). The backdrop was tough: high energy prices after the Iran war, an exceptionally strong El Niño, and a US government outside the Paris process ([Kite Insights](../sources/kite-insights-cop31-preview-2026.md)). Both hosts also have large fossil fuel interests. Australia is the world's third-largest fossil fuel exporter and Türkiye relies heavily on coal ([We Don't Have Time](../sources/cop31-implementation-wdht-2026.md)).

## What was agreed, and what was not

UN climate chief Simon Stiell said "a large share" of the roughly 160 agenda items were adopted, while conceding that the consensus system "certainly fell short today" (We Don't Have Time live blog). Most of what passed is procedural or carries the substance forward to 2027. Several items, including national adaptation plans and funding for carbon market infrastructure under Article 6.2, were closed with short decisions that push the real work to June or November 2027 (We Don't Have Time live blog).

The main texts adopted, as reported in the live blog and by experts speaking to Carbon Brief, were:

- **Just transition.** The work programme on a fair transition for workers and communities continues for three more years, with a review in 2029. The new just transition mechanism, also called the Belém-Antalya mechanism, will be "operationalised" in phases: its functions and rules are to be drafted in Bonn in June 2027 for adoption at COP32. Proposals on a steering committee, trade measures and carbon pricing were dropped (We Don't Have Time live blog). A former EU negotiator, Paul Watkinson, said decisions like this were adopted "albeit often in a weakened form" (Carbon Brief).
- **Loss and damage.** A decision was adopted. The final draft no longer noted the gap between USD 2.8bn in requests to the loss and damage fund and USD 342m allocated, saying only that allocations are insufficient compared with demand (We Don't Have Time live blog).
- **Adaptation.** A new platform to help the least developed countries put their national adaptation plans into practice was set up. The final presidency texts on the Adaptation Fund would move it to serving only the Paris Agreement once its first income from the Article 6.4 carbon market is turned into cash, and fix its board at 17 members with a developing-country majority, though the sources do not confirm their final adoption. Alden Meyer of the think tank E3G said a decision on the global goal on adaptation was also adopted (Carbon Brief), though the live blog reported on the final morning that no draft on it had yet appeared.
- **Smaller items.** These include the Standing Committee on Finance, the Climate Technology Centre host and Article 6 carbon markets. COP32 was confirmed for Addis Ababa from 8 to 19 November 2027, with 2028 going to the Asia-Pacific group and 2029 to the Eastern European group (We Don't Have Time live blog).

Three texts were rejected and go to Bonn in June 2027: the **mitigation work programme** (the main forum on cutting emissions), response measures, and the action plan on climate education and public participation (Carbon Brief). Meyer said Russia and Saudi Arabia blocked the extension of the mitigation work programme, which he called "a shame" that would normally have drawn far more attention. A review of progress on the Paris long-term goal was deferred to COP32 (We Don't Have Time live blog).

The cover decision itself went through four versions. The first, on Monday 16 November, had 144 paragraphs and called for accelerated action to phase out fossil fuels. The second, on Thursday, had 80 paragraphs and dropped fossil fuels entirely. The third, at about 03:00 on Sunday, had 94 paragraphs, and a lightly edited fourth went to the closing plenary (We Don't Have Time live blog; The Guardian). After objections from the floor, Kurum told delegates: "We recognise that there is no consensus on this document. Therefore, these decisions have not been adopted."

## The fight over fossil fuels

At COP28 in Dubai in 2023, every country agreed to "transition away from fossil fuels". No COP since has agreed how to do it, and COP30 in Belém ended without even mentioning fossil fuels after resistance from oil producers ([COP30](COP30.md), [DW](../sources/taff-coalition-cop31-dw-2026.md)). Antalya repeated that fight, in public this time.

The second draft replaced fossil fuels with an "irreversible global transition towards low greenhouse gas emissions and climate resilience". The final drafts tried a compromise: they opened by "recalling the UAE Consensus in its entirety", a reference to the Dubai deal that includes the transition away, without saying the words. Under UN drafting rules, "recalls" restates what exists and creates no new obligation (We Don't Have Time live blog). Mohamed Adow of Power Shift Africa counted that the third draft "recognises" the crisis 40 times and "recalls" past decisions 15 times, but "decides" to act only five times.

Saudi Arabia was the leading objector to any fossil fuel wording, with the Arab Group behind it, according to insiders and analysts (We Don't Have Time live blog). Bowen said some parties had "insisted if it was mentioned, they would veto". "I would have been happy with transition away from fossil fuels being in the text, but that was not going to get support," he told reporters (The Guardian).

On the other side, the camp grew over the final week. On Friday 20 November, 23 countries, fronted by the Dutch climate minister Stientje van Veldhoven, demanded the wording be restored. On Saturday evening, 30 to 40 countries led by the UK's Miatta Fahnbulleh walked together into the presidency offices. By then the EU said about 130 countries backed a fossil fuel mention, and the Guardian describes a loose "progressive coalition" of about 130 parties (We Don't Have Time live blog; The Guardian). Those three figures describe different moments, not a contradiction: a press conference, a delegation, and a wider list of supporters.

Most of the 130 did not object on the final day. The Guardian's Fiona Harvey notes that for many developing countries finance mattered more than fossil fuel wording. India, speaking for the Like-Minded Developing Countries, neither accepted nor rejected the text, and China said it would accept it despite concerns "to keep the process moving forward" (We Don't Have Time live blog).

Many campaigners welcomed the rejection: Greenpeace said no deal was better than a deal with the fossil fuel industry. Others, such as C40 Cities, called it one of the darkest hours in climate diplomacy (We Don't Have Time live blog). UN Secretary-General António Guterres said the Dubai commitment still stands: "The task now is to accelerate that transition, finance and deliver it."

## Carbon capture: the paragraph that would not go away

The cover text carried a paragraph promoting carbon capture, utilisation and storage (CCUS, which catches CO2 from chimneys and stores or reuses it) and carbon dioxide removal. Early drafts called for at least 500 million tonnes of CO2 captured a year by 2035, working towards 1.3 to 2.5 billion tonnes (New Scientist). For scale, existing projects capture at most around 60 million tonnes a year, less than 0.2% of the 38.2 billion tonnes emitted from fossil fuels in 2026, so reaching 2.5 billion tonnes would need a 40-fold expansion in a decade, according to Neil Grant of Climate Analytics (We Don't Have Time live blog).

The third draft dropped the numbers but kept an aspiration of "gigatonne scale globally by 2035", and the very last edit softened "accelerating" capture to "increasing" it (We Don't Have Time live blog). Analysts read the paragraph as a trade: carbon capture for Saudi Arabia in return for the presidency's electrification target. One High Ambition Coalition diplomat alleged a "stitch-up", a claim the live blog could not confirm. The least developed countries, small islands and civil society groups wanted it deleted.

Lobbying was heavy. The Center for International Environmental Law counted a record 700 CCUS lobbyists at COP31, 45 of them inside national delegations, more than the delegates of the 50 smallest national delegations combined (New Scientist). The scientist Johan Rockström argued that some carbon removal is now unavoidable because the 1.5°C carbon budget is used up, but that it must be additional to ending fossil fuels, not a substitute (We Don't Have Time live blog). For the wider debate, see [the carbon capture myth page](../myths/carbon-capture-will-fix-it.md).

## Electrification: "35 by 35"

The presidency's flagship was a goal to raise electricity's share of final energy use to **35% by 2035**, up from about 23% today. The figure appears to come from a May 2026 International Renewable Energy Agency report, which found a 1.5°C path needs 35% by 2035 and over 50% by 2050. Regions would move unevenly, with the EU at about 42% by 2035 and Africa about 26% (We Don't Have Time live blog). See [Electrify Everything](../solutions/electrification/Electrify%20Everything.md) for why moving cars, heating and industry onto electricity matters.

The pledge reportedly gathered "around 90" supporters outside the negotiations, though Carbon Brief noted confusion over the exact count, and Bowen said "more than 90" (Carbon Brief; The Guardian). The presidencies also launched an electrification delivery package of 14 initiatives, on grid spending, coal transitions and clean power investment (Carbon Brief). Inside the negotiations, the goal survived every draft only as one voluntary option, and it fell with the cover decision. The Guardian's verdict is that COP31 failed to agree the goal but put electrification "firmly on the map".

The main criticism was that the target never said the electricity had to be clean. Indigenous groups and campaigners argued that an electrification goal without renewables could lock in more gas power. Bill Hare of Climate Analytics writes that dirty electrification could add a tenth of a degree of warming by mid-century (The Guardian, opinion).

<!-- tag: china -->
China gave the goal its clearest backing. In a joint statement on Friday 20 November, China and Australia welcomed the 35% target "on a voluntary basis", without mentioning fossil fuels or finance (We Don't Have Time live blog). Wang Yi, a senior adviser to the Chinese government, told the Guardian that China, which produces more than a quarter of global emissions, will pursue "clean end-use electrification" and share its cheap clean technology with others. Electricity already makes up nearly 30% of China's energy use, and it is on track for 35% by 2030. Wang said the target itself was "no problem", but that requiring all the power to be clean "may be a little bit difficult, even for China". He also called for more dialogue on trade barriers such as the EU's carbon border tax (The Guardian).

## Finance and adaptation: who pays

Money was the other fault line, and for many developing countries the bigger one. At COP29 in Baku in 2024, rich countries pledged $300bn a year in public money for poorer countries by 2035, plus help to raise $1tn more from private sources, but it has been slow to arrive (The Guardian). COP30 then agreed to triple adaptation finance by 2035 ([COP31 preview](cop31-antalya-2026.md)). Many poor countries are in debt crises and spend more coping with climate impacts than on health or education (The Guardian).

The fight in Antalya was over the **baseline**, the starting year from which tripling is measured, which decides how much money it really means. Developing countries wanted a figure of USD 120bn a year. Observers say France, as co-chair of the finance talks, pushed a 2019 baseline that would give about USD 60bn a year by 2035, half that amount, and campaigners handed it a "Fossil of the Day" award for it (We Don't Have Time live blog). The USD 120bn figure was dropped from the second draft. The last cover drafts kept tripling "in principle", asked for a technical report on baseline options, and set up an "Antalya-Addis Ababa road map on adaptation and resilience finance", but all of that fell with the cover decision (We Don't Have Time live blog). Harjeet Singh of the Fill the Fund campaign said donors "recalled" their treaty duty to pay "but deleted every number that would show whether they have".

The blame here runs in more than one direction. Meena Raman of the Third World Network argued that everything developing countries wanted, on adaptation, just transition and finance, was pushed to COP32, and pointed to EU climate commissioner Wopke Hoekstra saying before the talks that finance was "dead in the water" (Carbon Brief). ActionAid praised the EU's stand on fossil fuels but blamed it for blocking adaptation finance (We Don't Have Time live blog). The EU replied that it is the world's leading donor, with EUR 32.3bn from public sources and EUR 9.3bn mobilised from private sources in 2025 (We Don't Have Time live blog). The Guardian also judged that Australia made a "strategic error" by repeatedly saying "this is not a finance Cop".

The Pacific's own fund shows the gap on the ground. The Pacific Resilience Facility, which pays for damage from extreme weather, is still about USD 300m short of its USD 500m goal. Australia pledged USD 67m three years ago, and a showcase event with the Australian prime minister drew one new offer: USD 5m from the miner Andrew Forrest. "We have money for wars but not to save communities," said Albon Ishoda, a Marshall Islands diplomat (The Guardian). On the sidelines, seven countries including Kenya, Spain, Tuvalu and the UK backed small "polluter pays" levies on high-emitting sectors such as aviation (Carbon Brief), and philanthropies announced $73 million for the Tropical Forest Forever Facility, a rainforest fund still about $2 billion short of its target ([DW](../sources/tfff-funding-gap-dw-2026.md)).

## The 1.5°C overshoot

COP31 came months after scientists confirmed that the world will pass 1.5°C of warming (The Guardian). The UN's 2026 Emissions Gap Report found 1.5°C can no longer be met without overshoot, that the best realistic peak is 1.7 to 1.8°C, and that current policies lead to 2.8°C ([New Scientist](../sources/un-overshoot-2.8c-newscientist-2026.md)). The UN's own scorecard showed national plans would cut global emissions by only about 6% by 2035 compared with 2019, or 9% if fully delivered ([UNFCCC](../sources/unfccc-paris-progress-reports-2026.md)).

Sources differ on where we are today. The live blog and Bill Hare put warming at about 1.4°C, while the Guardian's Fiona Harvey writes that temperatures have now risen above 1.5°C. The gap is probably a single hot year against a long-term average, but the sources do not say which they mean.

The Guardian says COP31 was the first COP to recognise and begin discussing overshoot, though there was no agreement on what to do about it. The final cover draft expressed deep concern that warming will exceed 1.5°C within a few years and proposed a high-level dialogue on overshoot at the next meeting. The progressive coalition had asked for two dialogues and a science report. Because the cover decision was rejected, the dialogue was not formally set up (We Don't Have Time live blog).

For small islands, this is not abstract. The 39 members of the Alliance of Small Island States called the projected overshoot an unacceptable consequence of putting profit over people (We Don't Have Time live blog). A 2024 UN report cited NASA research projecting a five-fold rise in flooding days on Pacific islands by 2050, with places such as Tuvalu, Fiji and the Marshall Islands facing more than 50 flooding days a year (The Guardian). "For us, it's about existence," said Ishoda.

## Coalitions: progress outside the consensus

With 198 countries needing to agree, groups of willing countries are increasingly moving on their own. "We are moving from doing by consensus to by coalition," one veteran observer told Carbon Brief.

The main example is the **Santa Marta process** on transitioning away from fossil fuels, which grew out of a conference of 57 nations in Colombia in April 2026 ([Santa Marta conference](../solutions/energy-transition/santa-marta-fossil-fuel-conference-2026.md)). At COP31, about 25 countries had published or announced national roadmaps away from fossil fuels, up from zero at the last COP and one at Santa Marta, according to van Veldhoven. Vanuatu, Denmark, Spain, Portugal, Luxembourg, Ireland and Tuvalu joined that list during the summit (Carbon Brief; We Don't Have Time). Colombia itself has pulled out under its new president, Abelardo de la Espriella, who took office in August promising more oil and gas (We Don't Have Time, citing the Associated Press).

Other coalitions continued. The Powering Past Coal Alliance has 65 countries and the Beyond Oil and Gas Alliance 13 core members. Ethiopia and Denmark became co-chairs of an eight-nation "group of negative emitters" (Carbon Brief). Not everyone welcomes this "two-tier multilateralism". Some developing countries worry it distracts from the treaty obligations of rich countries. Vanuatu's climate minister Ralph Regenvanu put it this way: such action "cannot take the place of negotiations, which need to lead to implementation" (Carbon Brief).

## Key players

**The two presidencies.** The split model was untested, and the Guardian concludes it slowed things down: consulting two presidencies "takes twice as long", and the gaps between drafts showed it. Observers also criticised a lack of transparency, with some countries shown only parts of drafts (We Don't Have Time live blog). Bowen said Australia "left nothing on the field" but that the red lines were "unbridgeable". Fiji's climate minister Lynda Tabuya defended him on the floor: "The failure here is not yours to carry. It is the responsibility of all parties." Joanna Depledge of the University of Cambridge noted that Kurum can fall back on the Action Agenda, while Bowen cannot (Carbon Brief).

Two Guardian pieces judge Australia's role, and both are commentary rather than straight reporting. In an analysis, Graham Readfearn argues the "f-word failure" was shared, rooted in a consensus process that lets a few countries block. In an opinion piece, Bill Hare, chief executive of Climate Analytics and a long-time adviser to small island states, argues co-hosting "didn't work" for a country whose coal exports have tripled since 1991 and whose LNG exports have grown 20-fold. He puts Australia's share of global fossil carbon emissions, at home and through exports, at about 4.3%.

**Petrostates.** Saudi Arabia and Russia, the two largest CO2 exporters in history ([Guardian](../sources/co2-exporters-history-carbon-brief-guardian-2026.md)), resisted fossil fuel wording, pushed carbon capture and blocked the mitigation work programme (Carbon Brief).

**Panama.** Its environment minister Juan Carlos Navarro wrote an open letter setting eight demands, including a cap on oil production and majority voting when consensus fails, and his attack on a "petroleum mafia" became a rallying cry (Carbon Brief; We Don't Have Time live blog). Yet at the International Maritime Organization, Panama has led talks on the weakest option for a shipping carbon levy (We Don't Have Time live blog).

**Small islands and the Pacific.** Pacific states wanted fossil fuels named, 1.5°C kept alive, and the International Court of Justice's advisory opinion on climate duties referenced. A UN special rapporteur said that opinion was challenged in the negotiating rooms (The Guardian). Vanuatu and the Marshall Islands rejected the final text. Greenpeace's Shiva Gounden described a "sickening disconnect" between the talks and the damage at home.

**Africa and the least developed countries.** "We didn't come here to beg," said Zanetor Agyeman-Rawlings, chair of the African Group of Negotiators. The 44 least developed countries called for carbon capture to be removed. Ghana accused Kurum of cutting off its delegation in the final plenary, and he later apologised (We Don't Have Time live blog).

<!-- tag: eu, germany, france -->
**The EU.** The EU led the formal objection and warned days earlier that European citizens would not accept a "fossil fuel" COP. Germany's environment minister Carsten Schneider said publicly that Germany could not agree to the text, and France's Monique Barbut called it a backward step (We Don't Have Time live blog). The EU was also criticised by developing countries and campaigners for its stance on adaptation finance, as set out above.

## Other stories from Antalya

**Disinformation and public awareness.** A survey of 4,009 adults for Climate Action Against Disinformation, taken from 3 to 12 November, found that 57% of people in Türkiye and 68% in Australia had not heard of COP31 or were unsure, and 82% of Australians saw little or no coverage of it. Yet 77% of people in Türkiye want more government action, and solar is viewed favourably by 93% in Türkiye and 83% in Australia. Among those with a view, 51% in Türkiye and 64% in Australia see climate disinformation as organised and deliberate, and many people underestimate how many others support climate action (CAAD). A UN-backed initiative also asked AI companies to disclose their energy, carbon, water and land footprints ([The Climate Watch](../sources/un-ai-climate-misinformation-cop31-2026.md)).

**AI and data centres.** The Turkish presidency and the International Telecommunication Union launched the Antalya Artificial Intelligence Pledge on 16 November, to encourage cleaner power, more efficient data centres and transparency on AI's footprint. Only nine countries had signed by 17 November: Andorra, Brazil, Colombia, Luxembourg, Sierra Leone, Somalia, South Korea, Turkey and Uganda. The pledge is voluntary and its call for "low- and zero-emission energy" leaves room for gas. Electricity use by AI-focused data centres rose 50% in 2025, according to the International Energy Agency (New Scientist). See [AI data centres and the energy crisis](../sectors/digital/ai-data-centre-energy-crisis.md).

**Law and accountability.** The news agency AFP announced at COP31 a dedicated desk covering climate and environmental law ([Covering Climate Now](../sources/afp-climate-litigation-desk-ccnow-2026.md)). Five campaign groups asked the UN to review FIFA's place in its Sports for Climate Action Framework, citing its partnership with Saudi Aramco and a 104-match World Cup ([Guardian](../sources/fifa-un-sports-climate-complaint-guardian-2026.md)).

## Is there a precedent?

Carbon Brief asked veteran observers whether Antalya resembles COP6 in The Hague in 2000, which was suspended without a deal, or Copenhagen in 2009, which ended with an accord that was only "taken note of". Most said no. Those summits failed at their core legal task, while COP31 adopted most of its mandated decisions and lost a political add-on. Catherine Abreu of the International Climate Politics Hub pointed out that cover decisions only became routine after Glasgow in 2021, and that most COPs have ended without one. She called the rejection "necessary to protect the process".

Others see something new. Climate lawyer Farhana Yamin called it "the first time those who want a higher level of ambition actively reject the lowest common denominator on offer", dividing fossil fuel exporters from "the rest of the world". Meena Raman of the Third World Network called it unprecedented because the presidency tried to settle in the cover text what the formal negotiations could not. Alden Meyer of E3G compared Ethiopia's task to Mexico's at Cancún in 2010, rebuilding trust after Copenhagen.

## What happens next

The unfinished files now move along a busy calendar:

- **Bonn, June 2027:** the mitigation work programme, response measures and climate education plan return, and the just transition mechanism's rules are drafted.
- **Tuvalu, July 2027:** the second conference on transitioning away from fossil fuels, co-hosted by Tuvalu and Ireland, with a preparatory meeting in Dublin. Tuvalu will draw up its own roadmap with the International Institute for Applied Systems Analysis (We Don't Have Time).
- **Addis Ababa, 8 to 19 November 2027:** COP32 inherits finance, adaptation, the just transition mechanism, the long-term goal review and the fossil fuel question. Ethiopia promised a "COP of hope". The AI pledge will also be reviewed there (New Scientist).

Reform of the process is now openly discussed. Stiell said how the process evolves is a live conversation he is having with countries, and Al Gore called it "an outrage" that "a handful of petrostates have veto power over any mention of fossil fuels" (We Don't Have Time live blog; The Guardian). Bowen's "unbridgeable divides" leave Ethiopia a year to find common ground before the next summit begins (The Guardian).

## What it means for Ireland and the EU

<!-- tag: ireland, eu -->
Ireland was unusually visible. Darragh O'Brien, Ireland's Minister for Climate, Energy and the Environment, delivered the EU's formal objection, calling the omission of fossil fuels "unconscionable" and saying the text ignored the implementation gap and risked widening the divide with what 1.5°C requires (We Don't Have Time live blog; The Guardian). Ireland was among the 23 countries demanding the fossil fuel wording back, and announced it will publish a national roadmap away from fossil fuels (Carbon Brief).

<!-- tag: ireland -->
Ireland also takes on a leading role outside the UN talks. With Tuvalu, it formally took over the Santa Marta process on 19 November and will co-host the second conference in July 2027, with a preparatory meeting in Dublin. O'Brien invited producer countries to join, saying their engagement is "critical and crucial to the success of any transition" (We Don't Have Time). Former Irish president Mary Robinson warned the summit lacked urgency on the record El Niño, which could push about 50 million people into severe hunger (We Don't Have Time live blog).

For the EU, Antalya leaves two open questions. Its stand on fossil fuels drew praise, but its position on adaptation finance drew anger from the Global South, and its carbon border tax is becoming a rallying point for a broader coalition of critics, according to Chatham House's Pelin Zorlu (We Don't Have Time live blog). Both will be central at COP32.
