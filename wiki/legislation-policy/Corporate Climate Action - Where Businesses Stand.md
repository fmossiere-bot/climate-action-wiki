### Summary

More companies than ever have made climate commitments, but a pledge is not the same as action. Most targets focus on the emissions that are easiest to measure, leave out the bigger picture, and lean on tools such as carbon offsets that often do not deliver what they promise. The gap between ambition and real progress is still large. In 2025, some of the world's largest companies also visibly stepped back, which raised fair questions about whether the corporate climate movement is holding together.

---

### Key Numbers

- Around **60%** of all Forbes 2000 companies now have net-zero pledges, and more than 9,700 companies had had their targets validated by the Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi) by early 2026
- By early 2026, 9,764 companies hold validated near-term targets and 2,325 hold validated net-zero targets
- **72% of firms** in a large US sample are not on track to meet their climate targets
- Companies with approved science-based targets cut their scope 1 and 2 emissions by 25% between 2015 and their latest reporting, while global emissions rose by 3.4% over the same period
- Only **10%** of assessed companies have net-zero targets that include their most material scope 3 emissions
- 40% of millennials have taken a job because of a company's sustainability commitments
- Over 50% of CEOs surveyed either do not believe they produce meaningful emissions or lack the tools to measure them

### Key Terms

Before looking at what companies are doing, it helps to pin down the main terms. They are often used loosely, and sometimes in misleading ways.

> **Net-zero** means that an organisation has reduced its emissions to a level close to zero, and that whatever small residual emissions remain are balanced by verified carbon removal. It is not the same as being "carbon neutral" through purchasing offsets.

**Scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions** describe where a company's emissions come from:

- **Scope 1** covers direct emissions from the company's own operations, such as fuel burned in boilers or company vehicles
- **Scope 2** covers indirect emissions from the electricity the company buys
- **Scope 3** covers all other indirect emissions up and down the supply chain, including purchased goods, staff travel, the use of the company's products and waste disposal. For most organisations this is the largest category, and the hardest to tackle

> **Science-based targets** are emissions reduction goals aligned with what climate science says is needed to limit warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius. They are set through the SBTi and require companies to follow a defined methodology rather than setting their own benchmarks.

**Offsetting** means paying for emissions cuts or carbon removal somewhere else to make up for your own emissions. Offset quality varies enormously, and many offsets have been shown not to deliver the reductions they claim.

### Why Companies Are Acting

Companies act on climate for a mix of reasons, some of them pressure and some of them opportunity.

**Regulation.** More and more governments require companies to disclose their climate-related financial risks. In Europe, the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) is pushing tens of thousands of companies to measure and report their emissions more carefully, including scope 3. CSRD can act as a catalyst, and measuring and reporting scope 3 is now a prerequisite for setting a net-zero target under SBTi rules. The direction is clear, even though the exact scope of CSRD has been revised as part of the EU's wider simplification process in 2025 and 2026.

**Market and technology risk.** In many sectors, lower-carbon alternatives are coming onto the market. In cement, for example, start-ups claim they can cut embedded emissions by 70 to 100%, which threatens the business models of the big established firms. Any company whose product or service has a high carbon footprint risks being pushed aside.

**Customer pressure.** Consumer research keeps showing that sustainability shapes buying decisions for a large share of shoppers. In the US consumer goods sector, around half of all growth between 2013 and 2018 came from products marketed as sustainable.

**Talent.** Organisations that do nothing on climate risk losing staff to competitors that do more. This is most visible among younger workers, and it is not limited to Europe and North America.

**Supply chain disruption.** Extreme weather is already disrupting global supply chains, and the risk is growing.

**Care.** Beyond the business case, many employees and leaders simply care. Pressure from staff has become a real force in large organisations, and it is becoming more common.

### The Current State: A Mixed and Worsening Picture

The number of corporate climate commitments has risen sharply. Net-zero pledges are now common among the world's largest companies. Yet halfway through what the United Nations calls the critical decade for climate action, progress on the ground is limited. Many companies still have no credible plan to reach their targets, and there is little evidence of deeper changes to how they do business.

The University of Birmingham analysed over 1,200 sustainability reports from Fortune Global 500 companies. It found that net-zero stories often work as symbolic reputation management rather than as a plan for real change.

**The retreat of 2025.** What made 2025 worrying was a visible rollback of commitments by some of the world's largest companies, led by oil and gas. BP scrapped its pledge to cut output by 40% by 2030, raised its production targets and halved its investment in renewables. Shell expects its fossil fuel output to rise every year through 2030, and TotalEnergies cut its clean-energy spending from **$5 billion to $4.5 billion**. Several major banks also backtracked. HSBC pushed its net-zero target back to 2050, citing economic changes, and Wells Fargo dropped its plans to green its lending altogether.

Coca-Cola abandoned its 2025 goal to cut virgin plastic use by 3 million tonnes and pushed its recycled-content targets back to 2035. PepsiCo cancelled its 2030 reusable packaging target.

**Quiet progress in the background.** Not all the news is bad. PwC's 2025 decarbonisation report describes an era of "quiet progress". Companies avoid announcing climate pledges that invite scrutiny, and instead make progress out of the spotlight. Some keep investing in durable carbon removal, tightening what they ask of suppliers and changing how they operate, without telling anyone.

**Most targets still leave out scope 3.** Climate Action 100+ found that only 10% of assessed companies have net-zero targets that include their most material scope 3 emissions. Scope 3 is usually most of a company's footprint, so targets that cover only scope 1 and 2 are incomplete.

**Offsets are still a problem.** A wide range of accounting practices blur how much progress companies are really making, including market-based electricity accounting and carbon credits used across sectors. Many companies still claim to be carbon neutral because they bought carbon credits, and many do not disclose key details about those purchases.

### What Is Changing: SBTi Version 2.0

The rules that decide what counts as a credible corporate climate target are being updated too. In November 2025, SBTi published a revised draft of its Corporate Net-Zero Standard Version 2.0. It brings in separate targets for each scope, a more flexible approach to scope 3 emissions, and a new way of recognising companies that take responsibility for the emissions they still produce. Version 2.0 is expected to become mandatory for new targets from January 2028.

Under the new standard, scope 3 targets will focus on the significant emissions categories, those above 5% of total scope 3. The accepted methods have also been widened to include working with suppliers, emissions intensity benchmarks and circularity targets.

The new standard keeps the principle that cutting emissions comes first. Carbon credits can only be used to neutralise the residual emissions left at the net-zero point, estimated at around **10%** of a company's remaining emissions by 2050, and for voluntary action beyond the value chain. Credits do not count towards reduction targets.

SBTi is also proposing a review every five years, in step with CSRD. At the end of each target period, companies would assess their progress and set new targets that take past performance into account. SBTi will also carry out spot checks at any point in the cycle to confirm companies are complying.

### What Good Practice Looks Like

Despite the overall picture, some companies are setting a higher bar. Credible climate plans tend to share these elements:

**Setting science-based targets through SBTi.** This gives an externally checked, consistent method aligned with the 1.5 degree pathway.

**Covering all three scopes.** A plan that ignores scope 3 is incomplete. Companies need to work with suppliers, customers and partners to tackle the full footprint of their products and services.

**Setting interim targets.** Near-term milestones keep companies accountable and stop the hard work being put off indefinitely.

**Using internal carbon pricing.** Putting a price on carbon inside the organisation, and applying it to investment decisions and budgets, changes behaviour. Research shows it cuts emissions per employee by around 13 to 15%. The recommended range is **50 to 100 US dollars per tonne** by 2030.

**Being transparent.** Publishing data, including when the results are disappointing, is a sign of real commitment. So is disclosing clearly how offsets are used, and in what volume.

**Using offsets sparingly.** Offsets should only cover residual emissions that cannot be eliminated, and only where it can be shown that the reduction would not have happened anyway (this is called additionality). Long-lasting carbon removal, such as direct air capture or biochar, is more credible than short-lived forestry credits.

---

### What This Means in Practice

Companies have been persuaded to build climate strategies, mainly by setting targets and disclosing emissions. Those targets have not yet led to real changes in how companies operate or in their business models, the kind of change needed to limit global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius.

The standards are getting stricter, and European regulation is pushing disclosure further down the supply chain. At the same time, some of the world's best-known companies are stepping back from earlier commitments, citing economic and political pressure.

The organisations best placed for the years ahead will be those that treat climate action as part of how they run the business, not as a communications exercise. That means setting targets grounded in science, measuring carefully, and reporting openly on both progress and shortfalls.
