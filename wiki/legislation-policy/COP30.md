# COP30

## What is it

**COP30** was the 30th meeting of the parties to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change. It was held in Belem, Brazil, from 10 to 21 November 2025. It was the first COP in the Amazon region, a deliberate choice by the Brazilian presidency to put the links between forests, biodiversity and climate at the centre of the talks. It also came ten years after the Paris Agreement was adopted, so it was a moment to look back on a decade of progress and shortfalls.

More than 190 countries took part, and the talks came close to collapse. A coalition of more ambitious countries clashed with oil-producing states over whether to commit to a roadmap for phasing out fossil fuels. A deal was reached in the end, but **the final text made no mention of fossil fuels**, which shows how hard that fight had become.

## Why it matters

Current national climate pledges (NDCs) still put the world on course for around 2.3 to 2.5°C of warming, well above the Paris Agreement's aim of 1.5°C. Extreme weather caused over $300 billion in damage in 2024 (Swiss Re Institute), hitting low-income and vulnerable countries hardest. Nearly 500,000 people die every year from extreme heat (The Lancet). Forest loss hit new records in the year before COP30, including in the Amazon itself. What the summit decided on finance, adaptation and fossil fuels shapes how fast, and how fairly, the world responds.

## Climate finance and the $1.3 trillion goal

COP29 was known as the "Finance COP". There, countries agreed to scale up climate finance for developing countries to **$1.3 trillion a year by 2035**. Under the New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG) agreed at COP29, developed countries are expected to lead in raising at least $300 billion a year by 2035 for climate action in developing countries. The Baku to Belem Roadmap, a joint plan by the COP29 and COP30 presidencies, sets out how to grow total international climate finance from all sources, public and private, to the full $1.3 trillion a year by 2035.

Adaptation has its own gap. Developing countries (excluding China) face a shortfall of up to **$365 billion a year** (UNEP Adaptation Gap Report 2025), against current funding of roughly $40 billion a year. Every $1 spent on adaptation is estimated to bring more than $10 in benefits over ten years (WRI analysis). At COP30, nations agreed to triple adaptation funding, up from the earlier pledge to double it. But the goal of roughly $120 billion a year was pushed back five years, to 2035 instead of the originally proposed 2030. Harjeet Singh of the Satat Sampada Climate Foundation called the outcome "an insult to every community currently underwater or on fire". He argued that the refusal to commit to $300 billion a year straight away "leaves the unprepared defenceless against inevitable ruin." For context, $300 billion a year is the size of the overall climate finance goal agreed at COP29 (the NCQG above), which covers far more than adaptation; the adaptation target itself is the roughly $120 billion a year.

## National climate pledges (NDCs)

Countries were due to submit new NDCs, their national climate plans, before COP30. As of September 2025, only 64 countries covering 30% of global emissions had done so. By 9 November, that had risen to 108 countries (including the EU and its 27 member states), covering 71% of emissions. By the end of the summit, some 122 countries had submitted new pledges. Even so, these plans would only cut global emissions by **around 10% by 2035** compared with 2019 levels, far short of what is needed to stay within 1.5°C.

Among the G20, the world's largest emitters, twelve put forward new NDCs: Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, the European Union, Indonesia, Japan, Russia, Turkiye, South Africa, the United Kingdom and the United States. The US NDC was in effect cancelled when President Trump signed an order to withdraw the country from the Paris Agreement, effective January 2026, though the pledge is still officially listed with the UNFCCC. The EU agreed to cut greenhouse gas emissions by 90% by 2040. The compromises included allowing up to 5% of the cuts to come from international carbon credits, delaying the new ETS2 carbon pricing system to 2028, and setting a 2035 target of a 66 to 72.5% cut on 1990 levels.

The UN's Global Stocktake set out the direction needed: move away from fossil fuels, triple renewable energy, double energy efficiency, halt deforestation and cut transport emissions.

## Nature, forests and the Tropical Forests Forever Facility

Food systems produce around one-third of global emissions, have historically caused 90% of deforestation, and use most of the world's fresh water. That puts them at the centre of any serious plan for nature and climate. At the COP30 Leaders' Summit, Brazil formally launched the **Tropical Forests Forever Facility (TFFF)**, which aims to give long-term, predictable funding to countries that protect and sustainably manage their tropical forests. Brazil, Indonesia, Norway and Portugal announced initial pledges, and almost 50 countries expressed support. Brazil has set a $125 billion target for the facility. It aims to raise $10 billion first from governments and philanthropies, to draw in more private, corporate and philanthropic investment.

Countries also committed to a package to strengthen the rights and funding of Indigenous Peoples and local communities. It includes an Intergovernmental Land Tenure Commitment, an improved Forest Tenure Pledge 2.0 building on an earlier $1.7 billion pledge, and a commitment to send at least 20% of TFFF finance to Indigenous Peoples and local communities. Countries also agreed to develop Sustainable Ocean Plans and a five-year roadmap for the Ocean and Climate Change Dialogue, in step with NDC and National Adaptation Plan cycles.

Even so, Brazil's environment minister Marina Silva did not secure a roadmap to end deforestation in the core agreement. The proposal died after it was tied to the fossil fuel phase-out roadmap. Toerris Jaeger of Rainforest Foundation Norway described this as a major setback, adding: "It is disappointing that countries did not agree to develop concrete plans to halt deforestation."

## The fossil fuel phase-out fight

A coalition of around 90 developed and developing nations pushed for the final COP30 text to commit to a roadmap for phasing out fossil fuels. Oil-producing states, including Saudi Arabia and its allies, blocked it, and the final decision made no direct mention of fossil fuels. It did include an indirect reference to the "UAE consensus", the wider COP28 package agreed in Dubai in 2023, which contained the first pledge to transition away from fossil fuels. Dr Joanna Depledge, a COP expert at Cambridge, called this a step backwards, arguing the Dubai fossil fuel language was being "deliberately diluted and obscured, not highlighted."

Blocked from the formal decision, the Brazilian presidency said the roadmap would go ahead outside the UN process instead. It was merged with a plan backed by Colombia and around 90 other countries, with a follow-up summit set for April 2026. Nikki Reisch of the Center for International Environmental Law said: "Those governments committed to tackling the climate crisis at its source are uniting to move forward outside the UN, under the leadership of Colombia and Pacific Island states, to phase out fossil fuels rapidly, equitably, and in line with 1.5C."

**Update, Santa Marta Conference (April 2026):** The promised summit took place in Santa Marta, Colombia, from 28 to 30 April 2026. Nearly 60 countries agreed to develop national roadmaps to phase out fossil fuel production and use. France published the first national roadmap by a developed country, and Colombia published a draft during the conference. A second conference is planned for Tuvalu in early 2027, co-hosted by Ireland. See the [Santa Marta fossil fuel conference](../solutions/energy-transition/santa-marta-fossil-fuel-conference-2026.md) page for full details.

**Update, Bonn intersessional and COP31 Turkey (June 2026):** At the June 2026 Bonn intersessional, the technical meeting that prepares the COP agenda, the Santa Marta initiative got a working name, Transition Away From Fossil Fuels (TAFF). How it will be organised is being negotiated behind closed doors. UN climate chief Simon Stiell pointed to the energy crisis caused by the Iran war, arguing that relying on fossil fuels now means countries are "importing inflation and economic instability." Outgoing COP30 president Andre Correa do Lago described the shift as a move from "COPs focused on negotiations to COPs focused on implementation." COP31 will be held in Antalya, Türkiye, on 9-20 November 2026, with Australia leading the negotiations and Fiji hosting the Pre-COP. See the [COP31 Antalya overview](cop31-antalya-2026.md). Meanwhile, the tripling of adaptation finance agreed at Belem is stuck on a dispute over the starting point: does "triple" mean three times the roughly $20 billion baseline of 2019, or the roughly $40 billion of 2025? African lead negotiator Antwi Boasiako Amoah warned that Africa cannot pay for adaptation by taking on more debt, and cautioned that TAFF risks splitting the multilateral process. Source: [DW, Tim Schauenberg, 18 June 2026](../sources/dw-oil-nations-taff-2026.md).

## Just transition mechanism

Countries agreed a **just transition mechanism (JTM)**, a plan to make sure the global move to a green economy is fair and protects the rights of workers, women and Indigenous people. Climate Action Network International, a large coalition of campaign groups, welcomed it as one of the strongest rights-based outcomes in the history of UN climate talks. Its executive director, Tasneem Essop, said: "The adoption of JTM was a win shaped by years of pressure from civil society. This outcome didn't fall from the sky: it was carved out through struggle, persistence and the moral clarity of those living on the frontlines of climate breakdown. Governments must now honour this JTM with real action. Anything less is a betrayal." Early attempts to attach dedicated funding to the JTM failed.

## In the news: South Korea's coal pledge

At COP30, South Korea said it was joining the Powering Past Coal Alliance, a group of around 60 nations and 120 regional governments, businesses and organisations committed to phasing out coal. It pledged to close all coal-fired power plants by 2040. South Korea has the world's seventh-largest fleet of coal power stations. It is also the world's fourth-largest importer of thermal coal, behind China, India and Japan, with about 8% of global trade in the fuel, and coal supplies around 30% of its electricity. The decision matters for Australia, because South Korea is Australia's third-biggest coal export market. The Australian government has been urged to prepare for falling thermal coal exports and to speed up green industries in response.

## Key facts

- COP30 ran from 10 to 21 November 2025 in Belem, Brazil, the first COP held in the Amazon region.
- Current NDCs leave the world on track for roughly 2.3 to 2.5°C of warming.
- Climate finance goal: $1.3 trillion a year by 2035, with developed countries mobilising at least $300 billion a year of that under the NCQG.
- Adaptation funding was tripled at COP30, but the roughly $120 billion a year target was pushed back to 2035, from an initially proposed 2030.
- The final COP30 text contained no direct mention of fossil fuels. A coalition of around 90 countries is pursuing a phase-out roadmap outside the UN process instead.
- Brazil launched the Tropical Forests Forever Facility with a $125 billion target, aiming to raise $10 billion initially from governments and philanthropies.
- South Korea joined the Powering Past Coal Alliance, pledging to close all coal plants by 2040.
