# COP30

## What is it

**COP30** was the 30th meeting of the parties to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, held in Belem, Brazil, from 10 to 21 November 2025. It was the first COP held in the Amazon region, a deliberate choice by the Brazilian presidency to put the connection between forests, biodiversity and climate at the centre of the talks. The summit also fell ten years after the Paris Agreement was adopted, making it a moment to take stock of a decade of progress and shortfalls.

The talks brought together more than 190 countries and came close to collapse, as a coalition of ambitious countries clashed with petrostates over whether to commit to a roadmap for phasing out fossil fuels. A deal was eventually reached, but **the final text made no mention of fossil fuels**, the clearest sign of how difficult that fight had become.

## Why it matters

Current national climate pledges (NDCs) still leave the world on track for around 2.3 to 2.5°C of warming, well above the Paris Agreement's 1.5°C aspiration. Extreme weather caused over $300 billion in damages in 2024 (Swiss Re Institute), hitting low-income and vulnerable countries hardest, and nearly 500,000 people die every year from extreme heat (The Lancet). Forest loss hit new records in the year before COP30, including in the Amazon itself. The summit's outcomes on finance, adaptation and fossil fuels shape how fast, and how fairly, the world responds to those trends.

## Climate finance and the $1.3 trillion goal

COP29 was known as the "Finance COP", where countries agreed to scale up climate finance to **$1.3 trillion a year by 2035** for climate action in developing countries. Under the **New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG)** agreed at COP29, developed countries are expected to lead in mobilising at least **$300 billion a year by 2035** for climate action in developing countries. The **Baku to Belem Roadmap**, a joint plan by the COP29 and COP30 presidencies, sets out the strategy to scale total international climate finance, from all sources, public and private, to the full $1.3 trillion a year by 2035.

On adaptation specifically, developing countries (excluding China) face a funding shortfall estimated at up to **$365 billion a year** (UNEP Adaptation Gap Report 2025), against current funding of roughly $40 billion a year. Every $1 spent on climate adaptation is estimated to generate more than $10 in benefits over ten years (WRI analysis). At COP30, nations agreed to **triple adaptation funding**, a step up from the previous pledge to double it, but the roughly $120 billion a year goal was pushed back five years, to 2035 rather than the originally proposed 2030. Harjeet Singh of the Satat Sampada Climate Foundation called the outcome "an insult to every community currently underwater or on fire", arguing that the refusal to commit to $300 billion a year immediately "leaves the unprepared defenceless against inevitable ruin."

## National climate pledges (NDCs)

Countries were due to submit new NDCs, their national climate plans, in the run-up to COP30. As of September 2025, only 64 countries covering 30% of global emissions had done so; by 9 November, that had risen to 108 countries (including the EU and its 27 member states), covering 71% of emissions. By the end of the summit, some 122 countries had submitted new pledges. Even so, the submitted plans would only cut global emissions by around 10% by 2035 compared with 2019 levels, far short of what is needed to stay within 1.5°C.

Among the G20, the world's largest emitters, twelve countries put forward new NDCs: Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, the European Union, Indonesia, Japan, Russia, Turkiye, South Africa, the United Kingdom and the United States. The US NDC was effectively voided after President Trump signed an order to withdraw the country from the Paris Agreement, effective January 2026, though the pledge remains officially listed with the UNFCCC. The EU reached a deal to cut greenhouse gas emissions by 90% by 2040, with compromises including allowing up to 5% of reductions to come from international carbon credits, delaying the new ETS2 carbon pricing system to 2028, and setting a 2035 target of a 66 to 72.5% cut on 1990 levels.

The UN's Global Stocktake set out the direction needed: transition away from fossil fuels, triple renewable energy, double energy efficiency, halt deforestation and cut transport emissions.

## Nature, forests and the Tropical Forests Forever Facility

Food systems generate around one-third of global emissions, have historically driven 90% of deforestation, and use the majority of the world's freshwater, making them central to any credible nature and climate agenda. At the COP30 Leaders' Summit, Brazil formally launched the **Tropical Forests Forever Facility (TFFF)**, a mechanism designed to provide long-term, predictable financing to countries that protect and sustainably manage their tropical forests. Brazil, Indonesia, Norway and Portugal announced initial funding pledges, and almost 50 countries expressed support. Brazil has set a $125 billion target for the facility and aims to raise $10 billion initially from governments and philanthropies, intended to spur further private, corporate and philanthropic investment.

Countries also committed to a package to strengthen Indigenous Peoples' and local communities' rights and finance, including an Intergovernmental Land Tenure Commitment, an improved Forest Tenure Pledge 2.0 building on a prior $1.7 billion pledge, and a commitment to direct at least 20% of TFFF finance to Indigenous Peoples and local communities. Countries also agreed to develop Sustainable Ocean Plans and a five-year roadmap for the Ocean and Climate Change Dialogue, aligned with NDC and National Adaptation Plan cycles.

Despite this, Brazil's environment minister Marina Silva failed to secure a roadmap on ending deforestation in the core agreement. The proposal was killed after being tied to the fossil fuel phase-out roadmap, in what Rainforest Foundation Norway's Toerris Jaeger described as a major setback, adding: "It is disappointing that countries did not agree to develop concrete plans to halt deforestation."

## The fossil fuel phase-out fight

A coalition of around 90 developed and developing nations pushed for the final COP30 text to commit to a roadmap for phasing out fossil fuels. Petrostates, including Saudi Arabia and its allies, blocked this, and the final decision made no direct mention of fossil fuels. It did add an oblique reference to the "UAE consensus", the broader COP28 package from Dubai in 2023 that contained the first pledge to transition away from fossil fuels. Cambridge COP expert Dr Joanna Depledge called this a retrograde step, arguing the Dubai fossil fuel language was being "deliberately diluted and obscured, not highlighted."

Blocked from the formal decision, the Brazilian presidency announced the roadmap would proceed outside the UN process instead, merged with a plan backed by Colombia and around 90 other countries, with a follow-up summit set for April 2026. Nikki Reisch of the Center for International Environmental Law said: "Those governments committed to tackling the climate crisis at its source are uniting to move forward outside the UN, under the leadership of Colombia and Pacific Island states, to phase out fossil fuels rapidly, equitably, and in line with 1.5C."

**Update, Santa Marta Conference (April 2026):** The promised summit took place in Santa Marta, Colombia, from 28 to 30 April 2026. Nearly 60 countries agreed to develop national roadmaps to phase out fossil fuel production and use. France published the first national roadmap by a developed country, and Colombia published a draft during the conference. A second conference is planned for Tuvalu in early 2027, co-hosted by Ireland. See the [Santa Marta fossil fuel conference](../solutions/energy-transition/santa-marta-fossil-fuel-conference-2026.md) page for full details.

**Update, Bonn intersessional and COP31 Turkey (June 2026):** At the June 2026 Bonn intersessional, the technical meeting that prepares the COP agenda, the Santa Marta initiative acquired a working name, **Transition Away From Fossil Fuels (TAFF)**, with its future structures negotiated behind closed doors. UN climate chief Simon Stiell pointed to the Iran war energy crisis to argue that fossil fuel dependence now means countries are "importing inflation and economic instability." Outgoing COP30 president Andre Correa do Lago framed the shift as one from "COPs focused on negotiations to COPs focused on implementation." COP31 will be held in Turkey in November 2026. The adaptation finance tripling agreed at Belem is meanwhile stuck on a baseline dispute over whether "triple" means three times the roughly $20 billion 2019 baseline or the roughly $40 billion 2025 baseline. African lead negotiator Antwi Boasiako Amoah warned that Africa cannot fund adaptation through additional debt, and cautioned that TAFF risks fragmenting the multilateral process. Source: [DW, Tim Schauenberg, 18 June 2026](../sources/dw-oil-nations-taff-2026.md).

## Just transition mechanism

Countries agreed a **just transition mechanism (JTM)**, a plan intended to ensure the global move to a green economy is fair and protects the rights of workers, women and Indigenous people. Climate Action Network International, a large coalition of campaign groups, welcomed it as one of the strongest rights-based outcomes in the history of UN climate negotiations. Its executive director, Tasneem Essop, said: "The adoption of JTM was a win shaped by years of pressure from civil society. This outcome didn't fall from the sky: it was carved out through struggle, persistence and the moral clarity of those living on the frontlines of climate breakdown. Governments must now honour this JTM with real action. Anything less is a betrayal." Early efforts to attach dedicated funding to the JTM failed.

## In the news: South Korea's coal pledge

At COP30, South Korea announced it was joining the Powering Past Coal Alliance, a group of around 60 nations and 120 sub-national governments, businesses and organisations committed to phasing out coal, pledging to close all coal-fired power plants by 2040. South Korea has the world's seventh-largest coal power fleet and is the world's fourth-largest thermal coal importer, behind China, India and Japan, accounting for about 8% of global trade in the fuel; coal supplies around 30% of the country's electricity. The decision matters for Australia because South Korea is Australia's third-biggest coal export market, and the Australian government has been urged to prepare for a shift away from thermal coal exports and to accelerate green industries in response.

## Key facts

- COP30 ran from 10 to 21 November 2025 in Belem, Brazil, the first COP held in the Amazon region.
- Current NDCs leave the world on track for roughly 2.3 to 2.5°C of warming.
- Climate finance goal: $1.3 trillion a year by 2035, with developed countries mobilising at least $300 billion a year of that under the NCQG.
- Adaptation funding was tripled at COP30, but the roughly $120 billion a year target was pushed back to 2035, from an initially proposed 2030.
- The final COP30 text contained no direct mention of fossil fuels; a coalition of around 90 countries is pursuing a phase-out roadmap outside the UN process instead.
- Brazil launched the Tropical Forests Forever Facility with a $125 billion target, aiming to raise $10 billion initially from governments and philanthropies.
- South Korea joined the Powering Past Coal Alliance, pledging to close all coal plants by 2040.
