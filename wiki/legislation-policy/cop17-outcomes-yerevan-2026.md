# What COP17 decided: the outcomes of the Yerevan biodiversity talks

## What is it

**COP17**, the UN biodiversity conference, ended in Yerevan, Armenia, in the early hours of 31 October 2026, after a final session of nearly 12 hours. It was the halfway point of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, the 2022 deal that set 23 nature targets for 2030, including protecting 30% of land and sea, restoring 30% of damaged ecosystems and raising $200 billion a year for nature ([Reuters](../sources/cop17-yerevan-pathway-reuters-2026.md)).

Its main job was the first **global review** of progress. A UN report published just before the talks found the world falling behind on 22 of the 23 targets. Countries accepted that verdict: the review "notes with deep concern" that unless action is "rapidly accelerated", the 2030 targets "will not be achieved" ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)). The parts of the review that call for faster action became known as the **Yerevan Pathway**.

This page covers what was decided. The background to the talks, written while they were going on, is on [Halfway to 2030](halfway-to-2030-biodiversity-cop17-overview.md) and [COP17 Biodiversity (Yerevan, 2026)](cop17-biodiversity-yerevan-2026.md).

## Why it matters

The good news is procedural but real: every major text was agreed. Two years earlier, COP16 in Cali ran out of time and had to finish in Rome months later ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)). At a time when international cooperation is under strain, that counts for something.

The harder news is that the money gap is no closer to closing. Rich countries missed their promise to provide at least $20 billion a year for nature by 2025, and environmental funding for 2026-30 is down 27% ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)). Nearly 90% of countries have set national targets, but fewer than half of those assessed are on track ([Reuters](../sources/cop17-yerevan-pathway-reuters-2026.md)). As one charity put it, the world cannot "plod on to 2030 without a course correction".

## Money: who pays for nature

Most of the time in Yerevan went on one question: should emerging economies such as China, Saudi Arabia, Qatar or India join the list of countries expected to pay? The list, set under Article 20 of the 1992 biodiversity convention, was last changed in 2006. Developed countries pushed hard to widen it. Many developing countries saw this as a way for rich countries to avoid their own obligations ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)).

The compromise is gentle. Countries that want to join the donor list can "signal interest" in confidential talks before COP18 and at every second COP after that, and no one can be added without their explicit consent. Developing countries can also give voluntarily without joining the list. Countries agreed that a **permanent financial mechanism** for nature will exist after 2030 under the COP's authority, a long-standing demand from developing countries, but left the decision on whether it will be a new fund until COP18. More than $1.8 billion in new money was announced during the conference ([Reuters](../sources/cop17-yerevan-pathway-reuters-2026.md)).

<!-- tag: ireland, eu -->
Ireland played a visible part. Christopher O'Sullivan, Ireland's nature minister, spoke for the EU delegation and argued that after 20 years the donor list should reflect "new realities", while saying the EU did not want to pull back on its own contributions ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)).

## The Cali Fund and nature's genetic data

The Cali Fund, set up at COP16, asks companies that profit from nature's genetic data (digital sequence information) to pay 0.1% of their revenue or 1% of their profits. By the end of COP17 it had pledges of only $10,565, with less than $9,000 deposited, and no large company in the sectors that use this data had paid. Most of the unresolved details were pushed to talks before COP18. The final decision keeps the suggested rates, sets out how money will be shared, and reserves half of it for Indigenous peoples and local communities. It allows early payments if the fund ever reaches $150m ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)). For the background, see [the Guardian's report on big pharma and the Cali Fund](../sources/cali-fund-big-pharma-freerider-guardian-2026.md).

## Harmful subsidies

Governments spend up to $1.8 trillion a year on subsidies that may harm nature, mostly in fossil fuels and farming. Target 18 aims to cut them by at least $500 billion a year by 2030. The global review keeps a paragraph on this, but softened from "urges" to "encourages", "in accordance with national circumstances", after pushback from Saudi Arabia and Russia. Governments agreed to speed up work towards the target ([Reuters](../sources/cop17-yerevan-pathway-reuters-2026.md)). A new "friends of target 18" group, co-chaired by Colombia and Finland, will help countries share ways to reform these subsidies ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)).

## Oceans, climate and science

Several texts lost language that had already been agreed in Cali. Russia insisted that the marine decision only "take note" of the High Seas Treaty, in force since January 2026, and that mentions of Indigenous peoples, women and youth be removed. The biodiversity and climate text dropped a reference to the International Court of Justice's 2025 opinion on states' climate duties, and to the IPBES assessment on transformative change, though the IPBES "nexus" assessment on biodiversity, water, food and health stayed in. New voluntary guidelines on nature-based solutions were agreed ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)).

## Indigenous peoples, markets and mountains

Countries agreed how the new permanent body for Indigenous peoples, created at COP16, will work. Brazil added a last-minute mention of people of African descent to the global review ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)).

<!-- tag: eu, china -->
On nature markets, the EU launched a "Friends of Credits for Nature and Biodiversity" group, and China announced a pilot nature market in Sichuan. Peru, sceptical that offsets protect anything that would not have been protected anyway, won a study on whether biodiversity credits and offsets add real value ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)).

The Armenian presidency also launched the Yerevan Platform on Mountains and Related Freshwater Ecosystems with UNEP. Mountains supply 55-60% of the world's yearly freshwater flows and support around 1.2 billion people, yet only 19% of mountain areas are protected ([UNEP](../sources/yerevan-platform-mountains-unep-2026.md)).

## Key facts

- COP17 confirmed the world is off track on 22 of the 23 global nature targets for 2030 ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)).
- Rich countries missed the target of at least $20 billion a year in nature finance by 2025 ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)).
- More than $1.8 billion was announced during the conference ([Reuters](../sources/cop17-yerevan-pathway-reuters-2026.md)).
- The Cali Fund ended the talks with $10,565 in pledges ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)).
- The decision on what form a permanent nature fund will take was deferred to COP18 ([Carbon Brief](../sources/cop17-key-outcomes-carbonbrief-2026.md)).
- Only 19% of mountain areas are protected, though they hold 25 of the world's 36 biodiversity hotspots ([UNEP](../sources/yerevan-platform-mountains-unep-2026.md)).
- COP18 will take place in Quito, Ecuador, in 2028 ([Reuters](../sources/cop17-yerevan-pathway-reuters-2026.md)).
