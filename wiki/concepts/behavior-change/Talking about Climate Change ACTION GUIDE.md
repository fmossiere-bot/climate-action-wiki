One thing we all have control over is talking about climate change, and in particular about the climate actions we are taking ourselves. These conversations are not easy, so here are a few guidelines on how to talk about it with family, friends or colleagues. 


> "Never doubt that a small group of thoughtful, committed, citizens can change the world. Indeed, it is the only thing that ever has." - Margaret Mead

## The Need for Conversations

Surveys from Stanford University and the Pew Research Center show clearly that people around the world are worried about the climate but are not talking about it, let alone acting on it. A majority say they are concerned, yet they rarely raise it with their family, friends and colleagues.

Starting those conversations is one of the most useful things you can do. Talking about sustainability and climate action with the people around you, and backing it up with what you do, lays the groundwork for change. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) estimates that shifting what a society sees as normal only needs **10% to 30%** of people to be deeply committed.

Damon Centola's research supports this: once about 25% of a group takes up a new behaviour or belief, the majority is likely to follow. Good conversations can build a circle of support and spark change around you.

# Leading by Example

What you do gives weight to what you say. If you are taking action yourself and setting a good example, your conversations about climate change carry more than words, and people find them far more convincing.

# More Science is Not the Answer

Going into a conversation set on proving scientifically that climate change is real, urgent and deserves action may not work. If someone's identity is closely tied to the opposite view, this approach can make them dig in, and attempts to educate can deepen the divide. What does work is linking climate science to its effects on us and on the things we care about.

# Knowing Your Audience

In her TED talk, [Inspiring Climate Activism Stories](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-BvcToPZCLI), the climate scientist Katharine Hayhoe stresses the value of climate conversations that feel personal. She suggests finding common ground by linking climate change to what people value most, such as family, work or hobbies. That might mean talking about how extreme weather affects the local area, or how climate change is changing outdoor pastimes like gardening or hill walking. Listening and trying to understand, rather than lecturing, makes the issue feel closer to home and draws people in.

# Using the Right Words

How we talk about climate change makes a big difference. Clear, positive language can encourage action and support for policies that tackle the crisis quickly. Rather than dwelling on sacrifice, point to the chance of better health and wellbeing in a world moving beyond fossil fuels. Avoid scientific jargon and misleading terms. "Natural disasters", for example, are often made worse by human activity, so they are not entirely natural. Speaking clearly can change how people see the issue and encourage them to act together.

# Ignore the Outliers

Put your effort into the majority who are open to action, rather than trying to convert the dismissive few. Campaigns often aim at the extremes, but the middle 50% of people, under the bell curve, is where effort makes the biggest difference. Bringing that willing majority on board builds the political will and community support that climate action needs.


## Communicating Solutions

Talking about solutions matters as much as naming the problems. A focus on problems alone can overwhelm people until they switch off. Many professionals argue that climate coverage needs hope, a sense of agency and dignity. Most stories leave out solutions, which leaves audiences feeling powerless. Pointing to positive steps and celebrating small wins can encourage people to act. Balance fear with hope people can act on, and don't overplay sacrifice.

Show the appealing side of climate action, such as the benefits of electric cars, induction cooking and vegetarian food. The falling price of renewable energy, and forecasts that its uptake will grow exponentially, paint a hopeful picture of what is possible. 

Climate tipping points are pushing the crisis forward, but positive social and economic tipping points are also under way, and these are within our control. Encourage people to join grassroots groups and local projects, to show the many ways individuals can help cut emissions.

## Guiding Children Through Climate Conversations

Children are not the main audience of this guide, but conversations with them are some of the most important ones to have. Explaining climate change to children means balancing truthfulness with optimism. 

The BBC's [How to Talk with Children About Climate Change](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ym2uyC6kVIs) sets out some useful approaches. Use language suited to the child's age to describe the problems, and put the weight on solutions that make a difference. Focus on the positive steps towards sustainability and involve children in them, so they come away with a sense of hope and of being able to help.

## Calling out 'Greenwashing'

Greenwashing is when companies falsely claim to be eco-friendly, misleading customers into thinking they are making responsible choices. It runs from product labels to advertising, and it harms the fight against the climate crisis by exaggerating good effects and hiding bad ones. Many shoppers want to reduce their environmental footprint, and greenwashing exploits that wish, which damages trust. Some supposed solutions, such as carbon offsetting, often fail to deliver real benefits: fewer than 5% of offsets actually remove CO2 from the atmosphere. Spotting and calling out greenwashing can make the issue feel relevant and personal to the people you talk to.

## Misinformation

Answer misinformation with clear facts about the benefits of renewable energy and the real progress being made. Keep a list of reliable sources to share after face-to-face conversations. Recent findings by the Center for Countering Digital Hate (CCDH) point to a worrying trend: YouTube is profiting from a surge in climate misinformation. According to the CCDH, 70% of this denial content now aims to discredit climate solutions, using sophisticated tactics to get around YouTube's existing rules, as reported by Fast Company ([YouTube's Profits from Climate Denial Content](https://www.fastcompany.com/91011266/youtube-making-millions-climate-denial-ccdh-says)). It shows the need for stronger measures against the spread of misinformation.

# Checklist for Conversations

### How to make climate conversations count:

1. **Share Your Story**: start with your own connection to climate change.
2. **Link to Priorities:** connect climate change to what matters to the person you are talking to, such as family, the economy or health.
3. **Find Common Ground**: use shared values to bridge differences.
4. **Discuss Immediate Impacts**: show how climate change is affecting us now.
5. **Promote Clean Energy**: talk about the benefits of renewable energy for the economy and for health.
6. **Mention Cost Savings**: point out the money that sustainable choices can save.
7. **Emphasise Urgency**: explain why we need to act quickly.
8. **Share Successes**: use examples of climate action that worked.
9. **Explore Futures:** talk about how what we do today can create a better tomorrow.
