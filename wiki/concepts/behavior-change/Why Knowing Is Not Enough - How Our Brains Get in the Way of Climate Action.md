# Why People Know About Climate Change But Do Not Act

Most people across Europe and beyond accept that climate change is real. Many say it worries them. Yet there is still a wide gap between knowing and doing. Most people understand the problem, but that understanding rarely turns into real changes in how they live.

Part of the reason lies in how the human brain works.

---

### Our Brains Were Not Built for This Kind of Threat

Human beings evolved to respond to immediate, visible dangers. A predator nearby. A rival tribe. A failed harvest. These are threats with a clear cause, a clear consequence, and a short time horizon.

Climate change does not fit that pattern. It moves slowly, its risks are a matter of statistics, and its effects are spread across the whole planet. It asks us to act now for benefits that will be felt decades from now, by people not yet born, in places we may never visit.

Psychologists have identified over 150 cognitive biases that shape human judgment and behaviour. Several of them help explain why we don't act on climate.

---

### Four Biases That Work Against Climate Action

**Hyperbolic discounting**

This is the tendency to value the present more than the future. Offered 100 euros today or 120 euros in a month, most people take the money now. For climate, it means the costs of acting today feel more real than the benefits, which only arrive over years or decades. Our ancestors never had to weigh that kind of trade-off, so our brains are not built for it.

**Limited concern for future generations**

Research in evolutionary psychology suggests that our sense of responsibility tends to extend only a few generations in each direction, from great-grandparents to great-grandchildren. Beyond that, the emotional connection fades. People do care about the future, but distant consequences rarely weigh as much as near ones. At its most severe, climate change threatens generations well beyond that narrow window.

**The bystander effect**

The more people there are in a group, the less likely any one of them is to act. It is a mental shortcut: in a group, we assume someone else will handle it. With climate change, the effect plays out on a global scale. With billions of people involved, the sense of personal responsibility thins out, and most people assume governments, companies or scientists are dealing with it.

**The sunk-cost fallacy**

Once significant time, money, or effort has been invested in a particular path, people find it very hard to change course, even when the evidence suggests they should. This helps explain the stubborn resistance to moving away from fossil fuels. Whole economies, industries and political identities have been built around them. Changing direction feels like admitting failure, even when carrying on has become the bigger risk.

---

### The Problem of Scale

These biases meet a structural problem. Climate change needs people to act together on a scale far beyond anything our social instincts evolved to handle.

Anthropological research on social group sizes, often referred to as Dunbar's Number, suggests that individuals can maintain meaningful relationships with roughly 150 people. Beyond that, trust and coordination become much harder. Climate action, by contrast, needs coordinated behaviour across millions or billions of people, most of whom will never meet.

Collective action is still possible, but the conditions for it have to be designed with care.

---

### What Can Actually Work

**Small groups and local framing**

Things change when people tackle climate issues in small, local groups. Projects that link climate impacts to local concerns, such as farming, tourism, fishing or public health, tend to draw more people in than abstract global messages. People respond to what they can see and relate to.

**Ownership of solutions**

When communities or individuals help shape a solution, rather than simply being told what to do, they tend to value it more and stick with it. This is sometimes called the endowment effect: we invest more in things we feel we helped create.

**Social comparison**

People adjust their behaviour to what they see others doing. Research on energy use has shown that households use less when they can see how they compare with their neighbours. That social signal carries more weight than the environmental argument on its own.

**Positive framing**

How a message is framed matters. People respond better to a picture of a future they would want than to warnings of catastrophe. "A shift to clean energy could prevent tens of thousands of premature deaths" tends to motivate more than "we are heading towards collapse." The facts stay the same. The aim is to present them in a way that opens people up to action, instead of shutting them down with anxiety or helplessness.

---

### A Note on Individual Action and Systemic Change

One point is often left unsaid in discussions like this. Changing individual behaviour matters, but it is not enough on its own. The cognitive biases described above affect everyone, including the people in positions to make systemic decisions. Policies, regulations and economic incentives shape the setting in which each of us makes choices.

Focusing only on individual action risks putting an unfair burden on people who have little power over the systems they live in. The approaches that work best tend to act on both levels at once. They help people act where they can, and push for the wider changes that make sustainable choices the default rather than the exception.
