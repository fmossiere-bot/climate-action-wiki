## What is it

A University of Cambridge study surveyed 5,000 people in Canada, France, Germany, Italy, the UK and the US, asking about their environmental values and their actual ecological footprint. For most people, caring more about the environment goes with a smaller footprint. **Among the wealthiest 30%, the opposite is true.** High earners with the strongest environmental ideals had an even larger footprint than other high earners who did not particularly value sustainability.

The main reason is flying. Well-off environmentalists fly a lot, often to experience other cultures, attend international conferences or keep up with friends abroad. They may try to make up for it by recycling, buying organic food or cutting single-use plastic, but these make almost no difference next to the carbon cost of frequent flights.

## Why it matters

The research challenges a popular idea in climate communication: that if we change people's values, their behaviour will follow. The Cambridge researchers are clear that **"Targeting the environmental attitudes of individuals with campaigns will not do the job" of reducing emissions.** What is needed is change at the level of policy and systems, so that low-carbon options are easy to reach and affordable and high-carbon habits cost more.

The finding also puts the familiar charge that environmentalists are hypocrites in a different light. The people concerned are not acting in bad faith. Real low-carbon alternatives to things like international flights are still mostly unavailable, even for people who badly want them.

## Key facts

> - The study covered 5,000 people in 6 countries: Canada, France, Germany, Italy, UK, US.
> - In the top 30% by socioeconomic status, people who cared about the environment emitted more than other high earners who did not prioritise it.
> - The main reason is frequent flying, one of the most emissions-heavy things an individual can do.
> - The term "carbon footprint" was popularised by BP as a deliberate strategy to shift responsibility from producers to consumers.
> - The UK and Germany have aviation taxes. Airfares rose 24% because of the Iran war energy crisis, but researchers say this is unlikely to put off high-income travellers.
> - France banned short-haul flights in 2023, but loopholes meant no routes were actually cancelled.
> - Greta Thunberg's Fridays for Future movement pushed the German government to adopt climate legislation, even though most protesters carried on flying.

## October 2026 update: the superyacht ratio

Sam Wollaston's Guardian report from the Monaco Yacht Show 2026 (20 October) adds one of the starkest numbers on the emissions of the very rich:

> - **Oxfam's 2024 carbon-inequality study**: 23 superyachts owned by 18 billionaires produce an average of **5,672 tonnes of CO2 per yacht per year**
> - **An average person on Earth would need 860 years** to produce the same amount
> - **The Walton family (Walmart heirs, 3 yachts)**: 56,000 nautical miles in a year and 18,000 tonnes of CO2 between them, the same as 1,700 Walmart employees
> - Larger yachts burn **>500 litres of diesel per hour**
> - **1,093 superyachts over 24 metres are under construction in 2026**, up from 755 in 2016

Grégory Salle (CNRS, author of *Superyachts: Luxury, Tranquility and Ecocide*) calls the pattern "conspicuous seclusion". The yachts are visible, famous and named, yet their owners are out of reach, thanks to registration in low-tax flag states (Cayman, Marshall, Isle of Man, Malta) and ownership through shell companies. There is also a specific physical harm beyond the carbon: anchors are destroying the Mediterranean's **Posidonia seagrass**, an important marine habitat and source of oxygen.

This case takes the paradox further than the research on high-income environmentalists. The super-rich do not need green attitudes to produce huge emissions, and the fleet is growing either way. See [superyachts ecocide (source)](../../sources/superyachts-ecocide-guardian-2026.md).
