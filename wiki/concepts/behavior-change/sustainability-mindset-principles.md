# The Sustainability Mindset Principles (Isabel Rimanoczy)

> **Editorial summary:** Original research by Fabien Mossière summarising Isabel Rimanoczy's *The Sustainability Mindset Principles* (Routledge). Rimanoczy identifies **12 thinking habits** across four areas — Ecological Worldview, Systems Thinking, Emotional Intelligence, Spiritual Intelligence — that help people act for the planet and keep acting over time. Her argument: information about the climate is not enough; lasting change comes from slowing down, feeling something about what we learn, and taking small real actions. The write-up also compares the framework to the **LOHAS** (Lifestyles of Health and Sustainability) consumer movement, and notes that Rimanoczy's five most-essential principles are My Contribution, Reflection, Self-awareness, Purpose and Mindfulness. **Note for Fabien:** the raw file closes with three checks the AI collaborator flagged before publication (Principle 7 wording, research citations, and adding a "Limits and criticism" section) — those are preserved verbatim below and worth reading before you promote the page beyond a draft.

The Sustainability Mindset Principles are a set of 12 principles developed by the researcher and educator Isabel Rimanoczy. They describe the ways of thinking and being that help a person act for the good of people and the planet, and keep acting over time. The principles are set out in her book _The Sustainability Mindset Principles_, written mainly for educators, coaches and trainers.

### What is a sustainability mindset?

Most sustainability education looks at the outside world: problems, rules, technologies and behaviours. A mindset sits on the inside. It is made of our values, our beliefs, our sense of purpose and the assumptions we share with the people around us. A mindset cannot be seen directly, but it shows in what we do.

In simple words, a sustainability mindset is a way of thinking and being that comes from four steps:

1. Seeing the bigger picture of the ecosystem we are part of.
2. Feeling something about it.
3. Looking inside at our own values and habits.
4. Acting for the good of the whole.

The principles grew out of Rimanoczy's research with business leaders who had championed sustainability in their organisations. She found that they went through similar steps. They understood the wider picture, they were moved by it, and then they asked themselves how they were part of the problem. The gap between their values and their actions pushed them to do something.

### Why the principles matter

**Information is not enough.** Knowledge about climate change and nature loss keeps growing, but action does not grow at the same speed. The book argues that the missing piece is inner work, not more facts.

**Feelings are the fuel.** Facts alone create weak commitment. People stop acting when it no longer saves money or "makes sense". When a fact connects to a person's own life and values, the motivation is stronger and lasts longer.

**It gives power back.** Common reactions to bad news are to look away, to doubt the science, or to blame governments and companies. All three leave the person powerless. Asking "how am I contributing to this?" is uncomfortable, but our own choices are what we can control. The book is careful here: research is divided on guilt as a motivator, and hope, pride and a sense of agency are often more effective.

**It connects personal wellbeing and the health of the planet.** Many of the habits that harm the planet also harm us: rushing, living on autopilot, buying things to feel better, feeling cut off from other people and from nature. The book reports studies that link connection with nature to greater happiness and to greener behaviour, and link mindfulness practice to lower stress and more care for others. In this view, looking after ourselves and looking after the planet support each other.

### The 12 principles

The principles are grouped into four areas.

|Area|Principle|In one line|
|---|---|---|
|Ecological Worldview|1. Ecoliteracy|Understanding the state of the planet, how the problems are linked, and what it means for me|
||2. My Contribution|Seeing how I am part of the problems without meaning to be, which opens the chance to act|
|Systems Thinking|3. Long-term Thinking|Every action has effects we cannot see straight away, so we consider them when we decide|
||4. Both+and Thinking|Many situations are not "this or that", and solutions can include everyone affected|
||5. Flow in Cycles|Nothing in nature moves in a straight line, and there is no waste in nature|
||6. Interconnectedness|Being different and being connected go together, in nature and in society|
|Emotional Intelligence|7. Creative Innovation|Resilience comes from creativity, experimenting and trusting our intuition|
||8. Reflection|Pausing to think about a situation before jumping into action|
||9. Self-awareness|Looking at our own values and beliefs gives us more freedom to choose how we act|
|Spiritual Intelligence|10. Purpose|A sense of purpose works as an inner compass|
||11. Oneness with Nature|We are part of nature, one species among many|
||12. Mindfulness|Being fully present grows awareness and compassion|

"Spiritual" is used here in a practical sense, separate from religion. It means feeling connected to something bigger, and the wish to contribute that follows from it.

The order is deliberate. It moves from the concrete (facts about the planet) to the personal (purpose, presence), so that trust builds along the way.

### How to build the mindset over time

The book's closing message is that the principles are thinking habits to grow, not content to learn. Habits form through experience and repetition. The main practices are:

- **Experience first, facts after.** A real experience, such as time in nature or meeting someone affected by a problem, does more than data. Data supports the experience, not the other way around.
- **Slow down.** Pace is the main obstacle. A busy life runs on automatic habits, and automatic habits often have effects we would never choose on purpose. Short pauses, repeated often, work better than one long session.
- **Stop, reflect, write, share.** A few minutes of silence and writing before speaking. A useful set of questions: What happened? How did I contribute? Do I feel the need to do something? What will I do?
- **Ask the contribution question gently.** "How am I part of this?" works best about something the person cares about, and when it is balanced with "what am I already doing that I am proud of?"
- **Look at influence, not only control.** What we control is small. How far our influence reaches is usually bigger than we think.
- **Take small, real actions.** One real action, however modest, teaches a person that they can make a difference, and the satisfaction makes them want to repeat it. Purpose is better treated as a small daily question ("what difference can I make today, here?") than as a grand life mission.
- **Spend regular time in nature.** Alone, unhurried, without a phone. One experience plants a seed. Repeating and varying it is what changes the relationship.
- **Try several practices and keep one.** Meditation is only one route. Body practices such as yoga, walking, gardening, art and seasonal rituals all count.
- **Do it with others.** Sharing in pairs or a circle, with no convincing and no right or wrong answers. Values change slowly, at each person's own pace. The aim is to widen awareness, not to change what people hold dear.

For those who cannot work with all 12, Rimanoczy names five that should not be left out: My Contribution, Reflection, Self-awareness, Purpose and Mindfulness.


### LOHAS and the Sustainability Mindset Principles

**What it is.** LOHAS stands for Lifestyles of Health and Sustainability. It started as a marketing term in the United States, used to describe a group of consumers with shared ideas and buying habits. These are people who look for products and services that are better for the environment, socially responsible, and healthier, both for the individual and for the planet. Typical LOHAS areas are organic food, natural health, green energy, eco-tourism and ethical investing. LOHAS consumers are described as people who care about health and the environment and act on it, follow social issues, and have an interest in personal development.

**When it emerged.** The term was coined in the late 1990s by the trade publisher Natural Business Communications, to name a fast-growing market for healthy and sustainable products. The idea is close to the "cultural creatives", a term from the 2000 book of the same name by Paul Ray and Sherry Anderson. It then spread outside the United States. In Japan, for example, it first appeared in the media in 2002. By 2008 the US LOHAS market was estimated at about 41 million people. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-030-02006-4_322-1)

**How it links to the principles.** Both start from the same idea: what is good for me and what is good for the planet belong together. This is the logic of Principle 4 (Both+and Thinking), and it runs through Principles 6, 11 and 12, which connect personal wellbeing with relationships, nature and presence.

The difference is where each one looks. LOHAS looks at the outside: what people buy and how they live. The Sustainability Mindset Principles look at the inside: the values, beliefs and habits behind those choices. In that sense the principles can be read as the inner side of a LOHAS way of living. They explain why some people keep making healthy and sustainable choices even when it costs more or takes more effort.

The principles also bring a useful challenge to LOHAS. Principle 5 (Flow in Cycles) asks "how much is enough?" and questions buying things to build our identity. A lifestyle built only on buying better products stays inside the same take-make-waste system. The mindset work adds the missing questions: do I need this, and what do my choices say about my values?
