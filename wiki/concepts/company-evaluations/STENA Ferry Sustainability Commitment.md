## The industry backdrop: IMO targets

Shipping as a whole has set itself demanding targets for cutting emissions. In 2023 the International Maritime Organization (IMO), the UN body that regulates shipping, adopted a revised strategy for reducing greenhouse gas emissions from ships. Its main elements are:

| Commitment | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Carbon intensity reduction** | At least 40% less CO2 per unit of transport work (emissions per unit of cargo multiplied by distance) by 2030, compared with a 2008 baseline. |
| **Uptake of zero or near-zero GHG fuels and technologies** | By 2030, these should make up at least 5%, aiming for 10%, of the energy used in international shipping. |
| **Indicative checkpoints for total annual GHG reductions** | By 2030: cut total annual GHG emissions by at least 20%, aiming for 30% (compared with 2008). By 2040: at least 70%, aiming for 80%. |
| **Net-zero by around 2050** | International shipping emissions should peak as soon as possible, then fall to net zero "by or around" 2050. The wording leaves some room for manoeuvre and recognises that countries have different capacities. |

The industry is trying a wide range of ideas in response, from sailing cargo ships to kite-assisted propulsion, batteries and carbon capture. It is an interesting space to watch.

## Stena Line's stated goals

Stena Line says its aim is zero fossil fuel emissions. To get there it is developing e-methanol and battery-powered vessels, investing in renewable energy for shore power (plugging ships into the grid while in port), and making its fleet more efficient. Its main projects include Stena Futuro, a concept vessel designed to use 20% less energy, and hybrid ferries that can run on methanol and batteries.

**Environmental initiatives:**

- **Fuel transition:** investing in methanol, including recycled methanol, and in hybrid vessels that can also run on battery power.
- **New vessel concepts:** developing next-generation ships, such as the Stena Futuro, that use less energy and emit less.
- **Renewable energy:** using 100% renewable electricity on shore and to power vessels in port through shore power.
- **Fleet efficiency:** using the fleet more fully and more efficiently to shrink its overall carbon footprint.
- **Reducing ocean harm:** using gentle, eco-labelled detergents and testing new anti-fouling techniques (ways to stop marine life growing on hulls).

**Broader sustainability efforts:**

- **Waste reduction:** fewer plastic disposables, better recycling rates and less food waste.
- **Human rights and workforce:** aiming for a diverse, healthy and gender-equal workforce, and committing to respect human rights throughout the supply chain.
- **Supplier responsibility:** developing new sustainability standards for suppliers.
- **Certifications:** the company holds ISO14001 certification and takes part in the Green Marine certification programme.

Stena Line's long-term goal is to run with zero fossil fuel emissions and to contribute to more sustainable transport, while offering integrated transport that links ferries with road and rail.

## What the website says

Stena Line's website stays fairly vague. It sets out broad goals: no negative impact on the climate, no harm to the oceans, full responsibility across its supply chain and CSR, a safe, healthy, diverse and gender-equal workforce, and responsible offers to customers. One blog post gives a clearer target: cutting total CO2 emissions from its vessels by 30% by 2030, as a step towards net zero.

The company also publishes a very long list of actions. The list is fairly disorganised, but the actions look real, even though most come without specific figures. They include using AI to cut fuel use, reducing single-use plastic such as water bottles, switching to more sustainable coffee, showing the CO2 equivalent of dishes on menus, and serving sustainable seafood certified by the Marine Stewardship Council (MSC).

## Reported progress

As of May 2024, Stena Line reported an 11% reduction in carbon emissions per tonne of cargo carried.

## What external research says

A 2021 academic paper on how shipping is responding to environmental challenges called Stena Line "a paradigm for shipping". The reason was its energy-saving programme, which has received more than $4.5 million a year and put in place more than 300 initiatives with the sole aim of burning less bunker fuel (heavy ship fuel) and eliminating emissions. The programme includes propeller alternation, solar panels, LED lighting, route scheduling, and eco-driving training for captains and crew.

## Sustainability reporting

Stena's published sustainability reporting is fairly thin. Its 2023 Annual Review gives relatively little detail, and its fuller sustainability report is hard to find on the main company website.

## Genuine action or communication chaos?

Travelling by ferry from Ireland to France, as I do most summers, I usually take Brittany Ferries but used Stena Line on the return leg this year. The contrast in sustainability communication between the two was striking. Where Brittany Ferries seems to have a clear north star, with consistent values and commitments, Stena Line felt scattered: CO2 equivalents listed on the food menu, while recycling bins were hard to find in some spaces; a stated commitment to sustainable fishing displayed next to a shop full of plastic bottles; sustainable chocolate sold next to conventional chocolate from other brands. To a critical eye, this could look like it borders on greenwashing. Having looked into it further, my conclusion is that it probably is not, though I remain somewhat conflicted about the communication itself.

**Concerns:**

1. **The "everything, everywhere, all at once" approach.** When a company lists everything, it becomes hard to tell what actually matters. Are readers meant to be equally impressed by sustainable coffee and methanol-powered ferries?
2. **Questionable impact of some initiatives.** Take the CO2 labels on canteen food: a nice gesture, but comparable schemes, such as calorie labelling at McDonald's, are not known to reliably change behaviour. It is unclear whether this is meaningful action or mostly performative.
3. **Verification challenges.** Some claims are hard to verify independently. The sustainable fishing claims are backed by MSC certification, which is reassuring, but clear numbers and outcomes are otherwise difficult to find.
4. **Information accessibility.** Finding Stena Line's actual sustainability reports is unnecessarily difficult: its annual review is sparse, and its detailed sustainability report is not prominently published.

**On the greenwashing question:** the external validation, the reported figures, and the scale of investment all point to genuine effort, so this does not read as greenwashing in the deliberate sense. But the scattershot communication approach makes it look more like greenwashing than the substance warrants. Overwhelming an audience with dozens of minor initiatives alongside major technological shifts, without a clear hierarchy or narrative, creates suspicion rather than trust, even when the underlying work is real.

**Open questions:** Does listing every initiative, no matter how small, help or hurt a company's credibility? What would better communication look like, perhaps a clearer priority ranking that foregrounds the big-impact initiatives? Is this level of granular detail standard across the maritime and logistics sector, or is Stena Line's approach unusually fragmented?
