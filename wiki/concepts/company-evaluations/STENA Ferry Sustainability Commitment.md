## The industry backdrop: IMO targets

The maritime industry as a whole is pursuing ambitious targets to cut its emissions. In 2023 the International Maritime Organization (IMO) adopted a revised strategy on reducing greenhouse gas emissions from ships. Its key elements are:

| Commitment | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Carbon intensity reduction** | At least 40% CO2 emissions per transport work (emissions per unit of cargo multiplied by distance) by 2030, relative to a 2008 baseline. |
| **Uptake of zero or near-zero GHG fuels and technologies** | By 2030, these should account for at least 5%, striving for 10%, of energy used in international shipping. |
| **Indicative checkpoints for total annual GHG reductions** | By 2030: reduce total annual GHG emissions by at least 20%, striving for 30% (relative to 2008). By 2040: at least 70%, striving for 80%. |
| **Net-zero by around 2050** | International shipping emissions should peak as soon as possible, then decline to net zero "by or around" 2050, with wording that leaves some flexibility and recognises differing national capacities. |

The industry is responding with a wide range of innovation, from sailing cargo ships to kite-assisted propulsion, batteries and carbon capture, an interesting space to watch as it evolves.

## Stena Line's stated goals

Stena Line says it is focused on achieving zero fossil fuel emissions by developing e-methanol and battery-powered vessels, investing in renewable energy for shore power, and improving fleet efficiency to reduce overall carbon emissions. Key initiatives include Stena Futuro, a concept vessel designed for 20% lower energy use, and hybrid ferries capable of running on methanol and batteries.

**Environmental initiatives:**

- **Fuel transition:** investing in methanol, including recycled methanol, and hybrid vessels that can also run on battery power.
- **New vessel concepts:** developing next-generation ships, such as the Stena Futuro, to reduce energy consumption and emissions.
- **Renewable energy:** using 100% renewable electricity for onshore operations and for powering vessels in port through shore power.
- **Fleet efficiency:** increasing the utilisation and efficiency of the fleet to reduce its overall carbon footprint.
- **Reducing ocean harm:** using gentle, eco-labelled detergents and exploring new anti-fouling techniques.

**Broader sustainability efforts:**

- **Waste reduction:** reducing plastic disposables, improving recycling rates, and cutting food waste.
- **Human rights and workforce:** aiming for a diverse, healthy and gender-equal workforce, and committing to upholding human rights throughout the supply chain.
- **Supplier responsibility:** developing new sustainability standards for suppliers.
- **Certifications:** the company holds ISO14001 certification and participates in the Green Marine certification programme.

Overall, Stena Line's long-term goal is to operate with zero fossil fuel emissions and contribute to more sustainable transport, alongside a focus on offering integrated, multimodal transport solutions.

## What the website says

Stena Line's website remains fairly vague, setting out high-level goals: no negative impact on the climate, no harm to the oceans, full responsibility across its supply chain and CSR, a safe, healthy, diverse and gender-equal workforce, and responsible offers to customers. One blog post states a clearer target: to reduce total CO2 emissions from its vessels by 30% by 2030, as part of its roadmap to a net-zero future.

The company also publishes a very long list of actions being taken. The presentation is fairly disorganised, but the actions themselves appear genuine, even though most are not backed by specific figures. They include using AI to reduce fuel consumption, reducing single-use plastic such as water bottles, switching to more sustainable coffee, listing the CO2 equivalent of food items on menus, and sourcing sustainable seafood certified by the Marine Stewardship Council (MSC).

## Reported progress

Stena Line has reported an 11% reduction in carbon emissions per tonne of cargo carried, as of May 2024.

## What external research says

A 2021 academic paper looking at the maritime industry's response to environmental challenges described Stena Line as "a paradigm for shipping" because of its energy-saving programme, under which more than $4.5 million per year has been invested, with more than 300 initiatives implemented with the sole aim of reducing bunker fuel consumption and eliminating emissions. The programme includes propeller alternation, solar panels, LED technology, route scheduling, and eco-driving through captain and crew training.

## Sustainability reporting

Stena's published sustainability reporting is fairly thin. Its 2023 Annual Review offers relatively little detail, and its more detailed sustainability report is not easy to find on the main company website.

## Genuine action or communication chaos?

Travelling by ferry from Ireland to France, as I do most summers, I usually take Brittany Ferries but used Stena Line on the return leg this year. The contrast in sustainability communication between the two was striking. Where Brittany Ferries seems to have a clear north star, with consistent values and commitments, Stena Line felt scattered: CO2 equivalents listed on the food menu, while recycling bins were hard to find in some spaces; a stated commitment to sustainable fishing displayed next to a shop full of plastic bottles; sustainable chocolate sold next to conventional chocolate from other brands. To a critical eye, this could look like it borders on greenwashing. Having looked into it further, my conclusion is that it probably is not, though I remain somewhat conflicted about the communication itself.

**Concerns:**

1. **The "everything, everywhere, all at once" approach.** When a company lists everything, it becomes hard to tell what actually matters. Are readers meant to be equally impressed by sustainable coffee and methanol-powered ferries?
2. **Questionable impact of some initiatives.** Take the CO2 labels on canteen food: a nice gesture, but comparable schemes, such as calorie labelling at McDonald's, are not known to reliably change behaviour. It is unclear whether this is meaningful action or mostly performative.
3. **Verification challenges.** Some claims are hard to verify independently. The sustainable fishing claims are backed by MSC certification, which is reassuring, but clear numbers and outcomes are otherwise difficult to find.
4. **Information accessibility.** Finding Stena Line's actual sustainability reports is unnecessarily difficult: its annual review is sparse, and its detailed sustainability report is not prominently published.

**On the greenwashing question:** the external validation, the reported figures, and the scale of investment all point to genuine effort, so this does not read as greenwashing in the deliberate sense. But the scattershot communication approach makes it look more like greenwashing than the substance warrants. Overwhelming an audience with dozens of minor initiatives alongside major technological shifts, without a clear hierarchy or narrative, creates suspicion rather than trust, even when the underlying work is real.

**Open questions:** Does listing every initiative, no matter how small, help or hurt a company's credibility? What would better communication look like, perhaps a clearer priority ranking that foregrounds the big-impact initiatives? Is this level of granular detail standard across the maritime and logistics sector, or is Stena Line's approach unusually fragmented?
