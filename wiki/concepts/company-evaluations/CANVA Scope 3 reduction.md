###### The global design platform is using partnerships and clean power projects to drive emissions reductions and set an example for other industries

> Canva's Scope 3 emissions are largely from the electricity used by its print suppliers. Canva brings these suppliers into a Virtual Power Purchase Agreement (VPPA) so they co-fund new solar projects, and the suppliers receive Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs) from those new projects. This reduces the suppliers' Scope 2 emissions, which in turn reduces Canva's Scope 3.

## About Canva

Canva is an online design platform whose stated mission is to let everyone in the world design anything and publish it anywhere. It has 190 million monthly users. They can also use the company's global network of print partners to turn their designs into physical products, from posters and invitations to tote bags and mugs.

## Where its Scope 3 emissions come from

Canva describes sustainability as a core part of its mission and works with Watershed, a carbon accounting company, to measure and reduce its emissions. It is tackling Scope 3 (the emissions in its supply chain) through a fixed-price VPPA designed by Watershed. The agreement funds a series of new solar power projects, paid for jointly with its print suppliers, to shrink the supply chain's footprint.

For Canva, the largest share of Scope 3 (Category 1, purchased goods and services, under the GHG Protocol) comes from the energy its print suppliers use around the world. When someone orders printed items through Canva, the printing happens at hundreds of other companies' sites worldwide. Those sites use electricity and fuel, and the emissions from that count as part of Canva's upstream Scope 3 emissions.

## What Canva is doing with Watershed

Rather than simply buying RECs for its suppliers, Canva is co-funding new solar projects together with them. This is a supply-chain VPPA: Canva pays part of the cost and its print suppliers pay the rest. The VPPA gives the project developer a guaranteed income over many years, and that income is helping to build new community solar farms in Illinois. This comes much closer to **additionality** (paying for clean power that would not otherwise exist) than simply buying RECs.

Suppliers receive enough RECs from the VPPA to cover their own electricity use. That lets them report market-based Scope 2 emissions (from the electricity they buy) of zero or close to it. Because its suppliers' Scope 2 emissions are lower, Canva can in turn report lower Scope 3 emissions. The chain runs like this: new solar farms produce clean electricity and RECs, the RECs let suppliers cut their Scope 2 emissions, and that lets Canva cut its Scope 3.

**Results reported by Canva and Watershed:**

- Based on today's grid, the solar projects are expected to avoid over 15,000 tonnes of CO2 a year. That is equivalent to around 38 million car miles, and roughly a 16% cut in Canva's 2022 footprint.
- The programme also aims to benefit the communities where the solar farms are built: local jobs, lower electricity bills and access to clean power, using the electricity that print partners don't use.
- By helping its print supply chain cut emissions, Canva hopes to show an approach that other industries can copy.

## Does it have any recognised certification?

None that we could find. Schemes like the SBTi (the Science Based Targets initiative) generally require clear reduction targets for Scope 1, 2 and 3 that do not rely on RECs or carbon offsetting, and RECs are the mechanism Canva is using. Even so, its RECs appear to bring real additionality and help its suppliers switch to renewable energy, so the approach can fairly be called effective. Overall, the emissions linked to this initiative are falling.

## Why this is more credible than typical Scope 3 claims

Many companies try to lower Scope 3 emissions by asking suppliers to buy RECs themselves, which often adds no new clean power, or by pushing suppliers to cut emissions without paying for anything. Canva goes further by financing new solar capacity together with its suppliers. This fits TCFD expectations for real decarbonisation of the value chain, with CDSB and ISSB guidance on material climate impacts, and with best practice for Scope 3 supplier engagement. It is one of the more solid ways of cutting supply-chain emissions in use today.

## What is a VPPA?

A Virtual Power Purchase Agreement is a financial contract between a company and the developer of a renewable energy project. Unlike a physical power purchase agreement, the company does not receive any electricity. The project sells its power on the wholesale market at the going price, and the company agrees to pay a fixed price for each megawatt-hour (MWh) that a specific wind or solar project produces. In effect it is a financial hedge rather than a delivery of power.

For each MWh produced, the company receives the matching RECs. Companies use these to claim market-based Scope 2 reductions. Using RECs to lower Scope 3 is more contested. The GHG Protocol's Scope 3 Standard lets a company use its suppliers' own emissions data, so suppliers' lower market-based Scope 2 can feed into the buyer's Scope 3, as in Canva's case. How far market-based instruments should count in Scope 3 is not settled, and it is part of the GHG Protocol's current revision of its standards. Companies sign VPPAs for several reasons: to protect themselves against swings in electricity prices over the long term, to support new renewable capacity when the deal is well designed, and to be able to say they helped get a project built.

## Are VPPAs guaranteed to finance new renewable projects?

No. A VPPA does not automatically add new renewable power. It can, but only if it is set up carefully. It adds new capacity only if it is signed before the project is financed or built, and only if the guaranteed income from the VPPA makes a real difference to whether the project gets built. If a VPPA is signed with a project that is already running, or one that had already secured its financing, it adds no new renewable capacity. The buyer is then essentially buying RECs from an existing asset.

Market standards reflect this caution. The Greenhouse Gas Protocol (GHGP) does not count VPPAs with existing projects as additional. The SBTi puts real emission cuts ahead of market-based claims. CDP and investors look for evidence that a VPPA supported a truly new project, sometimes described as "new build" RECs.
