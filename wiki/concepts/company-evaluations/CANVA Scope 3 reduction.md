###### The global design platform is using partnerships and clean power projects to drive emissions reductions and set an example for other industries

> Canva's Scope 3 emissions are largely from the electricity used by its print suppliers. Canva brings these suppliers into a Virtual Power Purchase Agreement (VPPA) so they co-fund new solar projects, and the suppliers receive Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs) from those new projects. This reduces the suppliers' Scope 2 emissions, which in turn reduces Canva's Scope 3.

## About Canva

Canva is an online visual communication platform with a mission to empower everyone in the world to design anything and publish anywhere. It has 190 million monthly users, who can also draw on the company's global network of print supply partners to turn their designs into physical products, everything from posters and invitations to tote bags and mugs.

## Where its Scope 3 emissions come from

Canva has made sustainability a core part of its mission and partnered with Watershed to measure and reduce its carbon emissions. It is addressing Scope 3 through a Watershed-developed, innovative fixed-price VPPA, which reduces its supply chain's environmental footprint through a series of new solar power projects funded in partnership with its print suppliers.

For Canva, the largest portion of Scope 3 (Category 1 or Category 11, depending on the accounting choice) comes from the energy used by its global print suppliers. When someone orders printed materials through Canva, the printing happens at hundreds of third-party facilities worldwide. These facilities consume electricity and fuel, and that consumption becomes part of Canva's Scope 3 upstream emissions.

## What Canva is doing with Watershed

Canva is not just buying RECs on behalf of its suppliers. Instead, it is doing something more material: co-funding new solar projects together with its print suppliers. This is a supply-chain VPPA, meaning Canva pays part of the VPPA cost and its print suppliers pay the rest. The VPPA gives the project developer long-term revenue certainty, and this revenue is helping to build new community solar farms in Illinois. This approach is much closer to true additionality than simple REC purchases.

Suppliers receive RECs from the VPPA, enough to cover their own electricity use. This lets suppliers claim market-based Scope 2 emissions of zero, or close to it, and in turn lets Canva claim reduced Scope 3 emissions, because its suppliers' Scope 2 emissions are now lower. The causal chain runs: new solar farms lead to clean electricity and RECs, which let suppliers reduce their Scope 2 emissions, which lets Canva reduce its Scope 3.

**Results reported by Canva and Watershed:**

- Based on today's grid, the solar initiative is expected to avoid over 15,000 tonnes of CO2 annually, equivalent to around 38 million car miles avoided, and an approximate 16% reduction of Canva's 2022 footprint.
- The programme aims for wider positive impact beyond emissions: local job creation, lower electricity bills, and access to clean power in the communities where the solar farms are built, using the excess generation not consumed by print supply partners.
- By helping its print supply chain decarbonise, Canva is aiming to demonstrate an approach other industries can copy.

## Does it have any recognised certification?

Not that is apparent. In general, certifications like SBTi (the Science Based Targets initiative) require clear reduction targets for Scope 1, 2 and 3 that do not rely on RECs or carbon offsetting, which is the mechanism Canva is using. That said, because its RECs appear to bring genuine additionality and help its suppliers move to renewable energy, the approach can reasonably be credited as effective. Overall, the emissions associated with this initiative are going down.

## Why this is more credible than typical Scope 3 claims

Many companies try to lower Scope 3 emissions by asking suppliers to buy RECs themselves, which is often non-additional, or by pressuring suppliers to reduce emissions without funding anything. Canva is doing something stronger: financing the actual build-out of new solar capacity in partnership with its suppliers. This aligns with TCFD expectations for real decarbonisation of the value chain, with CDSB and ISSB guidance on material climate impacts, and with best practice for Scope 3 supplier engagement under the SBTi's forest, land and agriculture (FLAG) and supply chain enablement principles. It is one of the more robust forms of supply-chain decarbonisation currently in use.

## What is a VPPA?

A Virtual Power Purchase Agreement is a financial contract between a company and a renewable energy project developer. Unlike a physical power purchase agreement, the company does not receive electricity directly: the project sells its electricity on the wholesale market at the market price, while the company agrees to pay a fixed price for each megawatt-hour (MWh) generated by a specific wind or solar project. This makes it a financial hedge, not a physical power delivery.

For each MWh generated, the company receives the associated RECs. These are used to claim market-based Scope 2 reductions, and are sometimes positioned, controversially, as influencing Scope 3 when tied to supplier enablement programmes, though this is usually not accepted by major accounting standards. Companies enter VPPAs for several reasons: as a long-term hedge against electricity price volatility, as a mechanism to support new renewable capacity when well designed, and for the ability to claim that the company helped catalyse a project's development.

## Are VPPAs guaranteed to finance new renewable projects?

Short answer: no. VPPAs are not automatically additional. They can provide additionality, but only when structured carefully. A VPPA provides additionality only if it is signed before the project is financed or built, and if the revenue certainty from the VPPA is material to enabling the project's construction. If a VPPA is signed with an already-operational project, or with a project that had already secured financing without the VPPA, it does not create additional renewable capacity. In that case, the buyer is essentially purchasing RECs linked to an existing asset.

Market standards reflect this scepticism: the Greenhouse Gas Protocol (GHGP) does not treat VPPAs with existing projects as additional, the SBTi emphasises real emission reductions over market-based claims, and CDP and investor expectations look for evidence that a VPPA supported a genuinely new project, sometimes described as "new build" RECs.
