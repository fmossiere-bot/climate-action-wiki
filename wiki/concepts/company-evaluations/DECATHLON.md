## About Decathlon

Decathlon is the third-largest maker and seller of sports goods in the world. Most of its climate impact sits before a product reaches the shop: 60.7% of its emissions come from extracting materials and making products.

**Rental trial in Belgium:** for the past four years Decathlon has tested a rental model in Belgium, priced between 25 and 95 euro a month. It seems to work for families and for the company, which says it becomes 5-10 times more profitable after four to five years. If one person rents 35 items a year, the estimated footprint is 191kg of CO2, and the company expects that to fall to 83kg by 2033. Buying the same items new today would come to around 2,100kg.

**Ecodesign products** now make up 38% of sales. In 2023, 38% of everything Decathlon sold was ecodesigned, with a carbon footprint up to 25% lower than the conventional version. The company reports that its absolute emissions fell by approximately 11.7% in 2023 compared with its 2021 baseline.

## Company commitments

### Decarbonising company operations

For Decathlon, Scope 1 and 2 emissions are largely about energy and material extraction. The company says it wants to cut emissions across its whole value chain, which goes wider than Scope 1 and 2. It has also dropped an earlier "economic intensity" target in favour of firm, absolute CO2 targets, and it applies the same approach to its Scope 3 emissions.

**Decathlon's targets:**

- **Overall net-zero target:** Decathlon has committed to reach net zero by 2050. Under the SBTi's definition, that means cutting absolute emissions by 90% and neutralising what is left, as a contribution to global carbon neutrality.
- **Near-term targets:** cut absolute Scope 1 and 2 GHG emissions by 42% by 2030, from a 2021 base year, and cut absolute Scope 3 GHG emissions by 42% over the same period.
- **Long-term targets:** cut absolute Scope 1 and 2 GHG emissions by 90% by 2050, from a 2021 base year, and cut absolute Scope 3 GHG emissions by 90% over the same period.

Decathlon's old commitment was about economic intensity: a 53% reduction in CO2 equivalent per euro of revenue by 2026, compared with 2016. Switching to absolute CO2 equivalent emissions is a real change. A company can lower its carbon intensity while still emitting more greenhouse gas overall, simply by growing, so an absolute target closes that gap. Decathlon is fairly open about its targets, the choices behind them and its timeline, and it describes them in a matter-of-fact way rather than overselling them.

### Scope 1, 2 and 3, explained

When a company measures its greenhouse gases, it looks at emissions from its own activity and from other businesses it relies on. These boundaries are called scopes, and there are three:

- **Scope 1:** direct emissions, for example gas combustion in store boilers.
- **Scope 2:** indirect emissions from purchased energy consumption.
- **Scope 3:** other indirect emissions across the value chain, including raw material extraction, product manufacturing, product transport, and employee commuting.

### The four levers for reducing its carbon footprint

Decathlon's plan to cut greenhouse gases rests on four levers: decarbonising its own footprint, improving its product range and designing products that last longer, growing circular business models, and influencing the wider system through advocacy and mobilisation.

**Raw materials:** extracting raw materials and making products account for 60.7% of Decathlon's carbon footprint, so this is where it focuses first. It invests in more sustainable raw materials, especially for metal, textiles and footwear, where the potential CO2 savings are high. It designs products to use fewer raw materials, prefers lower-emission materials such as wood over metal, raises recycled content as far as it can without hurting quality, and favours materials that can be recycled at end of life.

**Production energy:** helping suppliers use energy more efficiently, designing products that need fewer processing stages, using less energy-hungry processes such as new dyeing methods, and moving to a cleaner energy mix by ending coal use and adding renewables.

**Product transport:** organising upstream logistics so each product travels fewer kilometres, asking international carriers to use lower-carbon fuels, and working with delivery partners on lower-emission options.

**Decathlon's own sites:** using renewable energy, saving energy, and refurbishing existing buildings for new shops rather than building from scratch.

**New business models:** designing products that are durable, repairable and recyclable enough for rental and second life, and building second life, rental and repair services around them.

**Influencing the wider system:** training staff on environmental issues, encouraging walking and cycling among customers and staff, nudging customers towards more considered buying, and joining international initiatives such as World Clean Up Day events at Decathlon sites worldwide.

Most of the energy used in factories worldwide still comes from fossil fuels (coal, gas and oil). That is why Decathlon decided in 2017 to help its suppliers switch to cleaner energy. The scale of the issue has not changed much: in 2024, 60.7% of the company's total CO2 emissions still came from raw material extraction and the manufacturing of its sports goods.

## Towards a more circular model

Making products last longer, making them easier to repair and recycle, and encouraging second life and rental all move the company towards a more circular business model.

**Durability:** since 2020 Decathlon has developed its own methods and tests, and durability criteria are now written into the specifications of ecodesigned products. Engineers use a reference framework and durability standards based on these tests to judge how long a product will last and to choose tougher parts and materials. This year, frameworks were finished for running shoes, sailing shoes, everyday shoes, dance shoes, football shirts, jackets and coats, goalkeeper gloves, socks, yoga mats, knee pads, life jackets and suitcases.

**Repairability:** guided by the repairability index developed in France by ADEME (the French environment and energy management agency), Decathlon has set criteria for what makes a product repairable. The documentation must be easy to find, spare parts must be available, the product must come apart, and a repair must not cost more than 30% of the purchase price. In 2024, 624 products earned the "repairable ecodesign" label, which is 5% of all ecodesigned products.

**Recyclability:** Decathlon is trying to think about what happens at end of life from the design stage onwards. It runs training courses to help engineers understand how textiles and equipment can be recycled, and in 2024, 166 people took them.

**Circular business models:** Decathlon's circular sales grew by 10.4% in 2024 compared with 2023. They made up 3.15% of the group's global revenue, up from 2.87% in 2023, so still a small slice of the business. This covers second life products, a buy-back system and a growing rental offer, which Decathlon sees as a priority, especially for children's and adult bicycles. Repair sits at the centre of its circular approach, with 1,730 in-store workshops (up from 1,712 in 2023) and 3,841 technicians worldwide. Repair also helps keep products safe when they move into second life or rental.

## Certifications and standards

Decathlon reports its progress against SBTi (Science Based Targets initiative) targets and uses the GHG Protocol to count its emissions. Its sustainability approach is also certified by AFNOR, the French national standards body.

## Recent concerns and key questions

If you are weighing up Decathlon's commitments, as an investor or simply as a customer, these questions are worth asking:

- **Supplier engagement:** what share of production sites and suppliers have validated science-based targets of their own, and who checks upstream Scope 3 emissions?
- **Absolute versus intensity:** are reported emissions decreasing in absolute terms while the business continues to grow, or are emissions only levelling off rather than falling for good?
- **Circular business scale-up:** how fast can repair, rental and second-hand grow compared with total sales, and do the numbers add up for the business?
- **Material transitions:** how realistic are the plans for lower-impact materials and manufacturing, such as eliminating coal use, shifting to renewables, and increasing recycled content, and what cost or supply-chain risks do they carry?
- **Offsetting versus reduction:** does the 2050 net-zero target lean heavily on offsets and credits, or on real cuts, and when will commitments turn into action?
- **Transparency and reporting:** how clear is the company's disclosure on scope definitions, baseline year, methodology and external assurance?
- **Business model growth risk:** as Decathlon expands into new markets, stores and product categories, is that growth separated from emissions, and could side effects, such as the carbon in new buildings or extra logistics, cancel out gains made elsewhere?
