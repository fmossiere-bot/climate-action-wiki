## About Decathlon

Decathlon is the third-largest sport item producer and provider globally. 60.7% of its emissions come from material extraction and production.

**Rental trial in Belgium:** a rental model priced between 25 and 95 euro per month has been tested in Belgium over the past four years. The model appears both beneficial for families and more profitable for the company, 5-10 times more profitable after four to five years. Based on 35 articles rented per year per person, the estimated impact is 191kg of CO2, which the company projects could fall to 83kg by 2033. The equivalent impact of buying those items new today would be around 2,100kg.

**Ecodesign products** now account for 38% of sales: 38% of everything sold in 2023 was ecodesigned, with up to a 25% reduction in carbon footprint compared with conventional equivalents. Decathlon reports a reduction in absolute emissions of approximately 11.7% in 2023 compared with its 2021 baseline.

## Company commitments

### Decarbonising company operations

Scope 1 and 2 emissions for Decathlon are largely about energy and material extraction. The company states it wants to reduce emissions across its entire value chain, which is wider than Scope 1 and 2 alone. Notably, Decathlon has moved away from a previous "economic intensity" target toward hard, absolute CO2 targets, and applies the same approach to its Scope 3 emissions.

**Decathlon's targets:**

- **Overall net-zero target:** Decathlon is committed to reaching net zero by 2050. Under the SBTi's definition of net zero, this means aiming for a 90% reduction in absolute emissions, with the remaining residual emissions neutralised to contribute to global carbon neutrality.
- **Near-term targets:** Decathlon commits to reducing absolute Scope 1 and 2 GHG emissions by 42% by 2030, from a 2021 base year, and to reducing absolute Scope 3 GHG emissions by 42% over the same timeframe.
- **Long-term targets:** Decathlon commits to reducing absolute Scope 1 and 2 GHG emissions by 90% by 2050, from a 2021 base year, and to reducing absolute Scope 3 GHG emissions by 90% over the same timeframe.

Moving from its previous commitment to reduce economic intensity (a 53% reduction in CO2 equivalent per euro of revenue by 2026, versus 2016) to a commitment on absolute CO2 equivalent emissions is a significant step in the company's climate strategy: reducing carbon intensity does not necessarily mean emitting less greenhouse gas overall, so committing to absolute reductions closes that gap. Decathlon provides fairly transparent detail on its targets, the choices behind them and its timeline, in a tone that reads as matter-of-fact rather than overblown.

### Scope 1, 2 and 3, explained

To carry out a greenhouse gas assessment, companies analyse emissions from their own activity and from third parties. The boundaries within which emissions are analysed are called scopes, and there are three main types:

- **Scope 1:** direct emissions, for example gas combustion in store boilers.
- **Scope 2:** indirect emissions from purchased energy consumption.
- **Scope 3:** other indirect emissions across the value chain, including raw material extraction, product manufacturing, product transport, and employee commuting.

### The four levers for reducing its carbon footprint

Decathlon has organised its greenhouse gas reduction plan around four main levers: decarbonising its own footprint, optimising its product offer and designing products that last longer, scaling up circular business models, and influencing its wider ecosystem through advocacy and mobilisation.

**Raw materials:** raw material extraction and product manufacturing account for 60.7% of Decathlon's carbon footprint, making this the priority area for action. Its approach includes investing in more sustainable raw materials, particularly in the metal, textile and footwear sectors, where they have high potential for reducing CO2 emissions; designing products that use fewer raw materials through functional and technical optimisation; preferring lower-emission materials, such as wood over metal; increasing recycled content up to the technical maximum without compromising product quality; and favouring raw materials that are recyclable at end of life.

**Production energy:** working with suppliers to improve energy efficiency, designing products that require fewer processing stages, using less energy-intensive processes such as innovative dyeing methods, and transitioning to a less carbon-intensive energy mix by eliminating coal use and increasing renewable energy.

**Product transport:** optimising upstream logistics to reduce kilometres travelled per product, working with international transport suppliers to use less carbon-intensive fuels, and working with downstream transport suppliers on lower-emission solutions.

**Decathlon's own sites:** using renewable energy and energy efficiency strategies, and favouring refurbishment of existing buildings over new construction for new shops.

**New business models:** designing durable, repairable and recyclable products suited to rental and second life, and developing second life, rental and repair services.

**Influencing the wider system:** training staff on environmental issues, encouraging soft mobility among customers and staff, raising customer awareness toward more considered consumption, and taking part in international initiatives such as World Clean Up Day events across Decathlon sites worldwide.

Since energy used in production worldwide is still generated primarily from fossil fuels (coal, gas and oil), Decathlon decided in 2017 to help its suppliers transition to cleaner energy, given that in 2024, 60.7% of the company's total CO2 emissions came from raw material extraction and the manufacturing stages of its sports goods.

## Towards a more circular model

Extending product lifespan through durability or repairability, improving recyclability, and encouraging second life and product rental are all measures pushing the company toward a more circular business model.

**Durability:** using methodologies and tests developed since 2020, durability criteria are now built into the specifications for products following an ecodesign approach. Engineers use a reference framework and durability standards, based on this test-based methodology, to evaluate product durability and select more resistant components and materials. This year, reference frameworks were completed for running shoes, sailing shoes, everyday shoes, dance shoes, football shirts, jackets and coats, goalkeeper gloves, socks, yoga mats, knee pads, life jackets and suitcases.

**Repairability:** using the repairability index developed in France by ADEME (the French environment and energy management agency) as a guide, Decathlon has established criteria defining a product's repair potential: documentation must be accessible, replacement parts must be available, the product must be able to be disassembled, and the cost of repair must not exceed 30% of the purchase price. In 2024, 624 products were granted the "repairable ecodesign" qualification, representing 5% of all products following an ecodesign approach.

**Recyclability:** Decathlon is working to better account for product end of life and improve recyclability from the design phase onward. To raise engineers' awareness of textile and equipment recyclability, the company offers dedicated training courses; in 2024, 166 people were trained on these subjects.

**Circular business models:** Decathlon's circular sales grew by 10.4% in 2024 compared with 2023, representing 3.15% of the group's global revenue, up from 2.87% in 2023. This includes second life products, a buy-back system, and expanding product rentals, which Decathlon has identified as a priority, particularly for children's and adult bicycles. Product repair, meanwhile, is the cornerstone of Decathlon's circular economy approach, supported by a network of 1,730 in-store workshops (up from 1,712 in 2023) and 3,841 technicians worldwide; repair also plays a major role in supporting the safety of products moving into second life and rental.

## Certifications and standards

Decathlon reports progress against SBTi (Science Based Targets initiative) targets and uses the GHG Protocol as its accounting framework. It also holds AFNOR certification, the French national standards body's certification for its sustainability approach.

## Recent concerns and key questions

For anyone assessing Decathlon's commitments from an ESG risk or opportunity perspective, several questions are worth digging into:

- **Supplier engagement:** what proportion of production sites and suppliers have validated science-based targets of their own, and what oversight exists on Scope 3 upstream emissions?
- **Absolute versus intensity:** are reported emissions decreasing in absolute terms while the business continues to grow, or are there signs of emissions merely levelling off rather than structurally falling?
- **Circular business scale-up:** how quickly can the repair, rental and second-hand business scale meaningfully relative to total volume, and what are the unit economics and business model risks?
- **Material transitions:** how realistic are the plans for lower-impact materials and manufacturing, such as eliminating coal use, shifting to renewables, and increasing recycled content, and what cost or supply-chain risks do they carry?
- **Offsetting versus reduction:** does the 2050 net-zero target rely heavily on offsets and credits, or on internal reductions, and what is the timeline for moving from commitment to implementation?
- **Transparency and reporting:** how clear is the company's disclosure on scope definitions, baseline year, methodology and external assurance?
- **Business model growth risk:** as Decathlon expands into new markets, stores and product categories, is that growth decoupled from emissions, and are there externalities, such as built-environment carbon or logistics, that could offset any gains made elsewhere?
