## Some interesting facts

Coca-Cola is one of the most recognised brands in the world, with its logo recognised by 94% of the global population according to surveys. The company also played a significant role in shaping the modern image of Santa Claus: in the 1930s, its advertisements depicted Santa as a jolly, plump man in a red suit, an image that became the popular one we know today.

## Coca-Cola's overall impact score

Coca-Cola has a general impact score of -1.96 on Green Digest's scale, which runs from -5 to +5. Its impact is spread across 10 UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and 11 topics, split between positive and negative analyses.

In the socio-economic sphere, Coca-Cola delivers approximately 2.2 billion servings daily worldwide, making its products a fixture of daily life for billions of people. The company offers 3,900 products, ranging from sodas to water to energy drinks, through a network of 30 million retail outlets and 200 bottling partners. It employs roughly 79,000 people (as of 2024) and pays salaries above the industry and country average. It has achieved gender parity, with over 40% women's participation by ILO standards, at all levels except its senior leadership. The company also supports millions of farmers in its supply chain, and has reached more than 7 million people across 41 African countries by financing projects that provide access to clean water, sanitation and hygiene and that protect local watersheds. However, Coca-Cola derives about 64% of its sales from sugary soft drinks, which have been linked to obesity, cardiovascular disease and cancer, contributing to the global disease burden.

Environmentally, Coca-Cola emits over 64 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent annually (2023 figure), equal to the electricity use of more than 12 million homes for a year. The company also withdraws approximately 308.7 million cubic metres of water annually, 20% of it in areas of high or extremely high water stress. It generates approximately 1.75 million tonnes of waste (of which it recovers 90%) and uses almost 6 million tonnes of packaging, mainly PET bottles, contributing to plastic pollution given that 91% of plastics worldwide end up in landfill. It also sources about 6.5 million tonnes of raw materials, including sugarcane, timber and soy, which require hundreds of thousands of hectares of land to cultivate and so contribute to land degradation.

The impact score is current as of July 2024 and may change as it is continuously updated.

## Coca-Cola's ESG rating versus its impact score

For comparison, Coca-Cola holds an AAA rating in MSCI's ESG evaluation. However, ESG ratings from MSCI ESG Research are designed to measure a company's resilience to financially material ESG risks: they measure how effectively a company manages ESG risks to itself, not the company's impact on the world.

## Impact scores by SDG

Positively, and weighted by significance, Coca-Cola scores highest on Partnership for the Goals (+3.34), followed by No Poverty (+3.24) and Gender Equality (+1.44). Negatively, it scores worst on Good Health and Well-being (-1.61), followed by Responsible Consumption and Production (-2.75) and Climate Action (-3.49).

The company both positively and negatively influences two SDGs: Good Health and Well-being (SDG 3) and Decent Work and Economic Growth (SDG 8). For example, it positively affects Good Health and Well-being by providing enjoyment to billions of people daily through its products, but negatively affects the same SDG since 64% of its sales come from unhealthy products.

## Final assessment

Coca-Cola's key social and environmental impact lies in its role providing drinks that bring enjoyment to billions of people in over 200 countries. The company also provides thousands of jobs, supports millions of farmers in its supply chain, reaches millions of people through its community initiatives, and pays billions in taxes. On the other hand, Coca-Cola has a substantial negative impact through its emissions, water use and waste production, and derives most of its sales from unhealthy products linked to obesity, cardiovascular disease and cancer. Its overall score of -1.96 reflects the balance of all these factors.

## What Coca-Cola claims and has committed to

As of 2024-2025, Coca-Cola's key public sustainability commitments include:

- **Water stewardship:** since 2015, the company says it has "returned more than 100% of the water used in finished products globally, on an aggregate level, to nature and communities." It aims to return 100% of the water used at each of its roughly 200 "high-risk" facilities by 2035.
- **Packaging, plastics and waste:** targets of 35-40% recycled material across primary packaging (plastic, glass, aluminium) by 2035, including 30-35% recycled plastic globally. It aims to help ensure collection or recycling of 70-75% of the bottles and cans it introduces each year by 2035, and states that nearly all its packaging (over 95%) is, or will be, recyclable under its design standards.
- **Climate and emissions:** a target to reduce Scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions in line with a 1.5°C trajectory from a 2019 baseline, by 2035. Coca-Cola updated its voluntary environmental goals in 2024, extending many timeframes to 2035.
- **Agriculture and supply chain:** working with suppliers on more sustainable agricultural practices, aiming to improve climate resilience and preserve ecosystems.
- **Transparency:** the company has publicly evolved its voluntary water, packaging and climate goals as of late 2024, which it presents as an upgrade in ambition.

## Where critics raise concerns

- **Dropped or weakened targets:** Coca-Cola has been accused of quietly dropping a previously stated target of 25% reusable or refillable packaging by 2030, part of its earlier "World Without Waste" plan. Before a global plastics summit, this target was removed from its public goals, with the focus shifting toward recycled content, collection and recycling instead. Critics see this as a rollback away from reuse toward recycling.
- **Potentially misleading "100% recycled/recyclable" claims:** consumer and environmental groups in Europe, including BEUC, ClientEarth and ECOS, filed a legal complaint arguing that Coca-Cola (and other companies) mislead consumers by labelling bottles "100% recycled" or "100% recyclable" when some components, such as caps and labels, are not made from recycled material or are not recyclable in practice in many jurisdictions. Coca-Cola has agreed to clarify some of these labels, for example, that "100% recycled" refers only to the bottle body, not necessarily the labels or caps.
- **Focus on recycling over reduction:** critics argue that increasing recycled content or collection rates does not fully solve the problems caused by single-use plastic, particularly where collection systems are imperfect, recycling leads to downcycling, or bottles are lost to litter. The removal of the 25% reuse target is seen by some as a retreat from upstream solutions.
- **Plastic pollution record:** Coca-Cola is regularly listed among the world's largest plastic polluters. Reports and NGOs note that despite its recycling claims, large volumes of Coca-Cola plastic still appear in litter and waste audits globally.
- **Ambiguity in definitions:** because actual recycled content, the types of plastic used, collection methods and the quality of recycled material vary considerably by location, some claims may be technically accurate but misleading in terms of real-world impact. A bottle labelled "recyclable," for instance, is only meaningfully recyclable where local infrastructure supports it.

## Where the tensions lie

Putting these together, several tension points emerge. Coca-Cola appears to have scaled back a prior commitment to reuse and refillable packaging in favour of recycling and collection, which could reflect an avoidance of harder, more disruptive sustainability choices. What the company can promise in markets with strong waste infrastructure, such as Europe, may not translate to feasibility in many other markets, meaning some claims reflect "ideal practice" more than global reality. Labels like "100% recycled/recyclable" are powerful marketing tools that can overstate practical impact if consumers do not understand what is excluded. And with ambitious 2035 targets, the open question is whether progress is fast enough, and whether there is binding external verification and clear interim milestones, since weak accountability on those points invites scepticism.

## Recent changes

In 2024, Coca-Cola updated its voluntary environmental goals, extending many objectives to 2035. As part of that update, some previously public goals, notably around refillable and returnable packaging reuse, appear to have been removed or de-emphasised. The company has also responded to complaints about its labelling, such as "100% recycled," by agreeing to clarify what exactly the claim refers to.

## Overall assessment

There is substantial evidence that Coca-Cola is making genuine efforts: returning water, using recycled materials, setting science-aligned targets, and renewing its goals. But there are also valid criticisms, particularly around the shift away from reuse, ambiguity in claims, and whether commitments will translate into real, measurable reductions in harm, especially in regions with less recycling infrastructure. Much of the company's public messaging leans heavily on recyclability and collection rather than acknowledging trade-offs and limits, such as plastic leakage or the limits of recycling systems, which carries a real risk of over-simplified claims. There is also a plausible greenwashing risk in how ambitious goals are stated without always showing concrete intermediate results, or in how previous plans have quietly been modified or removed.
