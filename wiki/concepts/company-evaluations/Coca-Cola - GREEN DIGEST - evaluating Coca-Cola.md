## Some interesting facts

Coca-Cola is one of the best-known brands in the world: according to surveys, 94% of people worldwide recognise its logo. The company also helped popularise the modern image of Santa Claus. From 1931 its adverts, painted by Haddon Sundblom, showed Santa as a jolly, plump man in a red suit. Coca-Cola did not invent that look, which had appeared in earlier illustrations, but its adverts helped make it the picture most of us know today.

## Coca-Cola's overall impact score

Coca-Cola has an overall impact score of **-1.96** on Green Digest's scale, which runs from -5 to +5. The assessment covers 10 UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and 11 topics, with both positive and negative effects counted.

On the social and economic side, Coca-Cola serves about 2.2 billion drinks a day worldwide, so its products are part of daily life for billions of people. It sells 3,900 products, from fizzy drinks to water to energy drinks, through 30 million shops and outlets and 200 bottling partners. It employs roughly 79,000 people (as of 2024) and pays above the average for its industry and for each country. It has reached gender parity by ILO standards (over 40% women) at every level except senior leadership. The company also supports millions of farmers in its supply chain. By funding projects that bring clean water, sanitation and hygiene and protect local watersheds, it has reached more than 7 million people in 41 African countries. On the other hand, about 64% of its sales come from sugary soft drinks, which have been linked to obesity, heart and circulatory disease and cancer, and so add to the global burden of disease.

On the environmental side, Coca-Cola emits over **64 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent** a year (2023 figure), as much as the electricity used by more than 12 million homes in a year. It takes about 308.7 million cubic metres of water a year, 20% of it from areas where water is scarce or very scarce. It produces about 1.75 million tonnes of waste (and recovers 90% of it) and uses almost 6 million tonnes of packaging, mostly PET plastic bottles. That adds to plastic pollution, since 91% of plastics worldwide end up in landfill. It also buys about 6.5 million tonnes of raw materials, including sugarcane, timber and soy. Growing them takes hundreds of thousands of hectares of land, which adds to land degradation.

The impact score is correct as of July 2024 and may change, as it is updated continuously.

## Coca-Cola's ESG rating versus its impact score

For comparison, Coca-Cola holds MSCI's top ESG rating, AAA. But MSCI's ESG ratings are built to measure how well a company can withstand environmental, social and governance risks that could hurt it financially. They show how well a company manages risks to itself, not its impact on the world.

## Impact scores by SDG

On the positive side, weighted by how much each matters, Coca-Cola scores highest on Partnership for the Goals (+3.34), followed by No Poverty (+3.24) and Gender Equality (+1.44). On the negative side, Green Digest lists Good Health and Well-being (-1.61), followed by Responsible Consumption and Production (-2.75) and Climate Action (-3.49).

The company has both good and bad effects on two SDGs: Good Health and Well-being (SDG 3) and Decent Work and Economic Growth (SDG 8). It scores positively on Good Health and Well-being because billions of people enjoy its drinks every day, and negatively on the same goal because 64% of its sales come from unhealthy products.

## Final assessment

Coca-Cola's main social impact is providing drinks that billions of people in over 200 countries enjoy. It also creates thousands of jobs, supports millions of farmers in its supply chain, reaches millions of people through community projects and pays billions in taxes. Against that, its emissions, water use and waste do considerable harm, and most of its sales come from unhealthy products linked to obesity, heart disease and cancer. Its overall score of -1.96 weighs all of these together.

## What Coca-Cola claims and has committed to

As of 2024-2025, Coca-Cola's main public sustainability commitments include:

- **Water stewardship:** since 2015, the company says it has "returned more than 100% of the water used in finished products globally, on an aggregate level, to nature and communities." It aims to return 100% of the water used at each of its roughly 200 "high-risk" facilities by 2035.
- **Packaging, plastics and waste:** targets of 35-40% recycled material across primary packaging (plastic, glass, aluminium) by 2035, including 30-35% recycled plastic worldwide. By 2035 it aims to help get 70-75% of the bottles and cans it puts on the market each year collected or recycled. It says nearly all its packaging (over 95%) is, or will be, recyclable under its design standards.
- **Climate and emissions:** a target to reduce Scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions in line with a 1.5°C trajectory from a 2019 baseline, by 2035. Coca-Cola updated its voluntary environmental goals in 2024, extending many timeframes to 2035.
- **Agriculture and supply chain:** working with suppliers on more sustainable farming, to make crops better able to cope with climate change and to protect ecosystems.
- **Transparency:** in late 2024 the company publicly revised its voluntary water, packaging and climate goals, which it presents as raising its ambition.

## Where critics raise concerns

- **Dropped or weakened targets:** Coca-Cola has been accused of quietly dropping its earlier target of 25% reusable or refillable packaging by 2030, part of its "World Without Waste" plan. Ahead of a global plastics summit, the target disappeared from its public goals, and the focus moved to recycled content, collection and recycling. Critics see this as a step back from reuse towards recycling.
- **Possibly misleading "100% recycled/recyclable" claims:** consumer and environmental groups in Europe, including BEUC, ClientEarth and ECOS, filed a legal complaint. They argued that Coca-Cola (and other companies) mislead shoppers by labelling bottles "100% recycled" or "100% recyclable" when some parts, such as caps and labels, are not made from recycled material or cannot in practice be recycled in many places. Coca-Cola has agreed to clarify some of these labels, for example that "100% recycled" refers only to the body of the bottle, not necessarily the label or cap.
- **Focus on recycling over reduction:** critics argue that more recycled content or higher collection rates do not fully solve the problems of single-use plastic, especially where collection is patchy, recycling turns plastic into lower-grade material (downcycling), or bottles end up as litter. Some see the removal of the 25% reuse target as a retreat from solutions that cut waste at source.
- **Plastic pollution record:** Coca-Cola is regularly named among the world's biggest plastic polluters. Reports and NGOs point out that, despite its recycling claims, large amounts of Coca-Cola plastic still turn up in litter and waste audits around the world.
- **Vague definitions:** recycled content, the types of plastic used, collection methods and the quality of recycled material all vary a lot from place to place. So some claims may be technically accurate but misleading about the real-world effect. A bottle labelled "recyclable," for instance, is only really recyclable where local facilities can handle it.

## Where the tensions lie

Taken together, these points show several tensions. Coca-Cola seems to have scaled back an earlier commitment to reusable and refillable packaging in favour of recycling and collection, which may mean avoiding harder and more disruptive choices. What the company can promise in markets with good waste systems, such as Europe, may not be achievable in many other markets, so some claims describe "ideal practice" more than what happens worldwide. Labels like "100% recycled/recyclable" are effective marketing, and they can overstate the real benefit if shoppers do not understand what is left out. With ambitious 2035 targets, the open question is whether progress is fast enough, and whether there is binding outside verification with clear milestones along the way. Without that accountability, scepticism is understandable.

## Recent changes

In 2024, Coca-Cola updated its voluntary environmental goals and pushed many of them back to 2035. In that update, some goals it had published before, notably on refillable and returnable packaging, appear to have been dropped or played down. The company has also responded to complaints about labels such as "100% recycled" by agreeing to make clear exactly what the claim covers.

## Overall assessment

There is good evidence that Coca-Cola is making real efforts: returning water, using recycled materials, setting targets in line with science and renewing its goals. There are also fair criticisms, especially about the move away from reuse, vague claims, and whether its commitments will lead to real, measurable cuts in harm, above all in regions with fewer recycling facilities. Much of its public messaging leans heavily on recyclability and collection, and says little about trade-offs and limits, such as plastic escaping into the environment or the limits of recycling systems. That risks claims that oversimplify. There is also a plausible risk of greenwashing in how ambitious goals are announced without always showing concrete progress along the way, and in how earlier plans have been quietly changed or dropped.
