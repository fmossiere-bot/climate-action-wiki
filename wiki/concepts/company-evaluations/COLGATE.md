## Some facts about Colgate-Palmolive

Colgate-Palmolive is a very large company that dominates its market:

- It holds 41% of the global toothpaste market.
- 77% of its revenue comes from its Care/Hygiene and Home divisions.
- The company's plastic footprint is just over 287,000 metric tonnes across its whole business, which covers household and personal care products, food products, and health care and industrial supplies.
- The Break Free From Plastic Audit 2019 identified Colgate-Palmolive as the eighth-biggest plastic polluter worldwide, and the second-biggest polluter in Africa.

## Greenwashing?

At that scale, greenwashing matters more. When an established giant with around 40% of the market says it is moving to "recyclable tubes," the promise counts, but only if it comes with openness, independent checks, timelines and measurable results.

Many companies have launched "recyclable tubes," often only in a few markets or for part of their range. The technical problems are real. Barrier layers, tubes made of several materials, ink and caps all make recycling harder, and so does leftover product in the tube. What matters is the share of a company's tubes that can really be recycled, how "recyclable" is defined, and how many are actually collected and recycled in practice.

Customers have sued Colgate for greenwashing, with some reason. Colgate put sustainability claims prominently on its toothpaste packaging without, critics argue, backing them up. Supermarket shelves are full of similar claims: "fully recyclable," "100% responsibly sourced," "made using recycled content." According to reporting on the case, the Colgate tubes in question could only be recycled by a small number of customers in the United States.

The people bringing the case said they bought the products because they believed Colgate's claim that the tubes were recyclable. Like most shoppers, they expected to put them out in their kerbside recycling collection, and that turned out not to be possible. They argued that if it had been clear they could not recycle the tubes that way, they would not have bought the product. Colgate asked for the complaint to be dismissed, arguing that the term "recyclable tube" is an "accurate representation of the intrinsic character of the products," and pointed to a disclaimer included on the packaging. A judge allowed the suit to proceed.

**The technical claim and controversy:**

- Colgate developed a toothpaste tube made of a single material, HDPE (high-density polyethylene), designed to be recycled like a milk bottle, and has promoted it publicly and put it on sale.
- Several independent reports and the US lawsuit argue that many US recycling plants do not in fact accept toothpaste tubes, so the "recyclable tube" claims on packaging have been challenged in court. Media coverage has noted that Colgate has acknowledged these problems, and that some 2025 targets, particularly for flexible pouches, may slip.

## Colgate's circularity commitments

In a podcast interview, Ann Tracy, Colgate-Palmolive's Chief Sustainability Officer, talked about the company's progress on circularity (keeping materials in use instead of throwing them away). The main challenge she described is changing the products themselves. All of Colgate's products are made from plastic, and the goal is to reduce the company's plastic impact on the planet. Colgate is part of the Ellen MacArthur Foundation's Global Commitment.

Colgate has made two main commitments: to reduce the amount of virgin plastic it uses by a third by 2025, compared with a 2019 baseline, and, as part of the MacArthur Foundation commitment, to make all packaging recyclable, reusable or compostable by 2025.

For toothpaste, that meant redesigning tubes, which could not be recycled before, so that they could be. It was a hard design problem, because the product also had to stay acceptable to customers, and the work took several years. The first recyclable tube came out in 2019. Today 95% of tubes across the industry (Colgate's and other companies') use this recyclable design, because other manufacturers, including mayonnaise makers, have had to move to similar formats. Around 40% of all toothpaste tubes on the market are Colgate's own, in line with its 41% share of the global toothpaste market. The next challenge is the recycling itself. The company has set up partnerships in the US and is now expanding into Europe, while also teaching customers how to recycle the tubes properly.

## Talking Trash: a critical assessment

The NGO report "Talking Trash" (2020) is more critical. Much of the research it cites dates from 2019, so more recent data may give a somewhat different picture.

- Colgate-Palmolive's plastic footprint, as above, is just over 287,000 metric tonnes.
- The Break Free From Plastic Audit 2019 ranked the company as the eighth-biggest plastic polluter worldwide, and the second-biggest in Africa.
- The report found Colgate-Palmolive's commitments on plastic pollution fairly thin. There is little about collecting packaging, or about supporting laws that require separate collection. Even its plan for meeting its minimum recycled-content target seems to focus on buying more recycled material, without backing compulsory collection systems to supply it.
- There is also very little detail on reuse and refill systems. The exception is the company's part in TerraCycle's Loop scheme, using reusable packaging, from the first half of 2020, and even that seemed to be a small trial rather than a real reuse and refill system across Colgate-Palmolive's range.
- The company set a minimum recycled-content target of 25% across all its plastic packaging by 2025. It reported to the New Plastics Economy Global Commitment that its actual use of recycled content stood at 7% of total plastic packaging.
- Recycled content also varies a lot by market and is concentrated in a few brands. In Latin America, for example, recycled content in PET bottles rose to 50% (from 0% and 25% previously) across four bottle types.
- Colgate-Palmolive's public messaging can mislead on this point. The company states on its website that "approximately 45% of our packaging materials by weight globally now come from recycled sources," even though the comparable figure for plastic specifically is only 7%.
- The company says it has "long been minimising the volume and weight" of its packaging, but it does not mention any cut in the actual number of single-use plastic items. Its reductions centre on making packaging lighter for a few brands in a few countries, such as a Palmolive hand-soap bottle in Italy, a Suavitel bottle in Mexico and the cap on Colgate toothpaste in Poland. That is a modest record given the company's total packaging tonnage.
- The company highlights projects meant to "transform" its packaging, such as shrink sleeves with perforated tear strips (telling customers to remove the sleeve so the bottle is easier to recycle) and the recyclable HDPE toothpaste tube. But that tube sold at roughly six times the price of regular Colgate toothpaste, full roll-out of recyclable tubes across all brands was only planned for 2025, and it is unclear whether the recyclable tubes themselves contain any recycled material.

## Sustainability report targets

Colgate-Palmolive's own sustainability reporting repeats its main target of reducing virgin plastic use by one third by 2025, compared with 2019.
