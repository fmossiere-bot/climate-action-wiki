## Some facts about Colgate-Palmolive

Colgate-Palmolive is a large player with a dominant market share:

- It holds 41% of the global toothpaste market.
- 77% of its revenue comes from its Care/Hygiene and Home divisions.
- The company has a plastic footprint of just over 287,000 metric tonnes across its business, which spans household and personal care products, food products, and health care and industrial supplies.
- The Break Free From Plastic Audit 2019 identified Colgate-Palmolive as the eighth-biggest plastic polluter worldwide, and the second-biggest polluter in Africa.

## Greenwashing?

Given that scale, the question of greenwashing becomes more consequential: if a large incumbent with around 40% market share claims to shift to "recyclable tubes," the promise matters, but only if it is backed with transparency, third-party verification, timelines and performance metrics.

Many companies have rolled out "recyclable tubes," but often only in limited markets or across only part of their portfolio. The technical challenges are real: barrier layers, multi-material construction, ink and caps all complicate recycling, as does residual product left in the tube. What matters is what fraction of a company's tubes are actually recyclable, how "recyclable" is defined, and how many are actually collected and recycled in practice.

Customers have sued Colgate for greenwashing, and with some justification. Colgate has placed sustainability claims prominently on its toothpaste packaging without, critics argue, backing them up. Supermarket aisles are full of similar claims: "fully recyclable," "100% responsibly sourced," "made using recycled content." According to reporting on the case, the Colgate tubes in question were only recyclable for a small number of customers in the United States.

The plaintiffs alleged that they purchased the products because they believed Colgate's claims that the tubes were recyclable, and expected to be able to recycle them through their curb-side recycling programme, as most consumers would assume. This turned out not to be the case. They argued that had it been clear they could not recycle the tubes that way, they would not have purchased the product. Colgate filed to dismiss the complaint, arguing that the term "recyclable tube" is an "accurate representation of the intrinsic character of the products," and pointed to a disclaimer included on the packaging. A judge allowed the suit to proceed.

**The technical claim and controversy:**

- Colgate developed a monomaterial HDPE (high-density polyethylene) toothpaste tube, designed to be recyclable like a milk bottle, and has publicly promoted and shipped it.
- Multiple independent reports and the US lawsuit contend that many US recycling facilities do not actually accept toothpaste tubes, so consumer-facing "recyclable tube" claims have been challenged in court. Media coverage has noted that Colgate has acknowledged these challenges, and that some 2025 targets, particularly for flexible pouches, may be delayed.

## Colgate's circularity commitments

In a podcast interview, Colgate-Palmolive's Chief Sustainability Officer Ann Tracy discussed the company's circularity journey. The main challenge she described is the change required to the products themselves: all of Colgate's products are made from plastic, and the goal is to reduce the company's plastic impact on the planet. Colgate is part of the Ellen MacArthur Foundation's Global Commitment.

Colgate has made two headline commitments: to reduce the amount of virgin plastic it uses by a third by 2025, compared with a 2019 baseline, and, as part of the MacArthur Foundation commitment, to make all packaging recyclable, reusable or compostable by 2025.

For toothpaste specifically, that meant redesigning tubes to be recyclable, which they were not previously. This has been a serious design challenge, since Colgate also had to keep the product acceptable to customers throughout, and the design process took several years. The first recyclable tube was implemented in 2019. Today, 95% of tubes across the industry (not just Colgate's) use this recyclable design, since other manufacturers, including mayonnaise producers, have had to move to similar formats; close to 50% of all tubes on the market are Colgate's own. The next challenge is recycling itself: the company has rolled out partnerships in the US and is now expanding into Europe, alongside efforts to educate consumers on how to recycle the tubes correctly.

## Talking Trash: a critical assessment

The NGO report "Talking Trash" (2020) offers a more critical view, though much of the underlying research it cites dates to 2019, so more recent data may paint a somewhat different picture.

- Colgate-Palmolive's plastic footprint, again, stands at just over 287,000 metric tonnes.
- The Break Free From Plastic Audit 2019 ranked the company as the eighth-biggest plastic polluter worldwide, and the second-biggest in Africa.
- The report found Colgate-Palmolive's commitments on tackling plastic pollution to be relatively sparse: there is little discussion of packaging collection or support for legislation mandating separate collection. Even the company's strategy for meeting its minimum recycled-content target appears to focus on procuring more recycled content, without corresponding support for mandatory collection systems.
- There is also very little detail on the company's development of reuse-and-refill systems, beyond its participation in TerraCycle's Loop initiative with reusable packaging from the first half of 2020, and even that appeared to operate at a small, experimental scale rather than as a significant reuse-and-refill system across Colgate-Palmolive's product range.
- The company set a minimum recycled-content target of 25% across all its plastic packaging by 2025. It reported to the New Plastics Economy Global Commitment that its actual use of recycled content stood at 7% of total plastic packaging.
- Recycled content also varies significantly by market and concentrates on a few brands. In Latin America, for example, recycled content in PET bottles rose to 50% (from 0% and 25% previously) across four bottle types.
- Colgate-Palmolive's public messaging can be misleading on this point: the company states on its website that "approximately 45% of our packaging materials by weight globally now come from recycled sources," even though the comparable figure for plastic specifically is only 7%.
- While the company says it has "long been minimising the volume and weight" of its packaging, there is no mention of an absolute reduction in single-use plastic units. Its focus on reduction centres on lightweighting in a few brands and geographies, such as reducing the weight of a Palmolive hand-soap bottle in Italy, a Suavitel bottle in Mexico, and the cap on Colgate toothpaste in Poland, an unimpressive track record given the company's total packaging tonnage.
- The company highlights projects intended to "transform" its packaging portfolio, such as shrink sleeves with perforated tear tabs (directing consumers to remove the sleeve to improve bottle recyclability) and the recyclable HDPE toothpaste tube. However, that tube retails at roughly six times the price of regular Colgate toothpaste, with full roll-out of recyclable tubes across all brands only planned by 2025, and it is unclear whether the recyclable tubes themselves contain recycled content.

## Sustainability report targets

Colgate-Palmolive's own sustainability reporting reiterates its headline target of reducing virgin plastic use by one third by 2025, compared with 2019.
