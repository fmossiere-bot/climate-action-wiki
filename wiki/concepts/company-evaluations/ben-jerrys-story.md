> **Editorial summary:** Ben & Jerry's built its reputation as proof that a company could sell ice cream and fight for social causes at the same time. This page traces that story from a Vermont petrol station in 1978, through its unusual 2000 sale to Unilever with an independent board written into the deal, to the 2025 spin-off into the Magnum Ice Cream Company and the bitter, ongoing "Free Ben & Jerry's" campaign that followed. It draws on a July 2026 Guardian investigation and a Bloomberg Green podcast interview with co-founder Ben Cohen, and looks honestly at where the brand's climate commitments hold up and where they do not.

## The ice cream that started a movement

Ben Cohen and Jerry Greenfield met as childhood friends and opened their first ice cream shop in a converted petrol station in Burlington, Vermont, in 1978, funded by a $12,000 investment (Cohen borrowed nearly half of it). What started as a homemade, chunky-style ice cream company grew fast, and just a decade later Cohen and Greenfield were invited to the White House, where President Ronald Reagan named them US Small Business Persons of the Year.

From the start, the company picked fights most businesses would avoid. When the two men tried to expand into Boston in the 1980s, Pillsbury, which had just bought their rival Häagen-Dazs, pressured shared distributors to drop Ben & Jerry's or lose Häagen-Dazs. Rather than fight it quietly through the courts, Cohen and Greenfield ran a public campaign called "What's the Doughboy Afraid Of?", flying banners over Boston sports stadiums, printing an 800 number on their ice cream tubs, and mailing bumper stickers and "Legal Defense Fund" T-shirts to supporters. The calls flooded in, the press picked it up, Pillsbury's image took a beating, and it backed down. It set the template for how the brand would operate for the next four decades: turn its customers' goodwill into pressure, and use the brand itself as an activism tool, on causes from refugee support and LGBTQ+ rights to climate justice.

## A different kind of business

> Ben & Jerry's is widely regarded as one of the clearest examples of a "values-led" business, alongside companies like [Patagonia](patagonia-story.md). Its reputation rests less on any single certification and more on a decades-long, consistent record: it criticised the Iraq War, was an early corporate supporter of same-sex marriage, backed Occupy Wall Street while under corporate ownership, and called to "dismantle white supremacy" after the murder of George Floyd. That consistency is what built what Cohen calls a relationship with customers "based on shared values", rather than the more typical, and more fragile, connection built by advertising alone.

That reputation is genuine, but it is worth being precise about what it rests on. Ben & Jerry's has not needed to invent a green image through marketing, the way some brands accused of [greenwashing](../key-ideas/Greenwashing.md) have. Its credibility comes from having taken real, sometimes costly, public stances since long before "purpose-driven business" became a marketing category. The honest caveat, as this page sets out below, is that this reputation is strongest on social and political causes, and weaker on the company's own core environmental footprint: dairy.

## Commitments on climate, and their limits

> Ben & Jerry's has taken some concrete environmental steps. It has invested in renewable energy for its manufacturing, and its "Caring Dairy" programme, run for nearly two decades across farms in Vermont, the Netherlands, Germany and the UK, works with dairy farmers on climate-smart farming practices, farmer and farmworker welfare, animal welfare standards, and reducing the environmental impact of milk production. It has also launched a full line of non-dairy, oat-based frozen desserts as a lower-footprint alternative to its classic pints.

But when asked directly what more could be done, Cohen was unusually candid. Dairy farming is a significant source of methane, mostly from cattle burps rather than the joked-about "cow farts", and it remains one of Ben & Jerry's biggest environmental footprints. Asked whether the company should be telling customers plainly that milk and cream carry a real environmental cost, in the way it has been willing to take a stand on almost everything else, Cohen's answer was: "It's a good question. I don't know." The company has provided an alternative, the non-dairy range, but it has stopped short of saying outright that its flagship dairy product has a climate cost worth confronting head-on. That is a genuine gap between a brand that will campaign loudly on Gaza, ICE raids or voting rights, and one that has been quieter about the emissions baked into its own best-selling product. It is not straightforward greenwashing, since nothing has been misrepresented, but it is a useful case study in how far even an unusually values-led company will go when the hard choice is its own core product rather than someone else's policy.

## Sold to Unilever, on one condition

By 2000, Ben & Jerry's was a public company, and when the global food giant Unilever made a takeover offer, Cohen and Greenfield did not have a controlling stake to block it. "It happened against your wishes," as Cohen has put it, and he has since said he regrets taking the company public at all, believing that a B Corporation structure or a steward-ownership model, both of which existed by 2026 but not in the 1980s, would have prevented the takeover altogether.

What made the $326m deal unusual was what came with it. Cohen and Greenfield feared that Ben & Jerry's social mission would evaporate under a conventional owner, and resisted a sale on those grounds. Because the mission needed to keep evolving rather than being frozen into a fixed contract clause, the two sides instead created a wholly owned subsidiary with its own independent board of directors, holding legal authority in perpetuity over the brand's social mission, ice cream quality and use of the trademark, separate from Unilever's normal corporate control. The Wharton School held the structure up for years as a model for preserving a company's founding purpose after acquisition. Under it, Ben & Jerry's kept campaigning much as before, and Cohen has said that up to around 2017, he and Greenfield were broadly satisfied with how Unilever honoured the arrangement.

## Occupied territories and the breaking point

The relationship broke down over Israel and Palestine. In 2021, after a lengthy review, Ben & Jerry's independent board, its management, and Unilever all agreed the company should stop selling ice cream in the occupied West Bank, saying it was "inconsistent with our values". According to Cohen, the Israeli government threatened to expel all of Unilever's businesses from the country in response. Unilever then sold the Ben & Jerry's Israeli trademark to a local licensee, who continues to sell ice cream there under a "Milk & Honey" flavour, a decision the independent board says fell outside Unilever's authority and inside its own.

From there, the dispute widened. In November 2024, the board sued Unilever in US federal court, alleging it was being blocked from voicing support for Gaza and calling for a halt to US aid to Israel, a case still working through the courts. In 2025, Unilever removed Ben & Jerry's CEO David Stever, which the company's own board said was designed to stifle its activism, including a board-approved watermelon flavour created in solidarity with Palestine that was blocked before release (Unilever says Stever's role was simply restructured around the spin-off, and that he chose to leave). In July 2026, Ben & Jerry's philanthropic foundation was forced to close after Magnum cut its funding, following what Magnum describes as a governance investigation and what the foundation's trustees dispute.

## Splitting off into Magnum, and the fight to get free

In July 2025, Unilever spun its entire ice cream division, including Ben & Jerry's, Magnum, Wall's, Klondike, Breyers, Talenti and others, into a separate, newly listed company called the Magnum Ice Cream Company, worth roughly $9-11bn. Jerry Greenfield resigned from the board that September, the first time in nearly fifty years he had stepped away from the company, writing that "if the company couldn't stand up for the things we believed, then it wasn't worth being a company at all." Cohen stayed and launched the "Free Ben & Jerry's" campaign, asking Magnum to sell the brand back so it can operate independently again.

The campaign has gathered real momentum, roughly 200,000 petition signatures by mid-2026, a wave of comments on Magnum's own social media (which Magnum has since configured so that tagged posts no longer appear on its feeds), and growing press coverage. In mid-2026 Cohen escalated further, calling for a boycott of Magnum's other brands (Yasso, Breyers, Talenti, Klondike) to pressure the wider company through its sales. He argues Magnum is undermining the very asset it paid for: a brand whose customer loyalty is built on "authenticity and attitude", not advertising, and that muzzling its activism, including a reported policy against criticising Donald Trump, makes the brand "pretty hypocritical" and damages its long-term value. He has also pointed out that Magnum has declined to share the financial data that would let him or outside investors properly value a buyout.

Magnum rejects all of this. A company spokesperson told the Guardian that "Ben & Jerry's is not for sale, and we are confident in the future because Ben & Jerry's is thriving as part of the Magnum Ice Cream Company", citing 3% organic sales growth in 2025 and 9.2% growth in the second quarter of 2026, and pointing to recent statements from the brand on ICE, refugee support, cannabis justice and voting rights as evidence its voice is intact. Magnum also says its overall social mission has received €500m ($576m) in funding since 2000, more than the original purchase price, and that the brand's footprint has grown from four countries to more than forty. Management experts quoted by the Guardian are sceptical the boycott will work: consumer boycotts rarely sustain the long attention span needed to move a multi-brand conglomerate, and today's climate of "anti-woke" backlash and declining shareholder support for ESG resolutions makes Cohen's task harder still. Cohen's own answer is that he expects to win eventually because, in his words, "there's millions of people who support Ben & Jerry's and support its social mission."

## The honest picture

Ben & Jerry's is a genuine outlier: a mass-market food brand that spent decades taking real, sometimes costly, public positions rather than simply running feel-good adverts, and it built a fifty-year, largely credible reputation doing so. The current fight shows both the strength and the fragility of that model. The independent board structure, unusual and hard-won in 2000, did protect the brand's mission for two decades, but it has proven unable to survive a determined owner once a genuinely divisive issue, Israel and Palestine, was on the table. And on its own core environmental footprint, dairy, the company has offered a real alternative in its non-dairy range but has not been willing to say plainly what it says about almost everything else: that the status quo has a cost. Readers should treat Ben & Jerry's as a useful example of both what values-led business can achieve, and where its limits sit once ownership, or the product itself, is the thing being asked to change.
