# Valuing People and the Planet: Four Models of Development Beyond GDP

## What it is
Four very different models of development have each tried, in their own way, to put human wellbeing and planetary limits ahead of raw economic growth: the Sarvodaya Shramadana movement in Sri Lanka, the Green New Deal in the United States, Costa Rica's sustainable development path, and Doughnut Economics from the United Kingdom. **Each offers a different answer to the same underlying question: what should an economy actually be for, if not just growth?**

## Why it matters
GDP growth remains the default measure of national success almost everywhere, yet it says nothing about whether people's basic needs are met or whether that growth is destroying the ecological systems everyone depends on. These four models each attempt, in practice or in theory, to build an economy around meeting people's needs within the planet's limits rather than around growth for its own sake, and Costa Rica in particular offers a real, decades-long case study of what that can look like in practice.

## Sarvodaya Shramadana (Sri Lanka)
Sarvodaya is a Sri Lankan development movement founded by Dr A.T. Ariyaratne, combining Buddhist principles with grassroots community organising. Its central idea is that development should serve the "awakening of all," not just economic indicators, built through village-level self-help and shared labour (*shramadana*, literally "the gift of labour"). It remains one of the longest-running grassroots development movements of its kind.

## The Green New Deal (United States)
The Green New Deal, associated with US politician Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, is a proposed framework for rapid, large-scale decarbonisation paired explicitly with protections for workers whose jobs would be affected by that transition. It exists as a short, 14-page congressional resolution in two parts: one addressing the scale of emissions cuts needed, the other addressing the people whose livelihoods depend on the industries being transformed. The Green New Deal has become central to the climate agenda pursued by the Biden administration, though in its legislative form it has involved significant concessions to the oil industry along the way.

## Costa Rica's sustainable development model
Costa Rica has pursued development within a healthy environment as an explicit national goal, setting a target to become carbon neutral. The country banned new oil exploration, now generates 70-75% of its electricity from hydropower, and runs an electric grid that is effectively 100% renewable. It also offers a vehicle buy-back scheme for fuel-powered cars to encourage the switch to electric vehicles. A 2020 RAND study found that Costa Rica's carbon-neutrality goal is both achievable and likely to benefit the country economically.

Perhaps the most striking result is on forest cover. Between 1940 and 1983, more than 70% of Costa Rica's forests were cleared to expand agriculture, and by 1987 forest cover had fallen to just 21%. In 1996, the government reversed course, introducing strong legislation against further deforestation alongside financial incentives for farmers to reforest their land, funded in part by a 3.5% tax on petrol. That policy has been remarkably effective: forest cover recovered to 52% in the years that followed, and by early 2024 had reached 60% of national territory, now home to one of the most biodiverse forests on Earth. Costa Rica's experience is a rare real-world example of a country growing its economy while simultaneously reversing, rather than accelerating, environmental damage.

## Doughnut Economics (United Kingdom)
Developed by economist Kate Raworth, **Doughnut Economics** reframes the goal of an economy entirely: instead of pursuing continuous GDP growth, it aims to meet everyone's basic needs while staying within the planet's ecological limits, visualised as a doughnut-shaped space between a social foundation (below which people fall short of a decent life) and an ecological ceiling (above which the planet's systems become unstable). The **Doughnut Economics Action Lab (DEAL)** launched in September 2020 to help translate the model from theory into practice, downscaling it to specific cities, businesses and other organisations, including detailed applications for Amsterdam and Brussels. The model is deliberately both a description of the problem and a theory of change: it does not just diagnose an unbalanced economy, it proposes a route to rebalancing it, and its logic aligns closely with existing frameworks like ESG when applied inside companies working on sustainability strategy.

A major 2025 update to the framework, published in *Nature*, introduced a revised set of 35 indicators tracking social deprivation and ecological overshoot from 2000 to 2022. The findings are stark: although global GDP more than doubled over that period, median progress on reducing human deprivation was modest, and would need to accelerate roughly fivefold to meet everyone's basic needs by 2030. Meanwhile, growth in ecological overshoot would need to stop immediately, and then reverse nearly twice as fast, to keep the Earth system stable by 2050. The distributional picture is especially telling: the richest 20% of nations, home to just 15% of the global population, are responsible for more than 40% of annual ecological overshoot, while the poorest 40% of countries, home to 42% of the global population, experience more than 60% of the world's social shortfall. The researchers argue these trends make the case for moving nations away from a dependence on perpetual GDP growth and towards regenerative, distributive economic activity that prioritises human needs and planetary integrity, both within and between countries.

The updated research also directly tests a common economic assumption: that as economies become richer, their environmental impact naturally declines. It finds "no evidence" of the sustained absolute decoupling of economic growth from environmental impact that this assumption requires; economies are becoming more efficient, but total resource impact keeps rising regardless. It also confirms that, past a certain point, further growth does little to improve key social outcomes like health, education and happiness, reinforcing the case that wellbeing can, in principle, keep improving even without continued growth, provided resources are distributed more fairly.

## Planetary boundaries
Doughnut Economics builds directly on the concept of **planetary boundaries**, first introduced by Johan Rockström and colleagues in a landmark 2009 paper in *Nature*, "A safe operating space for humanity." The framework identifies nine boundaries that humanity must respect to keep the planet habitable: climate change, biodiversity loss, ocean acidification, ozone depletion, atmospheric aerosol pollution, freshwater use, biogeochemical flows of nitrogen and phosphorus, land-system change, and the release of novel chemicals. According to the 2025 Doughnut update, six of these nine boundaries are now being exceeded by human activity, and humanity had already moved outside the safe operating space for at least four of them even earlier: climate change, biodiversity loss, land-system change, and the nitrogen and phosphorus imbalance in biogeochemical flows. This acceleration in environmental damage traces back to what researchers call the "great acceleration" of economic growth since the 1950s, which has driven environmental degradation at an unprecedented and unparalleled pace.

- Costa Rica's forest cover fell from around 70% pre-1940 to just 21% by 1987, then recovered to 52% and, by early 2024, to 60% of national territory, driven by a 3.5% petrol tax funding farmer reforestation incentives since 1996.
- Costa Rica now generates 70-75% of its electricity from hydropower, with an electric grid that is effectively 100% renewable.
- A 2020 RAND study found Costa Rica's carbon-neutrality goal both achievable and economically beneficial.
- The Green New Deal exists as a 14-page US congressional resolution in two parts, covering emissions cuts and worker protections.

- The richest 20% of nations (15% of global population) produce over 40% of annual ecological overshoot; the poorest 40% of countries (42% of population) bear over 60% of the world's social shortfall.
