# Valuing People and the Planet: Four Models of Development Beyond GDP

## What it is
Four very different models of development have each tried, in their own way, to put human wellbeing and the planet's limits ahead of pure economic growth. They are the Sarvodaya Shramadana movement in Sri Lanka, the Green New Deal in the United States, Costa Rica's path of sustainable development, and **Doughnut Economics** from the United Kingdom. Each gives a different answer to the same question: if an economy is not simply for growth, what is it for?

## Why it matters
Almost everywhere, GDP growth is still the standard measure of a country's success. Yet it tells us nothing about whether people's basic needs are met, or whether that growth is destroying the natural systems we all depend on. Each of these four models tries, in practice or in theory, to build an economy around meeting people's needs within the planet's limits, instead of around growth for its own sake. Costa Rica in particular offers a real case study, stretching over decades, of what that can look like.

## Sarvodaya Shramadana (Sri Lanka)
Sarvodaya is a Sri Lankan development movement founded by **Dr A.T. Ariyaratne**. It combines Buddhist principles with grassroots community organising. Its main idea is that development should serve the "awakening of all", rather than economic indicators alone. It works through self-help at village level and shared work (*shramadana*, literally "the gift of labour"). It is one of the longest-running grassroots development movements of its kind.

## The Green New Deal (United States)
The Green New Deal, associated with US politician **Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez**, is a proposed plan for fast, large-scale decarbonisation, combined openly with protection for workers whose jobs would be affected by the change. It takes the form of a short, 14-page resolution in Congress, in two parts. One deals with how deep the emissions cuts need to be. The other deals with the people whose livelihoods depend on the industries being transformed. The Green New Deal became central to the Biden administration's climate agenda, although the laws that came out of it involved large concessions to the oil industry along the way.

## Costa Rica's sustainable development model
Costa Rica has made development within a healthy environment an explicit national goal, and has set a target to become carbon neutral. The country banned new oil exploration. It now generates **70-75% of its electricity from hydropower**, and its electricity grid is effectively 100% renewable. It also runs a scheme to buy back petrol and diesel cars, to encourage people to switch to electric. A 2020 RAND study found that Costa Rica's carbon-neutrality goal is achievable and is likely to benefit the country economically.

Forest cover is where the change shows most clearly. In 1940, forests covered around 70% of Costa Rica. Between 1940 and 1983, much of that was cleared to make way for farming, and by 1987 forest cover had fallen to just 21% of the country. In 1996 the government changed course. It brought in strong laws against further deforestation, along with payments to farmers to replant their land, funded partly by a **3.5% tax on petrol**. The policy worked very well. Forest cover recovered to 52% in the following years, and by early 2024 it had reached 60% of the country, which is now home to some of the most biodiverse forest on Earth. Costa Rica is a rare real example of a country growing its economy while reversing environmental damage, rather than speeding it up.

## Doughnut Economics (United Kingdom)
**Doughnut Economics**, developed by economist Kate Raworth, changes the goal of an economy completely. Instead of chasing endless GDP growth, it aims to meet everyone's basic needs while staying within the planet's ecological limits. It is drawn as a doughnut-shaped space between a social foundation (below which people do not have a decent life) and an ecological ceiling (above which the planet's systems become unstable). The **Doughnut Economics Action Lab (DEAL)** launched in September 2020 to help put the model into practice, scaling it down to individual cities, businesses and other organisations, including detailed versions for Amsterdam and Brussels. The model is meant to describe the problem and also offer a theory of change. It diagnoses an unbalanced economy and proposes a route to rebalance it. When companies use it to shape their sustainability strategy, its logic sits close to existing frameworks such as ESG.

A major 2025 update to the framework, published in *Nature*, introduced a revised set of **35 indicators** tracking social deprivation and ecological overshoot from 2000 to 2022. The findings are sobering. Global GDP more than doubled over that period, yet typical progress on reducing human deprivation was modest. It would need to speed up roughly fivefold to meet everyone's basic needs by 2030. At the same time, the growth in ecological overshoot would need to stop immediately, then reverse nearly twice as fast, to keep the Earth system stable by 2050. The split between rich and poor stands out. The richest 20% of nations, home to just 15% of the world's people, cause more than 40% of annual ecological overshoot. The poorest 40% of countries, home to 42% of the world's people, experience more than 60% of the world's social shortfall. The researchers argue that these trends support moving countries away from depending on endless GDP growth, towards economic activity that regenerates nature and shares resources fairly, putting human needs and a healthy planet first, both within and between countries.

The updated research also tests a common economic assumption: that as economies get richer, their environmental impact naturally falls. It finds "no evidence" of the lasting, absolute decoupling of economic growth from environmental impact that this assumption needs. Economies are becoming more efficient, but their total impact on resources keeps rising. The research also confirms that beyond a certain point, more growth does little to improve health, education or happiness. That supports the idea that wellbeing can, in principle, keep improving without continued growth, as long as resources are shared more fairly.

## Planetary boundaries
Doughnut Economics builds on the idea of **planetary boundaries**, first set out by Johan Rockström and colleagues in an influential 2009 paper in *Nature*, "A safe operating space for humanity." The framework names nine boundaries humanity must stay within to keep the planet habitable: climate change, biodiversity loss, ocean acidification, ozone depletion, aerosol pollution in the atmosphere, freshwater use, the nitrogen and phosphorus cycles, changes to land use, and the release of new chemicals. A 2023 update of the framework, led by Katherine Richardson, found that human activity was pushing past six of these nine boundaries. The 2025 Planetary Health Check added a seventh, ocean acidification. Humanity had already left the safe zone for at least four of them even earlier: climate change, biodiversity loss, land-use change, and the imbalance in the nitrogen and phosphorus cycles. Researchers trace this speeding-up of environmental damage back to the "great acceleration" of economic growth since the 1950s, which has degraded the environment at a pace never seen before.

## Summary findings
- Costa Rica's forest cover fell from around 70% of the country in 1940 to just 21% by 1987, then recovered to 52% and, by early 2024, to 60% of the country. Since 1996 the recovery has been driven by a 3.5% petrol tax that pays farmers to replant.
- Costa Rica now generates 70-75% of its electricity from hydropower, and its electricity grid is effectively 100% renewable.
- A 2020 RAND study found Costa Rica's carbon-neutrality goal both achievable and good for the economy.
- The Green New Deal is a 14-page US congressional resolution in two parts, covering emissions cuts and protection for workers.
- The 2025 *Nature* update to Doughnut Economics tracks 35 indicators of social deprivation and ecological overshoot across 2000-2022.
- The richest 20% of nations (15% of the world's population) cause over 40% of annual ecological overshoot. The poorest 40% of countries (42% of the population) bear over 60% of the world's social shortfall.
- Six of the nine planetary boundaries identified by Rockström et al. (2009) were being crossed by human activity in 2023 (Richardson et al.), and seven in 2025 (Planetary Health Check).
- The research finds "no evidence" of lasting absolute decoupling between economic growth and environmental impact.
