# What is Materiality?

## What is it
Most organisations are exposed to a wide range of ESG issues. **Materiality** is the process of working out which of those issues matter to a particular business, and how they affect it. A materiality assessment is a well-established tool for doing this. It helps a company measure its environmental footprint by identifying and sorting the issues that affect its legal compliance and performance, looking at both environmental and social impact, and at what stakeholders and investors care about.

At its simplest, materiality is an accounting rule. Information is material if getting it wrong, or leaving it out of a company's financial statements, could change the decisions of the people reading them. In ESG this is called **single materiality**: ESG factors, such as extreme weather, that threaten or create opportunities for a business's own profits. Single materiality does not tell you how "green" a company is. It only tells you how exposed its earnings may be to ESG-related risks. Under an ESG Risk Ratings method, an issue counts as material if including it in, or leaving it out of, a company's financial reporting is likely to influence a reasonable investor's decisions.

## Why it matters
Materiality assessments help businesses set sustainability goals, decide where to put resources, manage risk and choose which issues to deal with first. For the curious reader, materiality explains why two companies' sustainability reports can look so different. Each has usually gone through its own process of deciding what counts as material to it.

## Double materiality
**Double materiality** goes further than single materiality. It asks companies to look outwards as well as inwards, sometimes called an "inside-out and outside-in" view. Companies consider how ESG issues affect their profits, and also how their own decisions and operations affect the world around them. Europe has taken up the idea that both directions matter, but it has made far less progress in the United States. The underlying question, what companies should have to report and who decides, is still disputed.

Almost ten years ago, the EU began requiring companies to report non-financial information, to make them more accountable on ESG issues. It was the first time disclosure rules included the idea of double materiality. Large gaps soon appeared in the quality and consistency of the reporting, and companies and regulators disagreed on how the rules should be applied. The EU's rewritten rulebook, the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (**CSRD**), gives companies clearer requirements and covers many more businesses. It has been phased in across the 27 EU member states from 2024. See [What is CSRD?](../../legislation-policy/What%20is%20CSRD%3F.md) for detail on who is covered and when.

## Benefits of an ESG materiality assessment
Businesses today need to be clear about their ESG goals, policies and performance. A materiality assessment is a practical way to work out which ESG factors could affect the business, and how the business affects the world around it. Whatever a company's size, stage or resources, materiality is worth treating as a core part of decision-making for long-term environmental action. It shows where an organisation is adding to or taking away from value for society, and helps it put its resources where they will do most good.

## How to conduct a materiality assessment
A materiality assessment usually follows four steps:

1. Identify material topics and understand the context. Define the organisation's approach, purpose and scope. The reporting framework chosen will shape this step.
2. Research and list material issues. Build a full list of issues relevant to the organisation's location, industry and business, then map out the stakeholders up and down the supply chain.
3. Engage stakeholders. Make sure a wide range of voices is included. Then draw up and carry out an engagement plan, for example through focus groups, questionnaires or interviews, to gather what stakeholders see as most relevant.
4. Narrow, visualise and communicate. Cut the list down to the issues that really matter to the business, then present the resulting material issues clearly to stakeholders.

Materiality is not a one-off exercise. Research on the topic by Garst and colleagues (see Sources) describes it as more art than science. One person interviewed for the study described how a single company's framework had changed over time: "we now have 19 issues, and 169 topics underlie those issues. Because some of the topics have now become an issue such as 'biodiversity'. Previously, that was at the topic level, but it has been coming up on the agenda. [The topic] has been promoted [to issue]. And some issues have now become topics within issues and some topics merged, according to changes in the landscape."
