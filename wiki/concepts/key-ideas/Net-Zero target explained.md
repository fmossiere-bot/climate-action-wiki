# Net-Zero Target Explained

## What is it
**Net zero** means cutting greenhouse gas emissions as close to zero as possible, with whatever remains re-absorbed from the atmosphere, chiefly by oceans and forests. More precisely, it describes a state in which the greenhouse gases going into the atmosphere are balanced by removals out of it: all human-caused emissions are compensated by human-caused carbon removals.

Net zero is not the same as zero. Cutting all emissions entirely is unlikely, even though a handful of countries have pledged exactly that. Alongside deep, widespread emissions cuts, the world will also need to scale up removals. Net zero is also distinct from related terms such as carbon neutral, climate neutral and GHG neutral, which carry narrower or differently scoped meanings; see the [Oxford explainer](https://netzeroclimate.org/what-is-net-zero-2/) for the detailed distinctions. Net zero is now the agreed framework underpinning global climate ambition.

## Why it matters
The IPCC has concluded that the world needs to reach net-zero CO2 by 2050 to remain consistent with limiting warming to 1.5°C. Staying under 1.5°C means keeping the total amount of CO2 accumulating in the atmosphere under a fixed ceiling, known as the carbon budget. Each year the world currently emits around 50 gigatonnes (Gt) of CO2 equivalent, a figure explored further in the carbon budget section below.

## How the world gets to net zero
The nearer-term milestone on the way to net zero is a 45% cut in global greenhouse gas emissions compared with 2010 levels. Total GHG emissions today remain above 50 Gt CO2e, while in 2010 they stood at around 51 Gt CO2e, putting the 2030 target at roughly 28 Gt CO2e.

Net zero is reached by decarbonising most sectors, especially energy, while simultaneously scaling up the ability to remove carbon from the atmosphere. It is characterised by balancing the carbon flowing into the atmosphere against the carbon flowing out of it, through a combination of emissions reductions and carbon removals.

To stabilise global temperatures over the long term, the world needs to go further still, towards what is called **durable net zero**. This builds on the standard concept of net zero, which only accounts for carbon flows into and out of the atmosphere, and requires net human-caused flows between the lithosphere, the atmosphere, and the land and ocean biosphere to equal zero. That demands further emissions cuts and a further scale-up of carbon removals. The International Energy Agency's updated *Net Zero Roadmap* sets out the sector-by-sector milestones the energy system needs to hit on the way there.

## Are we on track?
No. According to the UN Environment Programme's *Emissions Gap Report*, the world remains far off course, and additional pledges plus stronger enforcement of existing ones will not close the gap on their own. (See the [Emission Gap Report 2024 - UNEP](Emission%20Gap%20Report%202024%20-%20UNEP.md) for the most recent assessment of how far behind the world remains.)

## Will warming stop the moment we reach net zero?
Yes, and according to [Carbon Brief](https://www.carbonbrief.org/explainer-will-global-warming-stop-as-soon-as-net-zero-emissions-are-reached/), almost immediately. The net-zero pledge requires removing all the carbon added to the atmosphere, through long-term removal techniques such as biomass energy with carbon capture and geological storage. Every added tonne of greenhouse gas is, in principle, matched by removal, and the existing natural carbon sink will continue removing around 5.5 GtC per year from the atmosphere regardless. Net-zero emissions therefore lead to a continuously decreasing atmospheric CO2 concentration, because ocean and land sinks keep absorbing roughly 5.5 GtC a year even once new emissions stop: 0 GtC minus 5.5 GtC leaves -5.5 GtC, or a decline of about 2 parts per million (ppm) a year. In terms of temperature, this decline is offset by the oceans, so temperature change is expected to level off too.

The picture is more nuanced underneath that headline. The Earth is currently out of thermal equilibrium: more energy from the sun is being trapped by greenhouse gases than is escaping back to space, and over 90% of that extra heat is going into warming the oceans. As the oceans keep warming, they take up less heat from the atmosphere, which pushes global average surface temperatures up further. At the same time, land and ocean sinks are absorbing roughly half of the CO2 humans emit each year; if emissions fall to zero, those sinks keep drawing down some of the CO2 already emitted, quickly at first and then more slowly as the system moves towards a new equilibrium, which further reduces atmospheric CO2 and the warming it causes.

If emissions were instead held at a constant, non-zero level (rather than falling to zero), warming would keep rising, because the system would not yet be at equilibrium; only once the Earth is emitting as much energy as it receives would that rise level off. Modelling of these "constant concentration" scenarios has found that there is additional warming already "in the pipeline" as the oceans slowly catch up to atmospheric temperature and the planet returns to radiative equilibrium, the point at which the energy the Earth absorbs from the sun equals the energy it radiates back to space. Estimates of that additional locked-in warming, if concentrations were held steady rather than cut to net zero, range from around [0.4°C](https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo813) to [0.5°C](https://static.scientificamerican.com/sciam/assets/media/pdf/hansen.pdf) over the following few centuries.

## The global carbon cycle and carbon budget
The global carbon cycle describes carbon held in stocks, in the air, land and water, for varying lengths of time, with fluxes moving carbon from one stock to another. A stock that emits carbon into the atmosphere is a **source**; one that draws carbon down is a **sink**. Today, around 55% of emitted carbon is removed by sinks each year, roughly 5.5 GtC, leaving a net addition of about 5.2 GtC to the existing atmospheric stock. Full detail on the 2022 carbon accounts is available from the [Global Carbon Project](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/14/4811/2022/).

Carbon and CO2 are not the same thing, though they are often used interchangeably: 1 gram of carbon (C) is equivalent to 3.67 grams of CO2. The overall carbon cycle sinks around 20 Gt of CO2e every year. Carbon persists in the atmosphere for hundreds of years, so as more is added annually, atmospheric concentration keeps climbing; that concentration, alongside other greenhouse gases, is what drives the temperature increase, because higher concentrations trap and reflect back more of the heat that would otherwise escape to space.

The standard conversion between concentration and mass is 1 ppm equals 2.13 GtC. At a current atmospheric concentration of around 412 ppm, that implies a stock of roughly 878 GtC.

Because scientists know how much further concentration can rise before it drives a given amount of extra warming, it is possible to calculate how much more net-positive carbon the world can still add to the atmosphere before breaching a given temperature limit. This remaining allowance is the **carbon budget**. Current estimates put it at under 420 Gt of CO2 over the next decade, though some researchers argue the true figure is considerably lower, with estimates as low as 200 Gt also in circulation.
