# Net-Zero Target Explained

## What is it
**Net zero** means cutting greenhouse gas emissions as close to zero as possible, and taking whatever is left back out of the atmosphere, mainly through oceans and forests. Put more precisely, it is a state where the greenhouse gases going into the atmosphere are balanced by the gases taken out of it. All the emissions caused by humans are matched by carbon removals caused by humans.

Net zero is not the same as zero. Stopping every last emission is unlikely, even though a handful of countries have pledged to do exactly that. So as well as deep cuts across the board, the world will also need to remove much more carbon. Net zero is also different from related terms such as carbon neutral, climate neutral and GHG neutral, which have narrower or differently defined meanings. The [Oxford explainer](https://netzeroclimate.org/what-is-net-zero-2/) sets out the differences in detail. Net zero is now the agreed framework behind global climate goals.

## Why it matters
The IPCC has concluded that the world needs to reach **net-zero CO2 by 2050** to keep warming to 1.5°C. Staying under 1.5°C means keeping the total amount of CO2 building up in the atmosphere below a fixed ceiling, known as the carbon budget. At the moment the world emits around 50 gigatonnes (Gt, billion tonnes) of CO2 equivalent each year. The carbon budget section below looks at this in more detail.

## How the world gets to net zero
The milestone on the way to net zero is a **45% cut in global greenhouse gas emissions** by 2030, compared with 2010 levels. Total emissions today are still above 50 Gt CO2e. In 2010 they were around 51 Gt CO2e, which puts the 2030 target at roughly 28 Gt CO2e.

Getting to net zero means decarbonising most sectors, energy above all, while building up the ability to take carbon out of the atmosphere. The aim is to balance the carbon flowing into the atmosphere with the carbon flowing out, through a mix of emissions cuts and carbon removals.

To keep global temperatures stable over the long term, the world needs to go further, to what is called **durable net zero**. Ordinary net zero only counts carbon moving in and out of the atmosphere. Durable net zero also requires the net human-caused flows between the rocks of the Earth's crust (the lithosphere), the atmosphere, and life on land and in the ocean to add up to zero. That means deeper emissions cuts and more carbon removal again. The International Energy Agency's updated *Net Zero Roadmap* sets out the milestones each part of the energy system needs to reach on the way.

## Are we on track?
No. The UN Environment Programme's *Emissions Gap Report* finds the world is still far off course. New pledges, and stronger enforcement of the ones already made, will not close the gap on their own. (See [Emission Gap Report 2024 - UNEP](Emission%20Gap%20Report%202024%20-%20UNEP.md) for the latest assessment of how far behind we are.)

## Will warming stop the moment we reach net zero?
Yes, and according to [Carbon Brief](https://www.carbonbrief.org/explainer-will-global-warming-stop-as-soon-as-net-zero-emissions-are-reached/), almost straight away. The net-zero pledge means removing all the carbon we add to the atmosphere, through long-term removal methods such as burning biomass for energy and capturing the carbon to store underground. In principle, every extra tonne of greenhouse gas is matched by a tonne removed. On top of that, the natural carbon sinks will keep removing around **5.5 GtC per year** from the atmosphere. So at net zero the amount of CO2 in the atmosphere keeps falling, because the ocean and land keep absorbing roughly 5.5 GtC a year even once new emissions stop. 0 GtC added minus 5.5 GtC absorbed gives -5.5 GtC, a fall of about 2 parts per million (ppm) a year. The oceans balance out the effect of this fall on temperature, so temperature change is expected to level off too.

Underneath that simple answer, things are more complicated. The Earth is currently out of thermal balance: greenhouse gases are trapping more of the sun's energy than escapes back to space, and over 90% of that extra heat is going into the oceans. As the oceans warm, they take up less heat from the atmosphere, which pushes average surface temperatures higher. At the same time, land and ocean sinks absorb roughly half of the CO2 humans emit each year. If emissions fall to zero, those sinks keep drawing down some of the CO2 already emitted, quickly at first and then more slowly as the system settles into a new balance. That lowers atmospheric CO2, and the warming it causes, a little further.

If emissions were instead held at a steady level above zero, warming would keep rising, because the system would still be out of balance. It would only level off once the Earth was giving off as much energy as it receives. Models of these "constant concentration" scenarios show that some extra warming is already "in the pipeline". It comes as the oceans slowly catch up with the atmosphere and the planet returns to radiative equilibrium, the point where the energy the Earth absorbs from the sun equals the energy it sends back to space. If concentrations were held steady rather than cut to net zero, estimates of this locked-in warming range from around [0.4°C](https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo813) to [0.5°C](https://static.scientificamerican.com/sciam/assets/media/pdf/hansen.pdf) over the following few centuries.

## The global carbon cycle and carbon budget
The global carbon cycle describes how carbon is stored in the air, on land and in water for different lengths of time, and how it moves from one store to another. A store that releases carbon into the atmosphere is a **source**. One that takes carbon out is a **sink**. Today, sinks remove around 55% of the carbon we emit each year, roughly 5.5 GtC, which leaves a net addition of about 5.2 GtC to what is already in the atmosphere. The full 2022 carbon accounts are available from the [Global Carbon Project](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/14/4811/2022/).

Carbon and CO2 are not the same thing, although people often use the words as if they were. **1 gram of carbon (C) equals 3.67 grams of CO2.** The carbon cycle's sinks take up around 20 Gt of CO2e every year. Carbon stays in the atmosphere for hundreds of years, so as more is added each year, the concentration keeps rising. That concentration, together with other greenhouse gases, is what drives warming, because more of these gases trap and send back more of the heat that would otherwise escape to space.

The standard conversion between concentration and mass is 1 ppm = 2.13 GtC. With around 412 ppm in the atmosphere today, that means a stock of roughly 878 GtC.

Scientists know how much further the concentration can rise before it causes a given amount of extra warming. So they can work out how much more carbon, net, the world can still add before it passes a given temperature limit. This remaining allowance is the **carbon budget**. The budget is a total, not a yearly amount. Estimates from around 2022 put it at under 420 Gt of CO2 in total, which at the rate the world was emitting would have run out in about a decade, though some researchers argued the real figure was much lower, with estimates as low as 200 Gt. The budget has shrunk quickly since. The latest scientific update put it at about 130 Gt of CO2 from the start of 2025 for a 50% chance of staying under 1.5°C, a little more than three years of emissions at current rates.
