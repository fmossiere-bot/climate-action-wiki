# Climate Politics and Climate Justice: Who Is Responsible, and Who Pays

## What it is
This page looks at who is actually responsible for the climate crisis, who should be taking responsibility, and to what extent, covering the roles of national governments, corporations, communities, climate justice movements, and individuals. **At its heart is a question of fairness: some countries, companies and individuals have contributed vastly more to the problem than others, yet the costs of a warming world are falling hardest on those who contributed least.**

## Why it matters
Global climate negotiations, corporate net-zero pledges and household-level choices all sit on top of unresolved arguments about who should cut emissions, who should pay for damage already done, and who gets a say in the decisions. Understanding these debates helps make sense of why international climate summits repeatedly fall short of what the science demands, and what "climate justice" actually means in practice rather than as a slogan.

## The Paris Agreement and the UN process
The **UNFCCC** (United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change) is the overarching framework for international climate regulation. It sets legally binding limits but has no enforcement mechanism. Parties to the convention meet every year at the Conference of the Parties (COP) to review progress. COP21, held in Paris in 2015, produced the landmark **Paris Agreement**, under which countries submit their own climate plans, known as **Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs)**, to the UNFCCC. Starting in 2024, countries began reporting transparently on their progress against these plans. The Agreement's headline goal is to keep warming well below 2°C, with an aspirational goal of 1.5°C.

The world is doing better than 20 years ago, when it was heading for around 4°C of warming, but is still far from on track. Fossil fuel production is expected to be around 110% higher in 2030 than is consistent with 1.5°C, and 69% higher than is consistent with 2°C, according to a 2023 UN Environment Programme report. Ideally, global emissions would already be falling by 7 to 8% a year, and that required rate of cut grows every year action is delayed. Some scientists, including James Hansen, argue the climate may in fact be more sensitive to emissions than previously thought, meaning even more drastic and faster action is needed.

At **COP27**, held in Egypt in November 2022, an assessment of 40 tracked climate indicators found none fully on track: 27 were heading in the right direction but well off pace, and 5 were moving in the wrong direction entirely. Two outcomes stood out as wins: acknowledgement that loss and damage from climate change is now unavoidable and that funds need to flow to poorer countries to address it, and acknowledgement that tackling climate change requires rethinking the global financial system. The disappointment was that delegates arrived hoping for a push on implementation but left without additional targets.

**COP28**, at the end of 2023, was a genuinely mixed outcome. On the positive side, countries formally established a "loss and damage" fund, channelling money from wealthy nations most responsible for climate change to poorer countries bearing the brunt of its impacts, an idea agreed in principle at COP27 but only worked out in detail in Dubai. On the negative side, the final agreement called only for "transitioning away from fossil fuels... in a just, orderly and equitable manner," a weaker formulation than many wanted; Samoan negotiator Anne Rasmussen said "the process has failed us," noting that dozens of island nations were not even in the room when the deal passed, and the Alliance of Small Island States said the nonbinding text contained "a litany of loopholes." The summit was further marred by reports that its president, Dr Sultan Ahmed Al-Jaber, who simultaneously heads the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company, had denied the science behind phasing out fossil fuels, and that the host nation had planned to strike oil deals with other countries on the sidelines. More than 2,400 people from the oil and gas industry attended.

## Defining politics, policy and justice
Three terms recur throughout climate debates and are worth defining precisely. **Politics** means the exercise and operation of power, the capacity to direct or influence the behaviour of others or the course of events. A 2021 review of the research literature found that "a common thread that emerges... is the central role of power, manifest in many forms, from a dogmatic political-economic hegemony and influential vested interests to narrow techno-economic mindsets and ideologies of control," after researchers asked why global emissions kept rising despite decades of international meetings and frameworks. Politics operates both materially, through the giving and withholding of money, and ideologically: adopting one way of thinking about how climate change should be solved always excludes other ways of framing the same problem.

**Policy**, per the Oxford Dictionary, is "a course or principle of action adopted or proposed by a government, party, business, or individual." In climate terms, government policies function much like laws, though they are often simply directives to other bodies, such as electricity utilities, to act a certain way. Every policy carries costs and benefits that are distributed unevenly across society: there are always equity questions embedded in any policy choice about who bears the costs and who receives the benefits, and there is no such thing as a policy with no losers, however often analysts claim to have found a "win-win."

**Justice**, in the climate context, is concerned most deeply with fairness. It touches on equality, human rights and collective rights, questions of who is most responsible for the problem, who suffers its impacts disproportionately, and who should pay for both mitigation and adaptation.

## Who is responsible: emissions, fairness and net-zero pledges
There are three common ways to divide up responsibility for emissions: by country's current annual emissions, by country's cumulative historical emissions, or on a per-person basis. Each tells a different story about fairness.

Looking at **emissions per capita**, all high-income countries emit more than their population's fair share, while all low-income countries emit less. Most lower-middle-income countries emit below their fair share, and upper-middle-income countries are mixed. The United States emits more than three times its population's fair share. China emits significantly more than its population share (29% of emissions versus 19% of population). India emits significantly less than its share (7% of emissions versus 18% of population). Brazil emits just over half its population share (1.5% of emissions versus 2.8% of population).

The principle of **Common But Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities (CBDR-RC)**, agreed at the 1992 Rio conference and supported by the UNFCCC, holds that the more industrialised a country, the more it has contributed to climate change, and that emissions targets should reflect that history. Industrialised countries have contributed 92% of the world's excess emissions beyond what would be a fair per-capita share. This principle is now better understood through the "loss and damage" debate, where some countries' losses vastly exceed others'. Dr Saleemul Huq, a lead author of IPCC reports and a leading climate adaptation expert, has been a prominent voice pressing this argument, and the finance figures involved have grown from an initial $100 billion loan target towards calls for close to $6 trillion by 2030.

It is not simply the case that addressing poverty and cutting emissions are in tension. Research by Jarmo Kikstra found that eradicating poverty, including providing the energy needed for food, housing, cooling and heating for the world's poorest, would add less than a 1% increase to global emissions.

On overall progress, the world is doing better than it once was, but is not on track for 1.5°C; current policy most likely puts the world on a path to around 2.5-2.9°C of warming, with a path to around 2.1°C visible if all new pledges are actually delivered. Per the United Nations, **net-zero** means "not adding new emissions to the atmosphere. Emissions will continue, but will be balanced by absorbing an equivalent amount from the atmosphere," achieved through two components: reducing emissions as close to zero as possible, and balancing remaining emissions through carbon removal, whether through natural means like tree planting or technology like direct air capture. Some 151 out of 202 countries have at least proposed or pledged net-zero commitments, per Net Zero Tracker, a collaboration between the University of North Carolina-Chapel Hill, the University of Oxford, and the nonprofits NewClimate Institute and the Energy & Climate Intelligence Unit. These pledges cover 89% of the world's population and 88% of global emissions. Carbon Brief has warned that folding emissions reductions and negative emissions together into a single "net-zero" target risks delaying actual emissions cuts and distracting from the priorities that matter most now. "Carbon neutrality" is often used interchangeably with "net-zero," despite the two terms sometimes meaning different things to different people, itself a small illustration of how definitional confusion complicates this whole debate.

## Corporate responsibility: fossil fuel companies
Fossil fuel companies are, in a real sense, the elephant in the room of climate politics: their business model depends on continued extraction and combustion of the product driving the crisis, and their lobbying influence over international negotiations, visible for instance in the more than 2,400 oil and gas attendees at COP28, shapes what governments are able to agree to. See [Facts About the Fossil Fuel Industry](FACTS About Fossil Fuel (Oil and Gaz) Industry.md) for detail on the scale of that industry's impact.

## Citizen power and the climate justice movement
Regular people are not powerless in this system. Research suggests that sustained political action, meaning strikes, protests and similar activism, by just 3.5% of a population is enough to force governmental change, and separate research finds that social movements have a measurable impact on reducing greenhouse gas emissions.

**Climate litigation** has become a significant lever. There were 1,587 documented climate court cases between 1986 and 2020, more than three-quarters of them in the United States. Examples include a group of children suing US states and fossil fuel companies (the first major case of its kind was won in Montana), an Ecuadorian tribe winning a 2019 case to save 500,000 acres of ancestral jungle from an oil company, a French court ordering the French government to do more on climate action, and a German judge travelling to visit a lake in Peru to assess the harm allegedly caused by Germany's largest power provider. The pace has continued: The Guardian documented 13 significant climate litigation outcomes in 2025 alone.

**Individual actions matter**, and most readers of a page like this are likely in the global top 1-10% of emitters. Country-level averages obscure real differences: Saudi Arabia's per-capita figure, for example, is heavily skewed upward by its role as an oil producer. A more consumer-focused view shows income is very strongly correlated with personal emissions, and this holds regardless of country. The UNEP Emissions Gap Report has calculated a fair global "carbon budget", the fixed amount of CO2-equivalent that can be emitted while keeping warming at a safe level, but in practice that budget is not shared equally; wherever you are, your share of it tracks your income.

The **"Polluting Elite"** campaign, drawing on research from the Stockholm Environment Institute and Oxfam, found that the richest 1% of people worldwide emit as much carbon pollution as the poorest 5 billion people combined. This means most of humanity will bear the consequences of a crisis driven disproportionately by a small number of people living unnecessarily lavish lifestyles, a dynamic that risks feeding a dangerous spiral of rising inequality and rising pollution together. Oxfam and 20 partner organisations, backed by voices including Sandrine Dixson-Declève of the Club of Rome and Hans Stegeman, chief economist of Triodos Bank, have called for taxing the wealthy and transforming a system built on greed and overconsumption into a fairer one.

Related research on social tipping points, sometimes called the "25% Revolution," finds that between 10% and 30% of a population committing to a new norm can be enough to shift the majority; the IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report similarly notes that shifting societal norms may only require 10-30% of individuals to be deeply committed, a finding echoed by Damon Centola's research showing that once roughly 25% of a group adopts a new belief or behaviour, the majority tends to follow.

## An example of national policy: France's building efficiency rules
France offers a concrete illustration of what ambitious climate policy looks like when it meets real-world friction. Landlords across the country are grappling with new restrictions on renting out poorly insulated homes, phased in gradually from 2023, intended to drive renovation of roughly two-thirds of France's 37 million homes by 2050. President Emmanuel Macron's government argues the intervention is necessary because buildings account for almost half of French energy consumption and a third of national carbon emissions, two-thirds of which comes from homes specifically; experts consider the scale of this intervention unique in Europe.

Under the policy, properties must be rated for energy efficiency from A (best) to G (worst) before being sold or rented. It combines carrot and stick: poorly insulated homes are gradually excluded from the rental market over a decade, while public money is available to help pay for insulation, new windows or upgraded heating. Landlords of F- and G-rated homes may no longer raise rents, and the worst G-rated properties can no longer be newly rented at all. By 2028, 5.2 million F- and G-rated homes, 17% of the total housing stock, will become ineligible for rental; by 2034, all E- and D-rated properties will also be excluded, more than half of all French homes. The policy is a useful case study in how equity issues are unavoidable even in well-intentioned climate policy, since landlords facing real financial strain are the ones being asked to fund a national decarbonisation goal.

## Summary findings
- The Paris Agreement, agreed at COP21 in 2015, has been signed by 195 countries, but has no global enforcement mechanism for the Nationally Determined Contributions countries submit.
- Fossil fuel production is on track to be 110% higher than 1.5°C-consistent levels by 2030, per a 2023 UNEP report.
- Current pledges put the world on a path to roughly 2.1-2.9°C of warming, still far above the 1.5°C target.
- Industrialised countries have contributed 92% of the world's excess emissions relative to a fair per-capita share.
- The USA emits more than three times its fair per-capita share; China emits 29% of global emissions against 19% of population; India emits 7% against 18% of population.
- 151 of 202 countries have proposed or pledged net-zero targets, covering 89% of world population and 88% of emissions (Net Zero Tracker).
- The richest 1% of people emit as much carbon pollution as the poorest 5 billion combined (Stockholm Environment Institute and Oxfam).
- Sustained protest by just 3.5% of a population has historically been enough to force government change.
- 1,587 climate court cases were documented between 1986 and 2020, over three-quarters of them in the US.
