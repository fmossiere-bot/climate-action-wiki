# Climate Politics and Climate Justice: Who Is Responsible, and Who Pays

## What it is
This page looks at who is responsible for the climate crisis, who should be taking responsibility, and how much. It covers national governments, companies, communities, climate justice movements and individuals. At its heart is a question of fairness. Some countries, companies and people have contributed far more to the problem than others, yet **the costs of a warming world fall hardest on those who contributed least**.

## Why it matters
Global climate talks, company net-zero pledges and household choices all rest on unsettled arguments: who should cut emissions, who should pay for the damage already done, and who gets a say. Understanding these debates helps explain why international climate summits keep falling short of what the science asks for, and what "climate justice" means in practice rather than as a slogan.

## The Paris Agreement and the UN process
The **UNFCCC** (United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change) is the overall framework for international climate rules. It sets shared goals but no legally binding emission limits for individual countries, and has no way to enforce them. The parties to the convention meet every year at the Conference of the Parties (COP) to review progress. COP21, held in Paris in 2015, produced the Paris Agreement. Under it, countries send the UNFCCC their own climate plans, known as Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs). From 2024, countries began reporting openly on their progress against these plans. The Agreement's main goal is to keep warming well below 2°C, and to aim for 1.5°C.

The world is doing better than 20 years ago, when it was heading for around 4°C of warming, but it is still far from on track. According to a 2023 UN Environment Programme report, fossil fuel production is expected to be around 110% higher in 2030 than is consistent with 1.5°C, and 69% higher than is consistent with 2°C. Ideally, global emissions would already be falling by 7 to 8% a year, and that required rate of cut grows each year action is delayed. Some scientists, including James Hansen, argue that the climate may be more sensitive to emissions than previously thought, which would mean even faster and deeper action is needed.

At **COP27**, held in Egypt in November 2022, an assessment of 40 tracked climate indicators found none fully on track. 27 were heading in the right direction but much too slowly, and 5 were moving the wrong way. Two outcomes counted as wins. Countries acknowledged that loss and damage from climate change can no longer be avoided and that money needs to flow to poorer countries to deal with it. They also acknowledged that tackling climate change means rethinking the global financial system. The disappointment was that delegates arrived hoping for a push on putting plans into practice, and left without any new targets.

**COP28**, at the end of 2023, had a mixed outcome. On the positive side, countries formally set up a "loss and damage" fund, which channels money from the wealthy nations most responsible for climate change to the poorer countries bearing the brunt of it. The idea had been agreed in principle at COP27 but was only worked out in detail in Dubai. On the negative side, the final agreement called only for "transitioning away from fossil fuels... in a just, orderly and equitable manner," weaker wording than many wanted. Samoan negotiator Anne Rasmussen said "the process has failed us," noting that dozens of island nations were not even in the room when the deal passed. The Alliance of Small Island States said the non-binding text contained "a litany of loopholes." The summit was also clouded by reports that its president, Dr Sultan Ahmed Al-Jaber, who also heads the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company, had denied the science behind phasing out fossil fuels, and that the host nation had planned to strike oil deals with other countries on the sidelines. More than 2,400 people from the oil and gas industry attended.

## Defining politics, policy and justice
Three terms come up again and again in climate debates, and it helps to define them. **Politics** means the exercise of power: the ability to direct or influence what others do or how events unfold. Researchers asked why global emissions kept rising despite decades of international meetings and frameworks. A 2021 review of the research found that "a common thread that emerges... is the central role of power, manifest in many forms, from a dogmatic political-economic hegemony and influential vested interests to narrow techno-economic mindsets and ideologies of control." Politics works both through money, by giving or withholding it, and through ideas: choosing one way of thinking about how to solve climate change always shuts out other ways of framing the same problem.

**Policy**, in the Oxford Dictionary's words, is "a course or principle of action adopted or proposed by a government, party, business, or individual." In climate terms, government policies work much like laws, though they are often simply instructions to other bodies, such as electricity companies, to act in a certain way. Every policy has costs and benefits that land unevenly across society. There are always fairness questions about who pays and who gains, and there is no such thing as a policy with no losers, however often analysts claim to have found a "win-win."

**Justice**, in the climate context, is mostly about fairness. It covers equality, human rights and collective rights: who is most responsible for the problem, who suffers most from its impacts, and who should pay for both cutting emissions and adapting.

## Who is responsible: emissions, fairness and net-zero pledges
There are three common ways to share out responsibility for emissions: by a country's current yearly emissions, by its total historical emissions, or per person. Each tells a different story about fairness.

Looking at **emissions per person**, every high-income country emits more than its population's fair share, and every low-income country emits less. Most lower-middle-income countries emit below their fair share, and upper-middle-income countries are mixed. The United States emits more than three times its population's fair share. China emits well above its population share (29% of emissions against 19% of population). India emits well below its share (7% of emissions against 18% of population). Brazil emits just over half its population share (1.5% of emissions against 2.8% of population).

The principle of **Common But Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities (CBDR-RC)** was agreed at the 1992 Rio conference and is supported by the UNFCCC. It holds that the more industrialised a country is, the more it has contributed to climate change, and that emissions targets should reflect that history. Industrialised countries have produced 92% of the world's excess emissions, meaning emissions beyond a fair per-person share. The "loss and damage" debate has brought this principle into sharper focus, because some countries' losses are far greater than others'. Dr Saleemul Huq, a lead author of IPCC reports and a leading expert on climate adaptation, was a prominent voice for this argument. The sums involved have grown from an initial target of $100 billion a year in climate finance (a mix of grants and loans) towards calls for close to $6 trillion by 2030.

Ending poverty and cutting emissions are not simply at odds. Research by Jarmo Kikstra found that eradicating poverty, including providing the energy the world's poorest need for food, housing, cooling and heating, would raise global emissions by less than 1%.

On overall progress, the world is doing better than it once was but is not on track for 1.5°C. Current policies most likely put it on a path to around 2.5-2.9°C of warming. If all new pledges were actually delivered, a path to around 2.1°C comes into view. The United Nations defines **net-zero** as "not adding new emissions to the atmosphere. Emissions will continue, but will be balanced by absorbing an equivalent amount from the atmosphere." It has two parts: cutting emissions as close to zero as possible, and balancing what is left through carbon removal, either natural, such as planting trees, or technological, such as direct air capture. Some 151 out of 202 countries have at least proposed or pledged net-zero targets, according to Net Zero Tracker, a joint project of the University of North Carolina-Chapel Hill, the University of Oxford, and the non-profits NewClimate Institute and the Energy & Climate Intelligence Unit. These pledges cover 89% of the world's population and 88% of global emissions. Carbon Brief has warned that folding emissions cuts and negative emissions into one "net-zero" target risks delaying real cuts and distracting from what matters most now. "Carbon neutrality" is often used as if it meant the same as "net-zero", though the two can mean different things to different people, a small example of how confusion over definitions complicates the whole debate.

## Corporate responsibility: fossil fuel companies
Fossil fuel companies are the elephant in the room of climate politics. Their business depends on continuing to extract and burn the product that drives the crisis. Their lobbying influence over international talks, visible in the more than 2,400 oil and gas attendees at COP28, shapes what governments are able to agree. See [Facts About the Fossil Fuel Industry](../../solutions/energy-transition/FACTS%20About%20Fossil%20Fuel%20%28Oil%20and%20Gaz%29%20Industry.md) for more on the scale of that industry's impact.

## Citizen power and the climate justice movement
Ordinary people are not powerless in this system. Research suggests that sustained political action, such as strikes and protests, by just **3.5% of a population** is enough to force a government to change. Separate research finds that social movements measurably reduce greenhouse gas emissions.

**Climate litigation** has become an important lever. There were 1,587 documented climate court cases between 1986 and 2020, more than three-quarters of them in the United States. Examples include a group of children suing US states and fossil fuel companies (the first major case of its kind was won in Montana), an Ecuadorian tribe winning a 2019 case to save 500,000 acres of ancestral jungle from an oil company, a French court ordering the French government to do more on climate, and a German judge travelling to a lake in Peru to assess harm allegedly caused by Germany's largest power company. The pace has continued: The Guardian documented 13 significant climate litigation outcomes in 2025 alone.

Individual actions matter too, and most readers of a page like this are probably among the world's top 1-10% of emitters. Country averages hide real differences. Saudi Arabia's per-person figure, for example, is pushed up by its role as an oil producer. Looking at what people consume instead shows that **income is very strongly linked to personal emissions**, whatever the country. The UNEP Emissions Gap Report has calculated a fair global "carbon budget", the fixed amount of CO2-equivalent that can be emitted while keeping warming at a safe level. In practice that budget is not shared equally. Wherever you live, your share of it follows your income.

The **"Polluting Elite"** campaign, drawing on research from the Stockholm Environment Institute and Oxfam, found that the richest 1% of people worldwide emit as much carbon pollution as the poorest 5 billion people combined. Most of humanity will bear the consequences of a crisis driven largely by a small number of people living needlessly lavish lives, and that risks a dangerous spiral in which inequality and pollution rise together. Oxfam and 20 partner organisations, backed by voices including Sandrine Dixson-Declève of the Club of Rome and Hans Stegeman, chief economist of Triodos Bank, have called for taxing the wealthy and turning a system built on greed and overconsumption into a fairer one.

Related research on social tipping points, sometimes called the "25% Revolution", finds that between 10% and 30% of a population committing to a new norm can be enough to shift the majority. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report likewise notes that shifting a society's norms may need only 10-30% of people to be deeply committed. Damon Centola's research points the same way: once roughly 25% of a group adopts a new belief or behaviour, the majority tends to follow.

## An example of national policy: France's building efficiency rules
<!-- tag: france -->
France shows what ambitious climate policy looks like when it meets real-world friction. Landlords across the country are dealing with new limits on renting out poorly insulated homes, phased in gradually from 2023. The aim is to renovate roughly two-thirds of France's 37 million homes by 2050. President Emmanuel Macron's government argues the measure is needed because buildings account for almost half of French energy use and a third of national carbon emissions, two-thirds of which come from homes. Experts consider an intervention on this scale unique in Europe.

Under the policy, properties must be rated for energy efficiency from A (best) to G (worst) before being sold or rented. It mixes carrot and stick. Poorly insulated homes are gradually removed from the rental market over a decade, while public money helps pay for insulation, new windows or better heating. Landlords of F- and G-rated homes can no longer raise rents, and the worst G-rated properties can no longer be newly let at all. By 2028, 5.2 million F- and G-rated homes, 17% of all housing, will no longer be allowed on the rental market. By 2034, all E- and D-rated properties will be excluded too, which is more than half of all French homes. It is a useful example of how fairness issues come up even in well-meant climate policy, because landlords under real financial strain are the ones being asked to pay for a national decarbonisation goal.

## Summary findings
- The Paris Agreement, agreed at COP21 in 2015, has been signed by 195 countries, but has no global way to enforce the Nationally Determined Contributions countries submit.
- Fossil fuel production is on track to be 110% higher than 1.5°C-consistent levels by 2030, according to a 2023 UNEP report.
- Current pledges put the world on a path to roughly 2.1-2.9°C of warming, still far above the 1.5°C target.
- Industrialised countries have produced 92% of the world's excess emissions compared with a fair per-person share.
- The USA emits more than three times its fair per-person share. China emits 29% of global emissions with 19% of the population. India emits 7% with 18% of the population.
- 151 of 202 countries have proposed or pledged net-zero targets, covering 89% of world population and 88% of emissions (Net Zero Tracker).
- The richest 1% of people emit as much carbon pollution as the poorest 5 billion combined (Stockholm Environment Institute and Oxfam).
- Sustained protest by just 3.5% of a population has historically been enough to force a government to change.
- 1,587 climate court cases were documented between 1986 and 2020, over three-quarters of them in the US.
