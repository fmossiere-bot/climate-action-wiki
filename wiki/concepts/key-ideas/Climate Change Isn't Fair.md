# Climate Change Isn't Fair: The Inequality of Who Emits and Who Pays

Chapter 8 of [FACTS on EVERYTHING - Under the Sky we Make](FACTS%20on%20EVERYTHING%20-%20Under%20the%20Sky%20we%20Make.md) goes into full detail on how unfairly climate change is both caused and felt.

According to the [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/the-world-s-top-1-of-emitters-produce-over-1000-times-more-co2-than-the-bottom-1), the world's top 1% of emitters produce over 1,000 times more CO2 than the bottom 1%. However you slice the numbers, what high emitters in rich countries, especially the United States, do on their own and together over the next decade will shape the outcome.

## Country Contributions and Pledges

How much each country contributed in 2022 is set out in [Global Emissions in 2022](Global%20Emissions%20in%202022.md). Looking at today's pledges, only one country appears on track for a 2030 pledge in line with 1.5°C: **Gambia**. The pledges and actions of every other major emitter fall well short. The top five emitting countries alone account for 60% of all carbon emitted.

## Individual Inequality by Income Group

Around **72% of climate pollution** can be traced back to choices households make about getting around, food and housing. In the United States, more income means more climate pollution. Worldwide, anyone earning more than $38,000 a year is in the richest 10% of the global population, a group responsible for about 50% of household carbon pollution.

- The richest 10% of Americans (over $201,000 a year) emit an average of **50 tonnes of CO2 per year**.
- Worldwide, the top 1% of emitters also emit more than 50 tonnes a year each.
- A median US household (around $69,000 a year) emits **16 tonnes a year**.
- The super-rich (the global top 1%) emit **10,000 times more carbon from flying** than the average person.

To stay within a carbon budget in line with 1.5°C, each person's share works out at around **2.5 tonnes per year**. Today, only 5% of Europeans live within it. India is roughly at this level already, and African countries are below it. The average footprint worldwide is 4.7 tonnes a person a year, about the same as two return flights between New York and Singapore.

The top 10% of emitters are responsible for **50% of all carbon emissions**. Around 85% of this group, roughly 700 million people, live in advanced economies, and around 50 million of them are millionaires or billionaires.

### Inequality Goes Even Further

Research from the Stockholm Environment Institute estimates that the **richest 0.1% emits 10 times more** than the rest of the richest 10% combined.

## Individual Emissions Vary Greatly by Country

The gaps in emissions between countries are still huge. But in recent years, the gaps *within* countries and regions have become even bigger than those *between* them.

- In the United States, the richest tenth of the population emits over **55 tonnes of CO2 per person** each year. Road transport makes up an especially large share of that, around a quarter.
- In the European Union, the richest tenth emits around **24 tonnes of CO2 per person**, lower than in the US partly because the power grids are cleaner. In both the US and the EU, the top tenth emits three to five times more than the median person, and around 16 times more than the poorest tenth. Even so, the poorest 10% in countries including the United States, Canada, Japan and South Korea still emit more than the median person worldwide.
- In China, the richest tenth emits almost **30 tonnes of CO2 per person** each year, 30% more than a decade ago, after a period of rapid economic growth. In India, the richest tenth emits just 7 tonnes of CO2 per person.
- Emissions inequality in China, India and other developing economies across Latin America, Africa and Asia is higher than in advanced economies, with the top tenth emitting five to eight times more than the median.

If the richest 10% of people in the world cut their emissions to the level of an average European (still a comfortable standard of living), global emissions could fall by roughly a third. Income and emissions go hand in hand. The [UNEP Emissions Gap Report](https://www.unep.org/interactive/emissions-gap-report/2020/#) calculates a fixed "carbon budget" that everyone on Earth could share while keeping temperatures at a safe level, but in practice how much each person uses depends closely on their income, wherever they live.

## Individual Emissions Also Vary Greatly Within a Country

According to data obtained by the Guardian, the richest 10% of people in many countries cause **up to 40 times more** climate-heating emissions than the poorest 10% of their fellow citizens. When international climate negotiations began in the 1990s, most of the inequality in emissions was between rich and poor nations. Three decades on, that has reversed: most of it now lies between rich and poor people within the same country. This changes how the climate crisis can be tackled, although support for the poorest and least-polluting nations is still vital.

The figures below use International Energy Agency data on energy-related CO2 emissions per person in 2021, across a dozen major countries plus the 27-nation EU:

- In the US, UK, EU and Japan, the richest 10% have carbon footprints about **15 times** greater than the poorest 10%.
- In China, South Africa, Brazil and India, the top 10% cause **30 to 40 times** more emissions than the bottom 10%.
- In South Korea, the top 10% cause **14 tonnes of CO2 per person** a year from buying goods alone, double the figure for the next richest 10% in the country.

In every country studied, the top 10% emit at least as much as the bottom 50% put together. In the US and China, the top 10% emit more than the bottom 70% put together. South Africa is the most extreme case: the top 10% have the same footprint as the other 90% of the population.

Transport, especially driving, is a big part of this. Across the countries analysed, the transport emissions of the richest 10% are 20 to 40 times higher than those of the poorest 10%. In the US and Canada, road transport makes up about a third of the top 10%'s footprint, and the richest 10%'s transport emissions match those of the bottom 70% of the population. The things people buy, such as furniture and electronics, are another big factor. The emissions built into these goods are 20 to 50 times higher for the richest 10%, and make up about a third of emissions in most countries. Even the poorest 10% in the US have a bigger footprint than 90% of people in India.

## How About Millionaires?

Tackling the emissions of the rich matters even more for the future. A [study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666791622000252) projects that the number of people with $1 million in wealth, mostly in the value of their homes, will rise from about **52 million in 2020 to 511 million in 2050** (adjusted for inflation), growing from 0.7% to 3.3% of the world's population. The researchers estimated that this growth in millionaires would lead to 286 billion tonnes of CO2 in total, about 70% of the carbon budget left for 1.5°C as of 2021. A more recent estimate put the [remaining carbon budget at 250 billion tonnes](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2023/oct/30/climate-crisis-carbon-emissions-budget), which suggests the growth in millionaires alone could use it all up.

Professor Stefan Gössling of Linnaeus University in Sweden, who led the study, explained why this matters for policy: "I think it is significant that such a small share of humanity will consume so much of the remaining carbon budget. It will not be enough to just impose taxes on carbon, as the rich can essentially afford to pay the extra amount, while poor people would be more affected."

## How About Billionaires?

A 2022 report found that billionaires are not saving the planet, despite their large donations. Big pledges get a lot of attention: Jeff Bezos committed [$10 billion to an Earth Fund](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/02/17/technology/jeff-bezos-climate-change-earth-fund.html), and Elon Musk offered a [$100 million prize](https://thehill.com/changing-america/sustainability/climate-change/535393-elon-musk-will-give-100-million-to-the-person/) to a carbon dioxide removal company. Reporting on billionaires' outsized influence on climate policy points out that they personally emit **one million times more** greenhouse gases a year than the average person on Earth, and that their wealth often comes from high-emitting companies that feed a wider culture of overconsumption.

### Billionaire Philanthropy in Context

Philanthropy is often used to polish a public image and extend political influence, a pattern seen often with fossil fuel companies. In a recent year, total giving to climate causes reached **$13 billion**. That sounds large, but it is small next to all philanthropic giving worldwide, around $822 billion. Even a big donation like Bezos's $10 billion, pledged over 10 years, needs context. Up to 60% of global greenhouse gas emissions can be traced back to the products and services people consume, and Amazon is by far the world's most popular place to buy them.

Billionaires do have a part to play, but the money has to match the size of the problem. The Climate Policy Initiative estimates that solving climate change will cost at least **$4.3 trillion per year**. The International Monetary Fund reports that the world currently spends only around $630 billion a year on climate finance, which leaves a gap measured in trillions.
