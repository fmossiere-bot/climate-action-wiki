# Climate Change Isn't Fair: The Inequality of Who Emits and Who Pays

See [FACTS on EVERYTHING - Under the Sky we Make](FACTS%20on%20EVERYTHING%20-%20Under%20the%20Sky%20we%20Make.md), Chapter 8, for full detail on the unfairness of how climate change is both caused and experienced.

The world's top 1% of emitters produce over 1,000 times more CO2 than the bottom 1%, according to the [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/the-world-s-top-1-of-emitters-produce-over-1000-times-more-co2-than-the-bottom-1). No matter how the numbers are cut, what high emitters in rich countries, especially the United States, do individually and collectively over the next decade will be critical to the outcome.

## Country Contributions and Pledges

The contribution by country in 2022 is set out in [Global Emissions in 2022](Global%20Emissions%20in%202022.md). Looking at pledges today, only one country appears on track for its 2030, 1.5°C-aligned pledge: **Gambia**. Every other major emitter's pledges and actions are highly insufficient. The top five emitting countries alone account for 60% of all carbon emitted.

## Individual Inequality by Income Group

Around **72% of climate pollution can be traced back to household consumption decisions**: mobility, diet and housing. In the United States, income buys climate pollution: anyone earning more than $38,000 a year is in the top 10% of American earners, a group collectively responsible for 50% of household carbon pollution.

- The richest 10% of Americans (over $201,000 a year) emit an average of **50 tonnes of CO2 per year**.
- Globally, the top 1% of emitters also emit more than 50 tonnes a year each.
- A median US household (around $69,000 a year) emits **16 tonnes a year**.
- The super-rich (the global top 1%) emit an astonishing **10,000 times more carbon from flying** than the average person.

To stay within a 1.5°C-aligned carbon budget, the individual limit works out at around **2.5 tonnes per person per year**. Today, only 5% of Europeans meet this limit. India is broadly at this level already; African countries are below it. The average global footprint per person is **4.7 tonnes a year**, roughly the equivalent of two round-trip flights between New York and Singapore.

The top 10% of emitters are responsible for **50% of all carbon emissions**. Around 85% of this group live in advanced economies, roughly 700 million people, of whom around 50 million are millionaires or billionaires.

### Inequality Goes Even Further

Research from the Stockholm Environment Institute estimates that the **richest 0.1% emits 10 times more than the rest of the richest 10% combined**.

## Individual Emissions Vary Greatly by Country

While disparities in emissions between countries remain profound, gaps in greenhouse gas emissions *within* countries and regions have, in recent years, become even more significant than those *between* countries.

- In the **United States**, the richest decile emits over **55 tonnes of CO2 per capita** each year, with road transport making up an especially high share, around a quarter, of that group's footprint.
- In the **European Union**, the richest decile emits around **24 tonnes of CO2 per capita**, lower than the US equivalent partly thanks to less emissions-intensive power grids. In both the US and the EU, the top decile emits three to five times more than the median individual, and around 16 times more than the poorest decile. Even so, the poorest 10% in countries including the United States, Canada, Japan and South Korea still emit more than the global median individual.
- In **China**, the richest decile emits almost **30 tonnes of CO2 per capita** each year, 30% more than a decade ago, following a period of rapid economic development. In **India**, the richest decile emits just **7 tonnes of CO2 per capita**.
- Emissions inequality in China, India and other developing economies across Latin America, Africa and Asia is higher than in advanced economies, with the top decile emitting five to eight times more than the median.

If the richest 10% of the global population reduced its emissions to the level of an average European (a comfortable standard of living), global emissions could be cut by roughly a third. Income is strongly correlated with emissions: the [UNEP Emissions Gap Report](https://www.unep.org/interactive/emissions-gap-report/2020/#) calculates a fixed "carbon budget" that everyone on Earth could emit while keeping global temperatures at a safe level, but in practice each person's share is closely tied to their income, wherever they live.

## Individual Emissions Also Vary Greatly Within a Country

The richest 10% of people in many countries cause **up to 40 times more** climate-heating emissions than the poorest 10% of their fellow citizens, according to data obtained by the Guardian. When international climate negotiations began in the 1990s, most inequality in emissions was between rich and poor nations; three decades on, the situation has reversed, and most inequality in emissions between rich and poor now exists within individual countries. This shift has major implications for how the climate crisis can be addressed, even as international support for the poorest and least-polluting nations remains vital.

Using International Energy Agency data on energy-related CO2 emissions per person in 2021 across a dozen major countries plus the 27-nation EU:

- In the **US, UK, EU and Japan**, the richest 10% have carbon footprints about 15 times greater than the poorest 10%.
- In **China, South Africa, Brazil and India**, the top 10% cause 30 to 40 times more emissions than the bottom 10%.
- In **South Korea**, the top 10% cause 14 tonnes of CO2 per person annually from buying goods alone, double the figure for the next richest 10% within the country.

In all countries studied, the emissions of the top 10% are at least as high as those of the bottom 50% combined. In the US and China, the top 10%'s emissions exceed those of the bottom 70% combined. South Africa is the most extreme case, where the footprint of the top 10% equals that of the remaining 90% of the population.

Transport, especially car use, is a major factor: the richest 10%'s transport emissions are 20 to 40 times higher than the poorest 10%'s across the countries analysed. In the US and Canada, road transport makes up about a third of the footprint of the top 10%, and the transport emissions of the richest 10% match the transport footprint of the bottom 70% of the population in those countries. Goods people buy, such as furniture and electronics, are another major factor: embodied emissions in these goods are 20 to 50 times higher for the richest 10%, making up about a third of emissions in most countries. Notably, the poorest 10% in the US still have a footprint bigger than 90% of people in India.

## How About Millionaires?

Tackling high emissions among the rich matters even more looking ahead. A [study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666791622000252) found the number of people with $1 million in wealth, mostly the value of their homes, is projected to rise from about **52 million in 2020 to 511 million in 2050** (inflation-adjusted), taking the group from 0.7% to 3.3% of the global population. The researchers estimated this growth in millionaires would result in accumulated emissions of **286 billion tonnes of CO2**, about 70% of the remaining 1.5°C-aligned carbon budget as of 2021. A more recent estimate put the [remaining carbon budget at 250 billion tonnes](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2023/oct/30/climate-crisis-carbon-emissions-budget), suggesting the growth in millionaires alone could exhaust it completely.

Professor Stefan Gössling of Linnaeus University in Sweden, who led the study, said: "I think it is significant that such a small share of humanity will consume so much of the remaining carbon budget. It will not be enough to just impose taxes on carbon, as the rich can essentially afford to pay the extra amount, while poor people would be more affected."

## How About Billionaires?

A 2022 report found that billionaires are not saving the planet, despite their large philanthropic contributions. High-profile pledges generate significant attention: Jeff Bezos committed [$10 billion to an Earth Fund](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/02/17/technology/jeff-bezos-climate-change-earth-fund.html), and Elon Musk offered a [$100 million prize](https://thehill.com/changing-america/sustainability/climate-change/535393-elon-musk-will-give-100-million-to-the-person/) to a carbon dioxide removal company. Coverage of billionaires' outsized influence on climate policy notes that billionaires personally emit one million times more greenhouse gases per year than the average person on Earth, and that their wealth often derives from high-emitting corporations that foster a broader culture of excessive consumerism.

### Billionaire Philanthropy in Context

Philanthropy is often used to build a positive public image and extend political influence, a pattern seen frequently with fossil fuel companies. Total philanthropy directed towards climate causes reached **$13 billion** in a recent year, a large-sounding figure that is small compared with overall global philanthropic giving of around **$822 billion**. Even a headline donation like Bezos's $10 billion, pledged over 10 years, needs context: up to 60% of global greenhouse gas emissions can be traced back to the products and services people consume, and Amazon is by far the world's most popular place to consume products and services.

Billionaires do have a part to play, but meaningful investment is needed to match the scale of the challenge. According to the Climate Policy Initiative, actually solving climate change is estimated to cost at least **$4.3 trillion per year**. The International Monetary Fund reports the world is currently spending only around **$630 billion a year** on climate finance, a shortfall measured in the trillions.
