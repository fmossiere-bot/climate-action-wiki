# Carbon Offsets: How They Work and Why They Often Fall Short

## What it is
A **carbon offset** is a cut in greenhouse gas emissions, or an increase in carbon storage, used to make up for emissions happening somewhere else. In practice it usually means buying a carbon offset credit: a certificate you can trade, representing one tonne of CO2 (or the same warming effect from another greenhouse gas) that a specific project, such as a wind farm or a protected forest, has supposedly avoided or removed. The reasoning is that greenhouse gases mix around the whole atmosphere, so it shouldn't matter where a tonne is cut. The trouble is that offsets let organisations keep emitting on the promise that the same amount is being cancelled out elsewhere, and proving that the promise is kept has turned out to be extremely hard.

## Why it matters
Almost every company or country with a net-zero pledge relies on offsetting to some extent. Yet it is one of the most disputed tools in climate action. Investigations on several continents have found that a large share of the offset credits on the market do not deliver the emissions cuts they claim. Knowing how offsets are meant to work, and where they usually break down, helps anyone judge whether a company's "carbon neutral" claim stands up.

## What carbon offsets are and how the market works
Offset credits come from individual "projects" that cut or remove emissions. They are often sold with promised "co-benefits", such as better local livelihoods, cleaner water or protection for wildlife. Buyers mostly choose offsets over cutting their own emissions for cost reasons: it is usually cheaper to pay for a cut "someplace else" than to make the same cut in your own operations.

The credits are traded through carbon offset "programmes". These set quality standards, review projects (usually through independent verifiers) and keep registries that issue, transfer and retire credits. **Compliance offset programmes** are regulated by governments and used by companies that have legal limits on their emissions. Voluntary offset programmes are run by businesses or NGOs for buyers with no such legal duty. The first big programmes, the Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) and Joint Implementation (JI), were set up in the late 1990s under the Kyoto Protocol, when there was no binding global emissions target. Both have since been heavily criticised. A European Commission review found the CDM had "fundamental flaws in terms of overall environmental integrity," and estimated that for 73% of the potential 2013-2020 Certified Emission Reduction (CER) supply, there was a low likelihood that the emission cuts were truly additional and not over-estimated.

Some companies also buy other tradable certificates linked to emissions claims, such as voluntary Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs), green Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) and Energy Efficiency Certificates (EECs). Most of these purchases are not covered by the certification standards that apply to real offset claims. Independent research summarised in the Greenhouse Gas Management Institute's Carbon Offset Guide suggests organisations that want credible, measurable reduction targets should generally avoid them.

## The seven core challenges with offsetting
Scientists and campaigners worry that polluters are using offsets as a licence to do nothing. That matters because annual emissions need to fall by 29 to 32 gigatonnes of CO2-equivalent by 2030 to keep a fighting chance of staying below 1.5°C, a five-fold increase on current ambition. Analysts group the problems with offsetting into seven main challenges.

**The atmosphere itself.** To have a chance of meeting climate goals, the world must cut roughly 90% of emissions directly, and nothing can replace that. Everyone needs to be cutting emissions now, whoever is paying whom. The only net-zero pathway that fits the science is one where an organisation cuts about 90% of its own emissions and then permanently removes and stores the rest. Paying to avoid emissions elsewhere while carrying on polluting at the same rate does not fit.

**Additionality.** A project only counts as a real offset if it would not have happened without the offset money. That is very hard to prove. If a wind farm would have been built anyway because it made business sense, or a forest was never really at risk of being cut down, then no extra carbon was cut or removed. Buying that credit simply lets the buyer's own emissions go uncancelled.

**Permanence.** A credible offset has to store carbon for a long time. Many projects fail this test. In 2020 the Lionshead Fire in Oregon destroyed one of the largest forest carbon offset projects in California's carbon market. It showed how a forest, and the carbon it holds, can be lost long before that carbon was meant to be released. Because temporary storage and permanent storage are not the same, comparing the cost per tonne of two removal projects can mislead if one stores carbon briefly and the other for good.

**Double counting.** Under the Paris Agreement every country has now pledged some level of emissions cuts. That shrinks the pool of truly "additional" cuts left to sell as offsets, and raises the risk that the same cut is claimed twice: once by the country hosting a forest-protection project, and again by the foreign buyer of its credits. There is no single global registry tracking every project and credit. A Bloomberg analysis found that about half of all offsets sold in voluntary markets have no buyer's name attached at all, and it is still unclear how the voluntary carbon market will fit with the legal framework of the Paris Agreement.

**Leakage.** Nothing stops a project developer taking money to protect one piece of land while clearing the plot next door for oil palms or cattle. The only real way to reduce leakage is to cut demand for the products behind it: timber, palm oil and meat.

**Overestimation of credits.** The baselines used to measure a project's impact are often set in ways that overstate the cut, and buyers often underestimate their own emissions as well.

**Social and environmental harm.** Offset projects should not threaten Indigenous livelihoods, local culture and traditions, or harm wildlife and water supplies. In practice, several well-documented projects have done exactly that.

## What real-world investigations have found
These seven challenges are real. Journalists and researchers have documented them again and again in offset markets around the world.

In **Brazil**, a long ProPublica investigation concluded that "the desperate hunger for these carbon credit plans appears to have blinded many of their advocates to the mounting pile of evidence that they haven't, and won't, deliver the climate benefit they promise." The same reporter's follow-up in California was titled "The Climate Solution Actually Adding Millions of Tons of CO2 Into the Atmosphere," and a 2021 study found the state's offsets programme engaged in "systematic over-crediting." A 2022 satellite analysis found no real climate benefit from ten years of forest carbon offsets in California, and wildfires are making the state's forests an ever shakier place to store carbon. Across the United States, the forest ecologist Charles Canham has said the flaws in America's offset markets "are structural and deep, and may be irredeemable." Researchers working with MIT Technology Review found that Amazon's forestry offset proposals may greatly overstate carbon cuts.

In Colombia, Carbon Market Watch found millions of bogus carbon credits that "do not deliver practical results for the climate, nor for forest conservation," and that also cost the public purse. In the **Republic of Congo**, an investigation found that farmers on the Batéké Plateau lost access to their own land after it was taken for a tree-planting offset project run for the French oil company TotalEnergies. One farmer said: "I feel like these people came to kill us on our own land." In Peru, a Bloomberg Green analysis found that millions of dollars from Disney boosted government forest protection while also worsening tensions with thousands of people living in the protected areas. In Borneo, Al Jazeera reported on an $80 billion carbon deal "hammered out in absolute secrecy and without credible due diligence," involving a politician linked to 1990s deforestation and an associate named in the Panama Papers. In Cambodia, the researchers Milne and Mahanty found that the checks used to validate and verify carbon projects led to what they called "bureaucratic violence," producing "profound environmental and social injustices, especially for indigenous communities facing illegal logging and land alienation." In Australia, an Australian Broadcasting Corporation investigation found that paying landowners simply to leave land uncleared can backfire.

The biggest findings concern **Verra**, the world's leading provider of forest carbon offsets. An analysis of 18 large REDD+ carbon-offsetting projects, reported by New Scientist, found that only around 6% of the credits issued were actually linked to saving trees. A related investigation by The Guardian concluded that 94% of the forest-related offset projects validated by Verra did not represent real carbon cuts. Verra publicly disputed this. It argued that its own method, which takes account of factors such as population, location and high-risk areas, puts its real impact closer to 50%. Even the two sides of the debate are far apart.

Elsewhere, researchers at UC Berkeley led by Dr Barbara Haya have built a public collection of research on offset quality and a database of voluntary market credits and projects. A joint investigation by The Guardian and Greenpeace found that the forest-protection offsets used by major airlines to claim "carbon-neutral flying" have a serious credibility problem, which matters because flying is often where people first come across offsets. A University College London study found that without radical reform, the global carbon market could undermine efforts to fight global warming. Bloomberg Green analysed over 260,000 transactions across three public registries and found that total demand for offsets fell 17% in 2022 compared with 2021, the first such drop. Over the same period, companies bought more renewable-energy credits, even though most experts have written those off because wind, hydro and solar are now the cheapest power sources in most of the world anyway. In September 2022 the British airline EasyJet said it would stop using most carbon credits on the way to its mid-century net-zero goal.

## Best practice: the Oxford Offsetting Principles, and what Microsoft and Stripe are doing differently
Researchers at Oxford developed a strict standard for credible offsetting, built on four principles. The first is to **cut emissions and use only high-quality offsets**: make direct cuts the priority, rely on offsets as little as possible, and keep environmental integrity and transparency. The second is to shift towards carbon removal offsetting. A true offset takes carbon out of the atmosphere rather than just avoiding future emissions, so over time a credible strategy should move from emissions-reduction projects to projects that actually remove carbon. The third is to shift to long-lived storage, favouring offsets that store carbon for many years or permanently over short-term ones. The fourth is to support offset markets that line up with net zero, for example through long-term contracts that give developers certainty, and by protecting natural ecosystems for their own sake, not only for their carbon.

Microsoft and Stripe are two companies often held up as doing better. Neither puts offsets at the centre of its climate strategy. Microsoft has deep targets for cutting its own emissions, including its Scope 3 emissions (those in its supply chain and from product use), which are notoriously difficult and which it openly admits it is struggling to reduce. Stripe makes no claim to offset its own emissions at all. Instead it pays early for durable carbon removal technologies to help that market grow. Both companies are unusually open, publishing detailed analysis of their offset purchases through CarbonPlan and in peer-reviewed journals including Nature. Both have also struggled to find enough high-quality projects that meet their strict criteria, and additionality remains a problem even for their most carefully checked purchases. Stripe, Shopify, Alphabet (Google's parent), Meta and others have since joined a $925 million joint commitment called Frontier to buy captured carbon between now and 2030.

For organisations without Microsoft's resources, there are four practical alternatives. First, follow the Oxford Offsetting Principles as closely as possible, even at small scale. Second, use careful accounting methods based on current science, such as Carbon Direct's approach to accounting for short-term storage in offsets, or check an offset provider against the Stockholm Environment Institute's best-practice guide. Third, consider **carbon insetting**: cutting emissions inside your own supply chain rather than buying credits from outside, which also strengthens relationships with suppliers and makes the supply chain more resilient. Fourth, as a 2022 Time investigation pointed out, it can sometimes be more truthful simply to donate to climate charities rather than buy offsets, though those charities deserve the same scrutiny. Tools such as Mongabay's tree-planting transparency tool can help with that checking. Another option is to drop "net-zero" as the main goal altogether if it mostly encourages grabbing cheap, low-quality credits, and instead set achievable internal reduction targets while backing one high-impact area of climate action with real conviction.

A separate, nature-based approach is **REDD+** (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation, plus sustainable forest management and conservation). It is a UN programme launched at COP13 in 2007, under which developed countries pay developing countries to protect their forests. By early 2022, more than 398 million REDD+ credits had been issued on the voluntary carbon market. It has not yet lived up to its potential as a large-scale source of funding, but some research suggests the overall benefit to communities can be greater than from logging or gold mining, even where the direct profits look smaller.

## Choosing an offset provider
The voluntary marketplaces vary a lot in quality. Sequestr.io gives little public information about its methods and has drawn scepticism over double counting and inflated credits; it mentions forestry projects without addressing wildfire risk. Pachama, popular in the US, sets project baselines using satellite monitoring (for example, crediting "additionality" when sensors detect healthier forest), requires at least ten years without harvesting, and tracks leakage by satellite. Ever.green (where Fabien has a personal connection through a colleague, Liz) works in a similar way to Google, funding new renewable energy projects to make up for fossil fuel use and issuing a certificate per megawatt-hour. CarbonNeutral works more as a certification standard than a pure offset marketplace and requires buyers to follow its own protocol. theCommons.earth is different again: an app that tracks a user's footprint, suggests ways to reduce it, and treats offsetting as only one part of the process. Ecologi, which runs on a subscription model like Wren, is B Corp certified, follows the Gold Standard, and publishes fairly detailed and trustworthy explanations of how it works.

**Gold Standard**, run by a Swiss non-profit, is widely seen as the leading independent certifier of offset credits, and it deliberately screens out high-risk projects. Because additionality is the hardest test for any offset, Gold Standard does not certify renewable energy projects in Western Europe, on the grounds that they would most likely have been built anyway.

## Summary findings
- Almost all companies with net-zero pledges buy offsets or use offsetting in some form, even though the sector is highly controversial (UN Environment Programme).
- A European Commission review found 73% of potential 2013-2020 CDM Certified Emission Reductions had a low likelihood of being truly additional and not over-estimated.
- Annual emissions need to fall by 29-32 gigatonnes of CO2e by 2030, a five-fold increase on current ambition, to keep 1.5°C within reach.
- An 18-project analysis found only around 6% of the REDD+ credits studied were actually linked to saving trees. The Guardian separately found 94% of Verra's forest-related projects did not represent real carbon cuts (Verra disputes this and puts its own impact closer to 50%).
- About half of all offsets sold in voluntary markets have no buyer's name attached, according to a Bloomberg analysis.
