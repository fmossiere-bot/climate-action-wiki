# Carbon Offsets: How They Work and Why They Often Fall Short

## What it is
A **carbon offset** is a reduction in greenhouse gas emissions, or an increase in carbon storage, that is used to compensate for emissions happening somewhere else. In practice this usually means buying a **carbon offset credit**, a transferable certificate representing one tonne of CO2 (or an equivalent amount of another greenhouse gas) that has supposedly been avoided or removed by a specific project, such as a wind farm or a protected forest. Because greenhouse gases mix globally in the atmosphere, the logic goes, it doesn't matter where a tonne is reduced. **The core problem is that offsets let organisations continue emitting on the promise that an equivalent amount is being cancelled out elsewhere, and proving that promise is genuinely met has turned out to be extremely difficult.**

## Why it matters
Almost every company or country that has made a net-zero pledge is relying, to some extent, on offsetting. Yet this is one of the most controversial tools in the climate toolbox: investigations across multiple continents have found that a large share of offset credits on the market do not represent the emissions reductions they claim to. Understanding how offsets are meant to work, and where they routinely break down, matters for anyone trying to judge whether a company's "carbon neutral" claim is credible.

## What carbon offsets are and how the market works
Offset credits are produced by discrete "projects" that reduce or remove emissions, and are often sold with claimed "co-benefits" such as improved local livelihoods, better water quality, or biodiversity protection. Buyers choose offsets over direct emissions cuts largely for economic reasons: it is usually cheaper to fund a reduction "someplace else" than to achieve the same cut in your own operations.

These credits trade through carbon offset "programmes", markets that set quality standards, review projects (typically via third-party verifiers) and run registries that issue, transfer and retire credits. **Compliance offset programmes** are government-regulated and used by companies with legal emissions obligations; **voluntary offset programmes** are run by businesses or NGOs for buyers with no such obligation. The first major programmes, the Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) and Joint Implementation (JI), were created in the late 1990s under the Kyoto Protocol, at a time when there was no binding global emissions target. Both have since been heavily criticised: a European Commission review found the CDM had "fundamental flaws in terms of overall environmental integrity," estimating that 73% of the potential 2013-2020 Certified Emission Reduction (CER) supply had a low likelihood of being genuinely additional and was not over-estimated.

Some companies also buy other tradable instruments associated with emissions-reduction claims, such as voluntary Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs), green Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs), and Energy Efficiency Certificates (EECs). Crucially, most of these purchases are not subject to the certification standards that apply to genuine offset claims, and independent research summarised by the Greenhouse Gas Management Institute's Carbon Offset Guide suggests these particular instruments should generally be avoided by organisations that want credible, quantifiable reduction targets.

## The seven core challenges with offsetting
Scientists and campaigners have raised concerns that offsets are being used by polluters as a licence for inaction, at a moment when annual emissions need to fall by 29 to 32 gigatonnes of CO2-equivalent by 2030, a five-fold increase on current ambition, to keep a fighting chance of staying below 1.5°C. Analysts group the problems with offsetting into seven main challenges.

**1. The atmosphere itself.** To have a chance of meeting climate goals, the world must cut roughly 90% of emissions directly, and there is no substitute for that. Every actor needs to be reducing emissions now, regardless of who is financing whom; the only scientifically consistent net-zero pathway is one where an organisation cuts about 90% of its own emissions and then permanently removes and stores the remainder, rather than paying to avoid emissions elsewhere while continuing to pollute at the same rate.

**2. Additionality.** A project is only a genuine offset if it would not have happened without the offset payment. This is very hard to prove. If a wind farm would have been built anyway because it made good business sense, or a forest was never actually at risk of being cleared, then no additional carbon was really reduced or removed, and buying that credit simply lets the purchaser's own emissions rise uncancelled.

**3. Permanence.** A credible offset needs to store carbon for a genuinely long period. Many projects fail this test: the 2020 Lionshead Fire in Oregon destroyed one of the largest forest carbon offset projects participating in California's carbon market, illustrating how a windfarm can be abandoned or a forest destroyed long before the carbon it stored would otherwise have stayed locked away. Because temporary storage and permanent storage are not equivalent, comparing the cost per tonne of two removal projects can be misleading if one offers only short-term storage and the other offers permanent storage.

**4. Double counting.** Under the Paris Agreement, every country has now pledged some level of emissions reduction, which shrinks the pool of genuinely "additional" cuts available for sale as offsets, and raises the risk that the same reduction gets claimed twice, for instance once by the country hosting a forest-protection project and again by the foreign buyer of its credits. There is no single global registry tracking every project and every credit: a Bloomberg analysis found that about half of all offsets sold in voluntary markets have no buyer's name attached to them at all, and there remains no clarity on how the voluntary carbon market will align with the legal architecture of the Paris Agreement.

**5. Leakage.** Nothing stops a project developer from taking funds to protect one piece of land while deforesting the next patch over to plant oil palms or graze cattle. Leakage can only really be reduced by cutting demand for the underlying commodities: timber, palm oil and meat.

**6. Overestimation of credits.** Baselines against which a project's impact is measured are often set in ways that overstate the reduction achieved, compounded by buyers underestimating their own emissions.

**7. Social and environmental harm.** Offset projects should not jeopardise indigenous livelihoods, local culture and traditions, or cause harm to biodiversity and water resources, yet in practice several well-documented projects have done exactly that.

## What real-world investigations have found
The seven challenges above are not theoretical: journalists and researchers have documented them repeatedly across offset markets worldwide.

In **Brazil**, a lengthy ProPublica investigation concluded that "the desperate hunger for these carbon credit plans appears to have blinded many of their advocates to the mounting pile of evidence that they haven't, and won't, deliver the climate benefit they promise." The same reporter's follow-up investigation in **California** was headlined "The Climate Solution Actually Adding Millions of Tons of CO2 Into the Atmosphere," and a 2021 study found the state's offsets programme engaged in "systematic over-crediting." A 2022 satellite analysis found no real climate benefit from ten years of forest carbon offsets in California, and wildfires are making forests an increasingly shaky bet for carbon storage in the state generally. More broadly in the **United States**, forest ecologist Charles Canham has said the flaws in America's offset markets "are structural and deep, and may be irredeemable," while researchers working with MIT Technology Review found Amazon's forestry offset proposals may significantly overstate carbon reductions.

In **Colombia**, Carbon Market Watch found millions of bogus carbon credits that "do not deliver practical results for the climate, nor for forest conservation," and also caused a loss of public revenue. In the **Republic of Congo**, an investigation found farmers on the Batéké Plateau lost access to their own land after it was taken for a tree-planting offset project run for the French oil company TotalEnergies; one farmer said "I feel like these people came to kill us on our own land." In **Peru**, a Bloomberg Green analysis found that millions of dollars from Disney bolstered government forest protection efforts while simultaneously exacerbating tensions with thousands of people living in the protected areas. In **Borneo**, Al Jazeera reported on an $80 billion carbon deal "hammered out in absolute secrecy and without credible due diligence," involving a politician linked to 1990s deforestation and a Panama Papers-named associate. In **Cambodia**, researchers Milne and Mahanty found that validation and verification processes for carbon markets gave rise to what they termed "bureaucratic violence," producing "profound environmental and social injustices, especially for indigenous communities facing illegal logging and land alienation." In **Australia**, an Australian Broadcasting Corporation investigation found that government funding to simply leave land uncleared can backfire.

The most sweeping findings concern **Verra**, the world's leading provider of forest carbon offsets. An analysis of 18 large REDD+ carbon-offsetting projects, reported by New Scientist, found that only around 6% of the credits issued were actually linked to saving trees. A related investigation by The Guardian concluded that 94% of forest-related offset projects validated by Verra did not represent genuine carbon reductions. Verra publicly disputed this, arguing its own methodology, which accounts for factors like population, location and high-risk areas, puts its real impact closer to 50%, a reminder that even the two sides of this debate disagree by a wide margin.

Elsewhere, researchers at UC Berkeley, led by Dr Barbara Haya, have compiled a public repository of research on offset quality and a database of voluntary market credits and projects. A joint investigation by The Guardian and Greenpeace found that the forest-protection offsetting market used by major airlines for "carbon-neutral flying" claims faces a significant credibility problem, a finding directly relevant given how often the public first encounters offsets in the context of flying. A University College London study found that without radical reform the global carbon market could undermine efforts to fight global warming. Bloomberg Green's analysis of over 260,000 transactions across three public registries found that total demand for offsets fell 17% in 2022 compared with 2021, the first such drop, even as corporate buyers increased purchases of renewable-energy credits despite most experts having written those off, since wind, hydro and solar are now the cheapest power sources in most of the world anyway. The British airline EasyJet said in September 2022 that it would stop using most carbon credits in pursuit of its mid-century net-zero goal.

## Best practice: the Oxford Offsetting Principles, and what Microsoft and Stripe are doing differently
Researchers at Oxford developed a rigorous standard for credible offsetting, built on four principles. First, **cut emissions and use only high-quality offsets**, prioritising direct mitigation and minimising reliance on offsets in the first place, while maintaining environmental integrity and transparency. Second, **shift to carbon removal offsetting**: true offsets remove carbon from the atmosphere rather than merely avoiding future emissions, so over time a credible offsetting strategy should move from emissions-reduction projects towards genuinely negative-emissions projects. Third, **shift to long-lived storage**, prioritising offsets that store carbon for many years or permanently over those offering only short-term storage. Fourth, **support the development of net-zero aligned offsetting markets**, for example through long-term contracts that give offset developers certainty, and through protecting natural ecosystems in their own right, not only for their carbon value.

Microsoft and Stripe are two companies often cited as charting a better path. Neither treats offsets as central to its climate strategy: Microsoft has deep direct-mitigation targets, including for its notoriously difficult Scope 3 emissions, which it has openly admitted it is struggling to reduce, while Stripe makes no claim to offset its own emissions at all, instead pursuing a strategy of accelerating early adoption of durable carbon removal technologies to help transform that market. Both companies are unusually transparent, publishing detailed analysis of their own offset purchasing through CarbonPlan and in peer-reviewed outlets including Nature. Both have also struggled to find enough high-quality projects meeting their stringent criteria, and additionality remains a persistent problem even for their most carefully vetted purchases. Stripe, Shopify, Alphabet (Google's parent), Meta and others have since joined a $925 million joint commitment called Frontier to purchase captured carbon between now and 2030.

For organisations without Microsoft's resources, four practical alternatives stand out. First, apply the Oxford Offsetting Principles as closely as possible even at small scale. Second, use rigorous accounting methods based on current science, such as Carbon Direct's approach to accounting for short-term durability in offsets, or vet an offset provider against the Stockholm Environment Institute's best-practice guide. Third, consider **carbon insetting**, embedding emissions-reduction activities directly within your own supply chain rather than buying external credits, which has the added benefit of strengthening supplier relationships and supply chain resilience. Fourth, as a 2022 Time investigation observed, it may sometimes be more honest simply to donate to climate charities rather than buy offsets, though those charities deserve the same scrutiny; tools such as Mongabay's tree-planting transparency tool can help with that due diligence. A further option is to abandon "net-zero" as the organising goal altogether if it is chiefly creating an incentive to grab cheap, low-quality credits, and instead set achievable internal reduction targets while backing one high-impact area of climate action with real conviction.

A distinct, nature-based approach is **REDD+** (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation, plus sustainable forest management and conservation), a UN programme launched at COP13 in 2007 through which developed countries pay developing countries to protect their forests. As of early 2022, more than 398 million REDD+ credits had been issued on the voluntary carbon market, and while it has not yet achieved its full potential as a large-scale funding mechanism, some research suggests the overall community benefit from REDD+ can exceed that of logging or gold mining, even where the direct profits look smaller.

## Choosing an offset provider
Several voluntary marketplaces illustrate the range of quality in this space. Sequestr.io offers little public information about its methodology and has drawn scepticism over double counting and credit overestimation; it mentions forestry projects without addressing wildfire risk. Pachama, popular in the US, calculates project baselines using satellite monitoring, for example crediting "additionality" when sensors detect improving forest health, requires at least ten years without harvesting, and uses satellite tracking to monitor leakage. Ever.green (where Fabien has a personal connection through a colleague, Liz) takes an approach similar to Google's, funding new renewable energy projects to compensate for fossil fuel use and issuing a certificate per megawatt-hour. CarbonNeutral operates more as a certification standard than a pure offset marketplace, requiring adherence to its own protocol. theCommons.earth is structured differently again, an app that tracks a user's footprint, automates recommendations to reduce it, and treats offsetting as only one part of that process. Ecologi, on a subscription model similar to Wren, is B Corp certified, follows the Gold Standard, and publishes relatively detailed, trustworthy explanations of its process.

**Gold Standard**, run by a Swiss non-profit, is widely regarded as the leading independent certifier of offset credits and deliberately screens out high-risk projects. Because additionality is the hardest test for any offset to pass, Gold Standard does not certify renewable energy projects in Western Europe, on the reasoning that these would most likely have been built anyway.

## Summary findings
- Almost all companies with net-zero pledges purchase or engage in offsetting, even though this sector is highly controversial (UN Environment Programme).
- A European Commission review found 73% of potential 2013-2020 CDM Certified Emission Reductions had a low likelihood of being genuinely additional.
- Annual emissions need to fall by 29-32 gigatonnes of CO2e by 2030, a five-fold increase on current ambition, to keep 1.5°C within reach.
- An 18-project analysis found only around 6% of REDD+ credits studied were actually linked to saving trees; The Guardian separately found 94% of Verra's forest-related projects did not represent genuine carbon reductions (Verra disputes this, estimating its own impact closer to 50%).
- About half of all offsets sold in voluntary markets have no buyer's name attached to them, per a Bloomberg analysis.
- Global demand for offsets fell 17% in 2022 versus 2021, the first such drop, across more than 260,000 tracked transactions since 2010 (Bloomberg Green).
- Stripe, Shopify, Alphabet and Meta committed $925 million through Frontier to purchase captured carbon by 2030.
- More than 398 million REDD+ credits had been issued on the voluntary carbon market as of early 2022.
