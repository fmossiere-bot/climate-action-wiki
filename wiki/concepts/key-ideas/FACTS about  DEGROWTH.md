# FACTS about Degrowth

## What is it

There are two competing schools of thought on how to solve the climate and ecological crisis while keeping economies running. The **green growth** model believes rapid decarbonisation is possible without hurting economic growth, by decoupling emissions from GDP. The **degrowth** model argues that this is not enough: to truly fix our environmental and social problems, wealthy economies need to shrink their material and energy throughput, not keep growing it. Both sides make genuinely compelling arguments and both have blind spots, which is why the debate provokes such strong feelings.

**Degrowth** (from the French *décroissance*) is a political and academic framework that challenges the assumption that endless economic growth is necessary, or even desirable, for human progress. It advocates a **planned, democratic reduction of production and consumption** in high-income nations to bring the economy back into balance with ecological limits, while pursuing social justice. Instead of measuring success by GDP, degrowth proponents look at wellbeing metrics such as health, education and leisure time.

## Why it matters

How this debate is resolved shapes almost every climate policy choice being made today, from the EU's growth-based climate legislation to the assumptions built into influential decarbonisation models like Project Drawdown. Understanding both sides helps make sense of why some serious economists and scientists think today's climate targets are achievable within capitalism, and why others think they are not.

## Origins and core principles

While the concept of *décroissance* originated in France in the 1970s with thinkers like André Gorz and Serge Latouche, it was brought to prominence in the English-speaking world largely through the work of **Joan Martinez-Alier**, a leading figure in ecological economics and political ecology at the Autonomous University of Barcelona (UAB), where he helped establish the Institute of Environmental Science and Technology (ICTA-UAB). His work on environmental justice and the critique of endless growth made Barcelona a global hub for degrowth research and activism.

The movement's core principles are:

- **Ecological limits:** the Earth has finite resources, and perpetual growth on a finite planet is impossible and destructive.
- **Social justice:** degrowth seeks to redistribute wealth and resources so that basic needs are met for everyone, not just those in high-income nations.
- **Reduced throughput:** decreasing the flow of materials and energy through the economy via circular economies, reduced consumption and localised production.
- **Redefining wellbeing:** prioritising human fulfilment and quality of life over material accumulation, including less work and more time for community and personal development.

## Public support for degrowth ideas

While the word "degrowth" is often misunderstood and can sound extreme, the ideas behind it have significant public support:

- A survey across 34 European countries found that on average **61% favoured a "post-growth" approach**, a broader term for moving beyond GDP growth as the central goal.
- Polls across multiple countries, including G20 nations, found a majority believe the economy should prioritise the health and wellbeing of people and nature over profit and wealth accumulation.
- Polling in the US and Europe has repeatedly found that the public believes protecting the environment is more important than economic growth.
- A French study found **67% had a "favourable" opinion of the term "degrowth" itself**, with support spanning the political spectrum.
- A 2015 YouGov poll found **64% of people in Britain** think capitalism is unfair, compared with 55% in the US and 77% in Germany.
- A 2020 Edelman Trust Barometer found a majority agreeing that "capitalism does more harm than good", rising to **69% in France**.
- A 2019 European Council on Foreign Relations poll asked whether the environment should be prioritised even if it damages economic growth; large majorities (55-70%) said yes.
- More broadly, **70% of people in middle- and high-income countries** believe overconsumption is putting the planet and society at risk, and that owning less would not compromise their happiness or wellbeing.

Despite this support, the movement still faces criticism over how a planned economic contraction would affect employment and public services.

## Green growth vs degrowth: two mental models

**The green growth model** holds that we should not stop growing the economy, that we can decouple economic growth from emissions, and that our current capitalist model provides the tools needed to do it. This is the model underlying almost all climate policy today, including the US Inflation Reduction Act, and it is also the assumption baked into influential decarbonisation frameworks like Project Drawdown and marginal abatement cost (MAC) curves.

There is real evidence that some countries are decoupling economic growth from carbon emissions. **Romania** is one of the most striking examples: its net greenhouse gas emissions intensity fell 88% between 1990 and 2023, meaning each dollar of economic activity now heats the planet almost ten times less than it did before, and its total emissions have plunged 75% since the end of the communist era. Today Romania is building some of the world's largest solar farms, with up to 1GW of capacity. That transition came at a cost: industries closed, farmers struggled, and the country shifted toward a service economy, which conveniently suits decarbonisation. Even accounting for imported emissions, the decoupling still holds. A 2023 study of 36 rich countries found 11 had fully broken the link between GDP and CO2, but **none had done so fast enough** to meet their share of the Paris Agreement's 1.5C target.

**The degrowth model** argues the current growth-based approach only makes progress at the margins, and that the growth model itself led us into the crisis. Jason Hickel, the most prominent degrowth proponent, argues that we cannot decarbonise fast enough while the economy keeps growing, because more growth means more energy demand; degrowth would reduce that demand and speed up the transition, and combined with a shorter working week and living wages, it could improve social outcomes too. George Monbiot, an environmental writer for *The Guardian*, has made a similar case: "less of everything is the only way to avert catastrophe."

## Material footprint and planetary boundaries

Material extraction has grown enormously alongside GDP: from **7 billion tonnes in 1900**, to **35 billion tonnes in 1980**, **50 billion tonnes by 2000**, and **92 billion tonnes by 2017**. Scientists estimate a safe level of material extraction at **50 billion tonnes a year**, a limit the world passed in 2000. According to the United Nations, **material extraction and processing is responsible for 90% of total biodiversity loss**. On current trends, extraction is projected to reach **200 billion tonnes by the middle of the century**.

Extraction is not only about non-renewable resources. Ramping up biomass extraction means clearing forests, draining wetlands, and destroying habitats and carbon sinks; ramping up fossil fuel extraction means more emissions, ocean acidification and climate breakdown; and ramping up extraction of ores and construction materials means more pollution, waste and plastic downstream.

Some illustrative gaps between rich and poor:

- **Meat consumption:** around 17kg per year in Kenya versus 120kg in the US.
- **Plastic consumption:** around 136kg per year in Europe versus 16kg in Africa and the Middle East.
- **Material footprint per person:** around 28 tonnes a year in Europe and 35 tonnes in the US, against a sustainable level estimated around **8 tonnes per person per year**.
- **Consumption-based emissions overshoot** (measured against the 350ppm CO2 planetary boundary): the US is responsible for 40% of the global overshoot, Europe for 29%, and the Global North overall for 92%. Some countries, like India, remain below their allotted share.

In 2009, a team led by **Johan Rockström** at the Stockholm Resilience Centre, with **Paul Crutzen** (who coined the term "Anthropocene"), published the concept of **planetary boundaries**: the idea that Earth's biosphere can absorb significant pressure, but breaks down past certain thresholds. The nine boundaries are climate change, biodiversity loss, ocean acidification, land-use change, nitrogen and phosphorus loading, freshwater use, atmospheric aerosol loading, chemical pollution, and ozone depletion. Example thresholds include atmospheric carbon concentration not breaching 350ppm, species extinction rates staying below ten per million species per year, and forest land conversion not exceeding 25% of total land surface.

## Climate change is not fair

Data from the Climate Vulnerability Monitor shows the impacts of climate change fall overwhelmingly on the Global South, through drought, shifting rainfall patterns, landslides and fire:

- The Global South already bears **82% of the total cost of climate change**.
- In 2010, that cost was already **$571 billion**; by 2030 the South is projected to bear 92% of the (growing) cost.
- Around **400,000 people died in 2010** from climate-related causes, mostly from hunger, with 98% of those deaths in the Global South. By 2030 the estimate rises to around 530,000 deaths, 99% of them in the South.

As one summary of this research puts it: the excess emissions of a few rich nations harming billions of people in poorer nations amounts to a crime against humanity.

## Why technology alone will not save us

One widely discussed technological "fix" is **BECCS** (Bio-Energy Carbon Capture and Storage): producing energy by growing biomass, burning it for power, and capturing the resulting emissions, in theory making it carbon negative. It was never intended as a way to keep economies growing while continuing to breach carbon boundaries, but its inclusion as a prominent scenario in IPCC reports drew criticism from scientists over its cost and its externalities: biodiversity loss, soil pollution, fertiliser overuse and deforestation. To produce enough biomass to meet the scenarios used, researchers found it would require land equivalent to roughly three times the size of India, reducing global forest cover to just 10%, and diverting land needed for food production.

As former ExxonMobil CEO Rex Tillerson once put it: "Climate change is an engineering problem and it has an engineering solution." By 2018, the IPCC's own numbers made clear that engineering alone would not be enough: to keep within 1.5C of the Paris Agreement, global emissions need to be cut roughly in half by 2030 and to zero by 2050, reversing 250 years of fossil-fuelled infrastructure in under 30 years.

Clean energy deployment illustrates the scale of the challenge. The world is now producing **8 billion more megawatt-hours of clean energy each year than in 2000**, enough to power a country the size of Russia. But over the same period, economic growth increased overall energy demand by **46 billion megawatt-hours**, dwarfing the clean energy gains. If the economy keeps growing at its projected rate, it will double in size by 2050, roughly doubling extraction, production and consumption from today's levels. To keep under 1.5C, global emissions need to fall by around 14% a year, a target continuous growth makes far harder to hit.

The transition to renewable energy itself requires a dramatic increase in the extraction of metals and rare-earth minerals. A 2017 World Bank report modelled the material needed to build enough solar panels and wind turbines to produce an annual output of about 7 terawatts of electricity by 2050 (roughly half of the global economy's power needs). Scaled to the full economy, this implies extracting around **34 million tonnes of copper, 40 million tonnes of lead, 50 million tonnes of zinc, 162 million tonnes of aluminium and 4.6 billion tonnes of iron**, an increase of around **35% over current extraction levels for electricity generation alone**, before accounting for vehicles and other uses.

Looking at the more holistic **material footprint** measure (rather than emissions alone) shows that rich nations' material consumption has not been falling. In fact it has been rising faster than GDP growth in recent decades, meaning there has been no real decoupling on this measure. As a result, the UN Environment Programme has acknowledged that absolute decoupling of GDP from material use is not possible at a global scale, undercutting the core assumption of the green growth model.

This pattern has a name: the **Jevons paradox**. During the industrial revolution, economist William Stanley Jevons observed that James Watt's more efficient steam engine did not reduce overall coal consumption, as everyone had assumed it would; instead, consumption rose, because the money saved through efficiency was reinvested by capitalists to expand production, which grew the economy and increased coal use further. The same dynamic, Jevons-style, has recurred with technologies from cotton gins to fishing trawlers: efficiency gains tend to enable more extraction from nature, not less.

## GDP: a flawed measure of progress

**Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** measures the total market value of goods and services produced in a country over a period. It was institutionalised after the Second World War as a convenient aggregate indicator of economic activity and recovery, and over time governments elevated GDP growth to the central benchmark of success.

The economist **Simon Kuznets** (1901-1985) played a foundational role in formalising national income measurement in the 1930s and 1940s, yet he himself repeatedly warned that GDP is not a measure of social welfare, cautioning that "the welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income." GDP treats all market output equally: $100 of teargas adds the same to GDP as $100 of potatoes, regardless of their social impact. It also ignores non-market activities such as household care and volunteer work, externalities like pollution and resource depletion, and inequality in how income is shared.

A widely cited justification for growth is that it drove the historical rise in life expectancy. But the researcher most associated with that claim, Thomas McKeown, was not looking at long-term data when he made it, and he himself pointed to a different cause: **sanitation**, not GDP growth, explains much of the rise in life expectancy in the late 19th century. Empirical evidence repeatedly shows it is possible to achieve high levels of human development without high levels of GDP, and past a certain threshold, more growth can start having a negative impact on human welfare, a pattern that becomes visible when using alternative metrics such as the **Genuine Progress Indicator (GPI)**.

## 2026 update: "the obsession with endless growth can only end in tears"

In an August 2026 Guardian reader Q&A, environment writers **Jonathan Watts** and **Ajit Niranjan** put the degrowth critique in blunt terms: "The obsession with endless GDP growth on a finite planet can only end in tears. The only question is when." Watts pointed to Kate Raworth's *Doughnut Economics*, Bill McKibben's *Economics as if the World Mattered*, and Jason Hickel's degrowth scholarship as the main academic challenges to growth-first economics, and argued the world needs not just economic degrowth but **ecological regrowth**: a system of trade, taxation and incentives that rewards countries and companies for restoring damaged ecosystems, not just for producing more.

Watts also criticised mainstream climate economics as "absurdly complacent". Nobel laureate **William Nordhaus** was singled out, via commentator Genevieve Guenther, for modelling a low GDP hit from climate change while omitting the full costs to labour productivity, infrastructure, insurance and non-coastal real estate, and for using models that assume smooth, linear warming even though many climate scientists warn of tipping points that bring sudden jolts and acceleration instead.

## Book note: Less Is More, by Jason Hickel

Hickel's *Less Is More* defines degrowth as "a planned reduction of excess energy and resource use to bring the economy back into balance with the living world in a safe, just and equitable way." Crucially, this doesn't mean stopping production altogether: it means choosing which parts of the economy need to grow (clean energy, healthcare, essential services) and which don't (the SUV sector, private jets).

Hickel argues capitalism, which has existed for around 500 years and is often (wrongly) conflated with open markets that predate it by far longer, is fundamentally structured around constant growth over time. It can accelerate the deployment of clean energy, he writes, but "it does nothing to reverse deforestation, overfishing, soil depletion and mass extinction." In 2019, **11,000 scientists from 150 countries** published an article calling on governments to shift from pursuing GDP growth and affluence toward sustaining ecosystems and improving wellbeing.
