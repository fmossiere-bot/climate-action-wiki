# FACTS about Degrowth

## What is it

There are two competing schools of thought on how to solve the climate and nature crisis while keeping economies running. The **green growth** model holds that fast decarbonisation is possible without hurting economic growth, by decoupling emissions from GDP. The degrowth model argues that this is not enough. To fix our environmental and social problems, it says, wealthy economies need to shrink the amount of materials and energy flowing through them, rather than keep growing it. Both sides make convincing arguments and both have blind spots, which is why the debate stirs such strong feelings.

**Degrowth** (from the French *décroissance*) is a political and academic framework that questions the idea that endless economic growth is necessary, or even desirable, for human progress. It calls for a planned, democratic reduction of production and consumption in high-income countries, to bring the economy back into balance with ecological limits while pursuing social justice. Instead of measuring success by GDP, supporters of degrowth look at measures of wellbeing such as health, education and free time.

## Why it matters

How this debate is settled shapes almost every climate policy choice being made today, from the EU's growth-based climate laws to the assumptions built into influential decarbonisation models like Project Drawdown. Understanding both sides helps explain why some serious economists and scientists think today's climate targets can be met within capitalism, and why others think they cannot.

## Origins and core principles

The idea of *décroissance* began in France in the 1970s with thinkers like André Gorz and Serge Latouche. It reached the English-speaking world largely through the work of **Joan Martinez-Alier**, a leading figure in ecological economics and political ecology at the Autonomous University of Barcelona (UAB), where he helped set up the Institute of Environmental Science and Technology (ICTA-UAB). His work on environmental justice and his critique of endless growth made Barcelona a world centre for degrowth research and activism.

The movement's core principles are:

- **Ecological limits:** the Earth has finite resources, and endless growth on a finite planet is impossible and destructive.
- **Social justice:** degrowth aims to share out wealth and resources so that everyone's basic needs are met, including outside high-income countries.
- **Reduced throughput:** cutting the flow of materials and energy through the economy through circular economies, lower consumption and local production.
- **Redefining wellbeing:** putting human fulfilment and quality of life ahead of piling up possessions, including less work and more time for community and personal growth.

## Public support for degrowth ideas

The word "degrowth" is often misunderstood and can sound extreme, but the ideas behind it have wide public support:

- A survey across 34 European countries found that on average **61% favoured a "post-growth" approach**, a broader term for moving beyond GDP growth as the main goal.
- Polls in many countries, including G20 nations, found a majority believe the economy should put the health and wellbeing of people and nature ahead of profit and wealth.
- Polling in the US and Europe has repeatedly found that people think protecting the environment matters more than economic growth.
- A French study found **67% had a "favourable" opinion of the word "degrowth" itself**, with support across the political spectrum.
- A 2015 YouGov poll found **64% of people in Britain** think capitalism is unfair, compared with 55% in the US and 77% in Germany.
- The 2020 Edelman Trust Barometer found a majority agreeing that "capitalism does more harm than good", rising to **69% in France**.
- A 2019 European Council on Foreign Relations poll asked whether the environment should come first even if it damages economic growth. Large majorities (55-70%) said yes.
- More broadly, **70% of people in middle- and high-income countries** believe overconsumption is putting the planet and society at risk, and that owning less would not make them less happy or less well.

Despite this support, the movement still faces criticism over how a planned shrinking of the economy would affect jobs and public services.

## Green growth vs degrowth: two mental models

**The green growth model** says we should keep growing the economy, that we can decouple growth from emissions, and that today's capitalist model has the tools to do it. This model sits behind almost all climate policy today, including the US Inflation Reduction Act. It is also built into influential decarbonisation frameworks like Project Drawdown and marginal abatement cost (MAC) curves, which rank emission cuts by how much they cost.

There is real evidence that some countries are decoupling economic growth from carbon emissions. **Romania** is one of the clearest examples. The greenhouse gas intensity of its economy, net of land use, fell 88% between 1990 and 2023, which means each dollar of economic activity now heats the planet almost ten times less than it used to. Its total emissions have dropped 75% since the end of the communist era. Today Romania is building some of the world's largest solar farms, with up to 1GW of capacity. That change came at a cost: industries closed, farmers struggled, and the country moved towards a service economy, which happens to suit decarbonisation. Even when imported emissions are counted, the decoupling still holds. A 2023 study of 36 rich countries found 11 had fully broken the link between GDP and CO2, but **none had done so fast enough** to meet their share of the Paris Agreement's 1.5C target.

**The degrowth model** argues that the growth-based approach only makes progress at the edges, and that the growth model itself led us into the crisis. Jason Hickel, the best-known supporter of degrowth, argues that we cannot decarbonise fast enough while the economy keeps growing, because more growth means more energy demand. Degrowth would reduce that demand and speed up the transition, he says, and together with a shorter working week and living wages it could improve people's lives too. George Monbiot, an environmental writer for *The Guardian*, has made a similar case: "less of everything is the only way to avert catastrophe."

## Material footprint and planetary boundaries

Material extraction has grown enormously alongside GDP: from **7 billion tonnes in 1900**, to 35 billion tonnes in 1980, 50 billion tonnes by 2000, and **92 billion tonnes by 2017**. Scientists put a safe level of extraction at 50 billion tonnes a year, a limit the world passed in 2000. According to the United Nations, material extraction and processing cause **90% of total biodiversity loss**. On current trends, extraction is expected to reach 200 billion tonnes a year by the middle of the century.

Extraction is not only about non-renewable resources. Taking more biomass means clearing forests, draining wetlands, and destroying habitats and carbon sinks. Taking more fossil fuels means more emissions, ocean acidification and climate breakdown. Taking more ores and building materials means more pollution, waste and plastic further down the line.

Some examples of the gaps between rich and poor:

- **Meat consumption:** around 17kg per year in Kenya against 120kg in the US.
- **Plastic consumption:** around 136kg per year in Europe against 16kg in Africa and the Middle East.
- **Material footprint per person:** around 28 tonnes a year in Europe and 35 tonnes in the US, against a sustainable level estimated at around 8 tonnes per person per year.
- **Consumption-based emissions overshoot** (measured against the 350ppm CO2 planetary boundary): the US is responsible for 40% of the global overshoot, Europe for 29%, and the Global North as a whole for 92%. Some countries, like India, remain below their fair share.

In 2009, a team led by **Johan Rockström** at the Stockholm Resilience Centre, with Paul Crutzen (who coined the term "Anthropocene"), published the idea of planetary boundaries. Earth's living systems can absorb a lot of pressure, but they break down past certain thresholds. The nine boundaries are climate change, biodiversity loss, ocean acidification, land-use change, nitrogen and phosphorus loading, freshwater use, atmospheric aerosol loading, chemical pollution, and ozone depletion. Example thresholds include keeping atmospheric carbon below 350ppm, keeping species extinction rates below ten per million species per year, and keeping no more than 15% of the Earth's ice-free land converted to cropland.

## Climate change is not fair

Data from the Climate Vulnerability Monitor shows that the impacts of climate change fall overwhelmingly on the Global South, through drought, shifting rainfall, landslides and fire:

- The Global South already bears **82% of the total cost of climate change**.
- In 2010, that cost was already $571 billion. By 2030 the South is expected to bear 92% of the (growing) cost.
- Around **400,000 people died in 2010** from climate-related causes, mostly from hunger, with 98% of those deaths in the Global South. By 2030 the estimate rises to around 530,000 deaths, 99% of them in the South.

As one summary of this research puts it, the excess emissions of a few rich nations harming billions of people in poorer nations amount to a crime against humanity.

## Why technology alone will not save us

One much-discussed technological "fix" is **BECCS** (Bio-Energy Carbon Capture and Storage). It means growing biomass, burning it for power and capturing the emissions, which in theory removes more carbon than it releases. It was never meant as a way to keep economies growing while carbon limits are broken. But it featured prominently in IPCC scenarios, and scientists criticised it for its cost and its side effects: biodiversity loss, soil pollution, overuse of fertiliser and deforestation. Researchers found that growing enough biomass for those scenarios would take land roughly three times the size of India, cut global forest cover to just 10%, and take land needed to grow food.

Former ExxonMobil CEO Rex Tillerson once said: "Climate change is an engineering problem and it has an engineering solution." By 2018, the IPCC's own numbers showed that engineering alone would not be enough. To stay within the Paris Agreement's 1.5C, global emissions need to be cut roughly in half by 2030 and to zero by 2050. That means reversing 250 years of fossil-fuelled infrastructure in under 30 years.

Clean energy shows the size of the challenge. The world now produces **8 billion more megawatt-hours of clean energy each year than in 2000**, enough to power a country the size of Russia. Over the same period, economic growth pushed up total energy demand by **46 billion megawatt-hours**, far more than the clean energy gains. If the economy keeps growing at its expected rate, it will double in size by 2050, roughly doubling extraction, production and consumption from today's levels. To stay under 1.5C, global emissions need to fall by around 14% a year, and continuous growth makes that much harder.

The switch to renewable energy itself needs a big increase in mining for metals and rare-earth minerals. A 2017 World Bank report modelled the materials needed to build enough solar panels and wind turbines to produce about 7 terawatts of electricity a year by 2050 (roughly half of the global economy's power needs). Scaled up to the whole economy, this means extracting around 34 million tonnes of copper, 40 million tonnes of lead, 50 million tonnes of zinc, 162 million tonnes of aluminium and 4.6 billion tonnes of iron. That is an increase of around **35% over current extraction for electricity generation alone**, before counting vehicles and other uses.

The broader **material footprint** measure, which looks at everything consumed rather than emissions alone, shows that rich nations' material use has not been falling. In recent decades it has risen faster than GDP, so on this measure there has been no real decoupling. The UN Environment Programme has accepted that absolute decoupling of GDP from material use is not possible at a global scale, which undercuts the core assumption of the green growth model.

This pattern has a name: the **Jevons paradox**. During the industrial revolution, economist William Stanley Jevons noticed that James Watt's more efficient steam engine did not cut overall coal use, as everyone had expected. Coal use went up instead, because the money saved through efficiency was reinvested to expand production, which grew the economy and increased coal use further. The same thing has happened again and again, with technologies from cotton gins to fishing trawlers: efficiency gains tend to allow more extraction from nature, not less.

## GDP: a flawed measure of progress

**Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** measures the total market value of the goods and services a country produces over a period. It became standard after the Second World War as a handy single figure for economic activity and recovery, and over time governments made GDP growth the main yardstick of success.

The economist **Simon Kuznets** (1901-1985) played a central role in setting up national income measurement in the 1930s and 1940s. Yet he warned more than once that GDP does not measure social welfare: "the welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income." GDP counts all market output the same way. $100 of teargas adds as much to GDP as $100 of potatoes, whatever their effect on society. It also leaves out unpaid work such as caring at home and volunteering, side effects like pollution and resource depletion, and how unequally income is shared.

A common argument for growth is that it drove the historical rise in life expectancy. But the researcher most linked to that claim, Thomas McKeown, was not looking at long-term data when he made it, and he himself pointed to a different cause: **sanitation**, rather than GDP growth, explains much of the rise in life expectancy in the late 19th century. The evidence shows again and again that high levels of human development are possible without high GDP. Past a certain point, more growth can start to harm human welfare, a pattern that shows up when using other measures such as the Genuine Progress Indicator (GPI).

## 2026 update: "the obsession with endless growth can only end in tears"

In an August 2026 Guardian reader Q&A, environment writers **Jonathan Watts** and **Ajit Niranjan** put the degrowth critique bluntly: "The obsession with endless GDP growth on a finite planet can only end in tears. The only question is when." Watts pointed to Kate Raworth's *Doughnut Economics*, Bill McKibben's *Economics as if the World Mattered*, and Jason Hickel's work on degrowth as the main academic challenges to growth-first economics. He argued that the world needs ecological regrowth as well as economic degrowth: a system of trade, taxes and incentives that rewards countries and companies for restoring damaged ecosystems, rather than only for producing more.

Watts also called mainstream climate economics "absurdly complacent". Citing commentator Genevieve Guenther, he singled out Nobel laureate **William Nordhaus** for modelling a small hit to GDP from climate change while leaving out the full costs to labour productivity, infrastructure, insurance and inland property. Nordhaus's models also assume smooth, steady warming, even though many climate scientists warn of tipping points that bring sudden jolts and acceleration instead.

## Book note: Less Is More, by Jason Hickel

Hickel's *Less Is More* defines degrowth as "a planned reduction of excess energy and resource use to bring the economy back into balance with the living world in a safe, just and equitable way." This does not mean stopping production altogether. It means choosing which parts of the economy need to grow (clean energy, healthcare, essential services) and which do not (the SUV sector, private jets).

Hickel argues that capitalism, which has existed for around 500 years and is often wrongly confused with open markets that are far older, is built around constant growth. It can speed up the roll-out of clean energy, he writes, but "it does nothing to reverse deforestation, overfishing, soil depletion and mass extinction." In 2019, **11,000 scientists from 150 countries** published an article calling on governments to move away from chasing GDP growth and wealth, and towards protecting ecosystems and improving wellbeing.
