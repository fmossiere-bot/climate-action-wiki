# The Economics of Climate Change and Carbon Pricing

## What it is
Mainstream economics underlies virtually all climate policy made today, which makes it worth understanding both what conventional economics gets right about climate change and where it breaks down. **Climate change is, in economic terms, a textbook "market failure": the costs of greenhouse gas emissions are not captured in the price of the goods and services that cause them, so the free market has no built-in mechanism to stop the damage.** Carbon pricing, through taxes or trading schemes, is the primary tool economists have proposed to fix that failure, but real-world pricing schemes remain far too low and too limited to do the job on their own.

## Why it matters
Whether a government chooses to regulate emissions directly, price them through a tax or market, or do a mix of both has enormous consequences for how fast and how fairly a country decarbonises. Carbon pricing is popular, including with parts of the oil and gas industry, precisely because it can look like a tidy, market-based fix, but understanding its real-world track record, and the deeper debate about what economic growth should even be for, is essential to judging whether current policy is remotely adequate.

## Rethinking what economics is for
Conventional economics is, in its basic form, largely responsible for how we evaluate GDP (Gross Domestic Product), the standard indicator of a country's economic health, and much of the resulting policy debate concerns whether governments should act to slow inflation or stimulate the economy with more money. But GDP leaves a great deal out. Unpaid labour, caregiving and community work, along with what is sometimes called "social capital," are essential to how economies actually function, yet none of it shows up in GDP figures. Pursuing economic growth as a goal in itself is, in this view, too simplistic; the real goal should be **human wellbeing**, to which money contributes but which also depends heavily on non-monetary resources.

This raises real, unresolved questions: what target should replace GDP growth, and how do we even measure human wellbeing? There is a growing body of research trying to answer this, including efforts to recognise unpaid work, proposals for new forms of currency, and the broader field of **ecological economics**. Part of rethinking economic behaviour also means being honest about how people actually make decisions: with limited information, often following the crowd, and behaving in ways that are sometimes competitive and sometimes cooperative.

## Why climate change is a market failure
Conventional economics rests on the law of supply and demand: in a free market, buyers and sellers are left to work out the optimal price between them, at the point where it is profitable enough to bring supply to market and demand is high enough to absorb it. Environmental pollution introduces a serious complication into this otherwise self-regulating system, a category of problem economists call **market failure**.

The clearest illustration is the **tragedy of the commons**: the idea that "people can't really be trusted to act in the collective good" when a resource is shared and unowned, though this theory is itself debated. Market failures are often best explained through our collective abuse of resources that belong to no one in particular, such as the planet's atmosphere or its fish stocks. Take fishing: if some fishers stop because it is better for the fish population, others may not, and there is no individual incentive to hold back unless everyone participates together. This reflects what might be called an **exploitation mindset**, the pattern of taking as much as possible for individual benefit within an abusive economic system, a theme also explored on this wiki in [Facts on Everything: Under the Sky We Make](FACTS on EVERYTHING - Under the Sky we Make.md).

## Carbon pricing: markets and taxes
Governments have three broad tools available to correct a market failure like climate change: direct regulatory policies (such as bans), market-based policies (such as putting a price on carbon), or some combination of the two; the IPCC publishes a full summary table of the policy instruments available to governments.

**How carbon pricing works, in principle:** taxing carbon makes carbon-free alternatives more competitive, though prices for consumers inevitably rise as a result. A carbon trading scheme instead makes emitters pay for the extra carbon they use above an allotted cap, in theory giving policymakers more precise control over aggregate emissions. Whether either approach genuinely works in practice is contested; what is rarely discussed alongside these supply-side tools is the alternative of directly reducing demand for carbon-intensive goods and services in the first place.

The World Bank's *State and Trends of Carbon Pricing* report offers the clearest picture of where the global carbon market actually stands. As of the report, 68 carbon pricing initiatives existed worldwide, with three more scheduled for implementation, together covering 23% of global emissions. Global carbon pricing revenue had shot up by almost 60% in a single year, to around $84 billion. Despite this growth, prices remain far too low: updated estimates of the "right" price for carbon range from around $185 per tonne of CO2-equivalent, according to Resources for the Future, up to much higher figures still.

### Case study: the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS)
The EU ETS is the largest carbon trading system in the world, run as a cap-and-trade market. It has faced persistent challenges: an oversupply of permits, prices that have often been too low to change behaviour meaningfully, and ongoing questions about how much real emissions impact it has actually delivered.

## Why carbon pricing keeps falling short
Despite its popularity, a meta-analysis of existing schemes has found the real-world effect of carbon pricing and carbon exchanges on emissions has been very limited, largely because prices remain too low. One estimate suggests a carbon price would need to range from **$135 to $5,500 per tonne of CO2** by 2030, and from **$690 to $31,100 per tonne** by 2100, to reflect the true cost of the damage caused, figures far above anything currently implemented anywhere in the world.

Carbon pricing also runs into structural political pressure. Countries and regions more reliant on fossil fuels face greater pressure to delay implementation, carve out exceptions (aviation and shipping are common examples), or set the price too low to matter. That pressure comes both from industry lobbyists and from labour unions, as seen in France's Gilets Jaunes ("yellow vests") protests, which were triggered in large part by a proposed fuel tax increase. Even where a price is implemented, there is a risk of **carbon leakage**, where companies simply relocate their polluting activities to jurisdictions with weaker rules. And there are unavoidable equity and justice issues: because the cost of a carbon price is often passed on to consumers through higher energy bills, it tends to hit the poorest hardest, even though they typically contribute the least to the problem. Addressing climate change credibly will likely require a mix of new laws, regulations, subsidies, private investment and social tipping points working together, rather than carbon pricing alone.

## The Social Cost of Carbon
The **Social Cost of Carbon (SCC)** is an attempt to translate the damage from an additional tonne of CO2-equivalent into a single monetary figure, so that the cost of continuing to emit can be directly compared with the cost of cutting emissions. The SCC has significant limitations: the Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs) it typically relies on have foundational flaws, and by most accounts the true damage from emissions is greatly underestimated by current SCC figures.

An alternative approach, pursued by the **Climate Impact Lab**, uses empirically derived data rather than the standard IAM framework. Based on this model, some research estimates the SCC at $185 per tonne, roughly triple the previously recommended figure, a number that lines up closely with the "right" carbon price estimates cited above from Resources for the Future.

## Summary findings
- Conventional GDP-based economics leaves out unpaid labour and social capital, both essential to how economies function.
- 68 carbon pricing initiatives were in place worldwide, with 3 more scheduled, together covering 23% of global emissions (World Bank, State and Trends of Carbon Pricing).
- Global carbon pricing revenue rose almost 60% year-on-year to around $84 billion.
- The "right" price for carbon has been estimated at around $185 per tonne of CO2e by Resources for the Future, far above most existing schemes.
- A credible carbon price would need to reach $135-$5,500 per tonne by 2030 and $690-$31,100 per tonne by 2100 to reflect true damages.
- The Climate Impact Lab's empirically-derived Social Cost of Carbon estimate is around $185 per tonne, roughly triple earlier recommendations.
- The EU ETS, the world's largest carbon market, has struggled with permit oversupply and persistently low prices.
- France's Gilets Jaunes protests illustrate the political risk of carbon taxes that ignore distributional equity.
