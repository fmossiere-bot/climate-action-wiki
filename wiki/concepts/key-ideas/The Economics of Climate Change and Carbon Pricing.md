# The Economics of Climate Change and Carbon Pricing

## What it is
Almost all climate policy made today rests on mainstream economics, so it helps to understand what conventional economics gets right about climate change and where it breaks down. In economic terms, climate change is a textbook **market failure**. The cost of greenhouse gas emissions is not included in the price of the goods and services that cause them, so the free market has no built-in way to stop the damage. Carbon pricing, through taxes or trading schemes, is the main tool economists have proposed to fix that. But the schemes that exist today are priced far too low and cover too little to do the job on their own.

## Why it matters
Governments can regulate emissions directly, put a price on them through a tax or a market, or mix the two. That choice has huge consequences for how fast, and how fairly, a country cuts its emissions. Carbon pricing is popular, even with parts of the oil and gas industry, because it looks like a tidy, market-based fix. To judge whether current policy is anywhere near enough, it helps to know how carbon pricing has worked in practice, and to ask the deeper question of what economic growth is for.

## Rethinking what economics is for
In its basic form, conventional economics shapes how we judge GDP (Gross Domestic Product), the standard measure of a country's economic health. Much of the policy debate that follows is about whether governments should slow inflation or boost the economy with more money. But GDP leaves a lot out. Unpaid work, caring and community work, and what is sometimes called "social capital" (the networks and trust between people) are all essential to how economies run, yet none of it appears in GDP figures. In this view, chasing economic growth for its own sake is too simple. The real goal should be **human wellbeing**, which money helps with but which also depends heavily on things money cannot buy.

That raises questions nobody has fully answered yet. What should replace GDP growth as the target, and how do we measure wellbeing? A growing body of research is working on this, including efforts to recognise unpaid work, proposals for new kinds of currency, and the wider field of **ecological economics**. Rethinking economic behaviour also means being realistic about how people actually make decisions: with limited information, often following the crowd, and sometimes competing while at other times cooperating.

## Why climate change is a market failure
Conventional economics rests on the law of supply and demand. In a free market, buyers and sellers settle on the best price between them, at the point where it pays to bring goods to market and there is enough demand to buy them. Pollution throws a serious complication into this system, which otherwise regulates itself. Economists call this kind of problem a **market failure**.

The clearest example is the **tragedy of the commons**: the idea that "people can't really be trusted to act in the collective good" when a resource is shared and belongs to no one, though the theory itself is debated. Market failures are easiest to see in the way we collectively overuse things nobody owns, such as the atmosphere or fish stocks. Take fishing. If some fishers hold back to protect the fish population, others may not, and no single fisher has a reason to hold back unless everyone does. This reflects what could be called an exploitation mindset: taking as much as possible for yourself inside an economic system that rewards it. The idea is explored further in [Facts on Everything: Under the Sky We Make](FACTS%20on%20EVERYTHING%20-%20Under%20the%20Sky%20we%20Make.md).

## Carbon pricing: markets and taxes
Governments have three broad ways to correct a market failure like climate change: direct regulation (such as bans), market-based policies (such as putting a price on carbon), or a mix of the two. The IPCC publishes a full table of the policy tools available to governments.

**How carbon pricing works, in principle:** a tax on carbon makes carbon-free options more competitive, although prices for consumers go up as a result. A carbon trading scheme instead makes emitters pay for any carbon they use above a set cap, which in theory gives policymakers tighter control over total emissions. Whether either approach works in practice is disputed. What is rarely discussed alongside these tools is the other option: cutting demand for carbon-heavy goods and services in the first place.

The World Bank's *State and Trends of Carbon Pricing* report gives the clearest picture of where carbon pricing stands. At the time of the report there were **68 carbon pricing initiatives** worldwide, with three more due to start, together covering 23% of global emissions. Revenue from carbon pricing had jumped by almost 60% in a single year, to around $84 billion. Even so, prices are still far too low. Updated estimates of the "right" price for carbon start at around $185 per tonne of CO2-equivalent, according to Resources for the Future, and go much higher.

### Case study: the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS)
The EU ETS is the largest carbon trading system in the world, run as a cap-and-trade market (a limit on total emissions, with permits that companies can buy and sell). It has struggled with too many permits in circulation, prices that have often been too low to change behaviour, and continuing questions about how much it has actually cut emissions.

## Why carbon pricing keeps falling short
For all its popularity, a review of existing schemes found that carbon pricing and carbon trading have had very little real effect on emissions, mainly because prices are too low. One estimate suggests a carbon price would need to be between **$135 and $5,500 per tonne of CO2** by 2030, and between $690 and $31,100 per tonne by 2100, to reflect the real cost of the damage. Those figures are far above anything in place anywhere in the world.

Carbon pricing also faces steady political pressure. Countries and regions that depend more on fossil fuels come under more pressure to delay, to carve out exceptions (aviation and shipping are common ones), or to set the price too low to matter. That pressure comes from industry lobbyists and also from trade unions, as France's Gilets Jaunes ("yellow vests") protests showed. They were set off largely by a planned rise in fuel tax. Even where a price is in place, there is a risk of **carbon leakage**, where companies move their polluting activities to places with weaker rules. And there are unavoidable questions of fairness. The cost of a carbon price is often passed on to consumers through higher energy bills, so it tends to hit the poorest hardest, even though they usually contribute least to the problem. Tackling climate change properly will probably need new laws, regulations, subsidies, private investment and social tipping points working together, rather than carbon pricing alone.

## The Social Cost of Carbon
The **Social Cost of Carbon (SCC)** tries to turn the damage done by one extra tonne of CO2-equivalent into a single money figure. That way, the cost of carrying on emitting can be compared directly with the cost of cutting emissions. The SCC has serious limits. The Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs) it usually relies on have basic flaws, and most experts think current SCC figures greatly underestimate the real damage.

The **Climate Impact Lab** takes a different approach, using data from real-world observations instead of the standard IAM framework. Based on this model, some research puts the SCC at $185 per tonne, roughly triple the figure recommended before. That lines up closely with the Resources for the Future estimate of the "right" carbon price mentioned above.

## Summary findings
- Conventional GDP-based economics leaves out unpaid work and social capital, both essential to how economies function.
- 68 carbon pricing initiatives were in place worldwide, with 3 more scheduled, together covering 23% of global emissions (World Bank, State and Trends of Carbon Pricing).
- Global carbon pricing revenue rose almost 60% in a year, to around $84 billion.
- Resources for the Future estimates the "right" price for carbon at around $185 per tonne of CO2e, far above most existing schemes.
- To reflect the real damage, a carbon price would need to reach $135-$5,500 per tonne by 2030 and $690-$31,100 per tonne by 2100.
- The Climate Impact Lab's estimate of the Social Cost of Carbon, based on real-world data, is around $185 per tonne, roughly triple earlier recommendations.
- The EU ETS, the world's largest carbon market, has struggled with too many permits and prices that stay low.
- France's Gilets Jaunes protests show the political risk of carbon taxes that ignore who pays.
