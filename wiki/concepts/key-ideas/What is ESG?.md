# What is ESG?

## What is it
**ESG** stands for **Environmental, Social Responsibility and Governance**. These are the three "pillars" that ESG reporting frameworks ask companies to report against, covering the non-financial risks and opportunities inherent in a company's day-to-day activities. For investors, ESG data is meant to offer a view beyond a company's financial statements alone, looking instead at how robust, resilient and well-governed the underlying business actually is, alongside factors such as its commitment to renewable energy, its support for environmental causes, and whether its employees work under safe and fair conditions.

## Why it matters
ESG reporting increasingly determines whether large companies can raise capital, win investors, and avoid legal exposure over misleading sustainability claims. Understanding what ESG covers, and where it is heading in EU law, is essential background for judging whether a company's sustainability claims hold up. See [What is Materiality?](What%20is%20Materiality%3F.md) for how companies decide which ESG issues actually matter to their business.

## ESG reporting requirements under EU law
The EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (**CSRD**) requires large companies to report on the impact of climate change on their organisation, including external assurance on the information they provide. It extends sustainability reporting requirements to all large companies, whether listed or not, removing the previous 500-employee threshold: a company is in scope if it meets two of three criteria (revenues above €40 million, total assets above €20 million, or more than 250 employees), or if its securities are listed on an EU-regulated market. Companies already reporting under the older Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD) must transition to the CSRD, and subsidiaries of non-EU parent companies are also brought into scope. The CSRD works alongside the **EU Taxonomy**, a common classification of which economic activities count as environmentally sustainable; together, the two are designed to help redirect capital flows towards sustainable activities. See [What is CSRD?](../../legislation-policy/What%20is%20CSRD%3F.md) for the full detail on scope and timelines.

## Why net-zero strategy is now a business imperative
According to McKinsey, the transition to a net-zero economy has passed an inflection point from a business perspective. Large public companies face growing pressure to eliminate carbon emissions across their supply chains, operations, products and services, and capital providers face similar pressure over their financed emissions. That pressure is spreading from large public companies to smaller public companies and to private companies. More than half of global GDP is now generated in countries with net-zero mandates in place, and McKinsey argues that the distance between low-carbon leaders and high-carbon laggards will widen as competitive rules continue to shift.

## The rising wave of ESG litigation
Bloomberg reports that in-house legal teams globally are bracing for a wave of greenwashing litigation as disclosure requirements tighten. Norton Rose Fulbright's **2023 Annual Litigation Trends Survey**, covering more than 430 general counsel and in-house litigation leaders, found that **28% said their ESG dispute exposure increased in 2022**, and **24% expected it to deepen over the following 12 months**. The key drivers cited were the absence of clear ESG metrics and requirements, and heightened regulatory scrutiny. Only 8% of those surveyed said they were actually involved in ESG-related class actions in the previous year, but around 37% of those wary of future class actions viewed ESG as a "major driver" of litigation risk. Norton Rose disputes partner Rachel Roosth noted that ESG litigation risk is not limited to the energy industry: stakeholders are increasingly pushing companies in every sector for more information on topics such as waste management and diversity, equity and inclusion.

## Building ESG capability inside organisations
Deloitte's research on green workforce transformation argues that implementing an ESG strategy also requires building a "green workforce": staff across HR, finance, legal and IT capable of addressing green challenges such as climate change, biodiversity loss, pollution and waste. Deloitte's Maturity Matrix toolkit is designed to help organisations identify where they stand in that transformation and what blockers are stopping them progressing to the next level. Separately, professional services firm Crowe notes that developing an ESG strategy involves working through a range of considerations, including relevant standards, customer expectations, investor concerns, and funder or lender requirements, and that many companies not yet subject to mandatory ESG reporting are voluntarily taking steps such as paper-free workplaces, sustainable travel policies, remote-work options and more environmentally friendly purchasing.

One example of a company trying to lock in its sustainability commitments structurally, rather than relying on management goodwill, is Tony's Chocolonely, which has explored issuing a "golden share" that would act as a guardian over its sustainability efforts, making them harder for future shareholders to reverse.

## Summary findings
- More than half of global GDP is now generated in countries with net-zero mandates (McKinsey).
- 28% of 430+ general counsel surveyed reported increased ESG dispute exposure in 2022; 24% expected it to deepen (Norton Rose Fulbright, 2023 Annual Litigation Trends Survey).
- Only 8% of those surveyed were actually involved in ESG-related class actions, but 37% of the wary group saw ESG as a major litigation driver.
- The CSRD removes the previous 500-employee threshold, extending mandatory sustainability reporting to all large EU companies meeting two of three size criteria.
- The EU Taxonomy's reporting requirements began phasing in from January 2022, with fuller application from January 2023.
