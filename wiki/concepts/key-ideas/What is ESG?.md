# What is ESG?

## What is it
**ESG** stands for **Environmental, Social and Governance**. These are the three "pillars" that ESG reporting frameworks ask companies to report on. Together they cover the non-financial risks and opportunities that come with a company's day-to-day work. For investors, ESG data is meant to show more than the financial statements can: how solid, resilient and well run the business really is. It also covers things such as the company's commitment to renewable energy, its support for environmental causes, and whether its staff work in safe and fair conditions.

## Why it matters
ESG reporting has more and more influence on whether large companies can raise money, attract investors, and avoid legal trouble over misleading sustainability claims. Knowing what ESG covers, and where EU law is taking it, helps you judge whether a company's sustainability claims stand up. See [What is Materiality?](What%20is%20Materiality%3F.md) for how companies decide which ESG issues matter to their business.

## ESG reporting requirements under EU law
The EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (**CSRD**) requires large companies to report on how climate change affects their organisation, and to have that information checked by an outside auditor. It applies sustainability reporting to all large companies, listed or not, and drops the old 500-employee threshold. A company is covered if it meets two of three criteria (turnover above €40 million, total assets above €20 million, or more than 250 employees), or if its shares or bonds are listed on an EU-regulated market. Companies already reporting under the older Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD) must move to the CSRD, and subsidiaries of parent companies outside the EU are also covered. The CSRD works alongside the **EU Taxonomy**, a shared list of which economic activities count as environmentally sustainable. Together, the two are meant to steer money towards sustainable activities. See [What is CSRD?](../../legislation-policy/What%20is%20CSRD%3F.md) for full detail on who is covered and when.

## Why net-zero strategy now matters to business
According to McKinsey, the shift to a net-zero economy has reached a turning point for business. Large listed companies are under growing pressure to cut carbon emissions across their supply chains, operations, products and services, and the banks and investors that fund them face similar pressure over the emissions they finance. That pressure is now spreading to smaller listed companies and to private companies. **More than half of global GDP** is now produced in countries with net-zero targets in place. McKinsey argues that the gap between low-carbon leaders and high-carbon laggards will keep widening as the rules of competition change.

## The rising wave of ESG litigation
Bloomberg reports that in-house legal teams around the world are preparing for a wave of greenwashing lawsuits as disclosure rules tighten. Norton Rose Fulbright's 2023 Annual Litigation Trends Survey asked more than 430 general counsel and heads of in-house litigation. It found that **28% said their exposure to ESG disputes increased in 2022**, and 24% expected it to grow over the following 12 months. The main reasons they gave were the lack of clear ESG measures and requirements, and closer attention from regulators. Only 8% said they had actually been involved in ESG-related class actions in the previous year. But around 37% of those worried about future class actions saw ESG as a "major driver" of legal risk. Rachel Roosth, a disputes partner at Norton Rose, pointed out that ESG legal risk is not limited to the energy industry. Stakeholders in every sector are pushing companies for more information on topics such as waste management and diversity, equity and inclusion.

## Building ESG capability inside organisations
Deloitte's research on green workforce transformation argues that putting an ESG strategy into practice also means building a "green workforce": staff across HR, finance, legal and IT who can deal with green challenges such as climate change, biodiversity loss, pollution and waste. Deloitte's Maturity Matrix toolkit is designed to help organisations see where they stand and what is holding them back from reaching the next level. Separately, the professional services firm Crowe notes that building an ESG strategy means weighing up a range of things, including relevant standards, what customers expect, what investors are worried about, and what funders or lenders require. Many companies that do not yet have to report on ESG are taking voluntary steps, such as going paper-free, sustainable travel policies, remote working and greener purchasing.

Some companies try to build their sustainability commitments into their structure, so they do not depend on the goodwill of whoever is running the business. **Tony's Chocolonely** has explored issuing a "golden share" that would act as a guardian over its sustainability work, making it harder for future shareholders to reverse.

## Summary findings
- More than half of global GDP is now produced in countries with net-zero targets (McKinsey).
- 28% of 430+ general counsel surveyed said their exposure to ESG disputes increased in 2022, and 24% expected it to grow (Norton Rose Fulbright, 2023 Annual Litigation Trends Survey).
- Only 8% of those surveyed had actually been involved in ESG-related class actions, but 37% of the worried group saw ESG as a major cause of legal risk.
- The CSRD drops the old 500-employee threshold, making sustainability reporting mandatory for all large EU companies that meet two of three size criteria.
- The EU Taxonomy's reporting requirements began phasing in from January 2022, with fuller application from January 2023.
