# Carbon Emission Facts: Agriculture, Transport and Ocean Carbon

## Agriculture

Agriculture (together with forestry and other land use) is the third largest source of greenhouse gas emissions, after energy supply and industry and ahead of transport, according to the IPCC. Counting the wider food system, it accounts for at least 25% of all greenhouse gas emissions. Livestock are a large part of that. In the three regions responsible for 80% of all livestock emissions (Asia, South America and Africa), emissions from livestock rose 44% between 1990 and 2010. Livestock alone are responsible for **14.5% of total global greenhouse gas emissions**, mostly from milk and beef production. That is more than half of agriculture's share.

The most direct way to cut agriculture emissions is for people to eat less meat, which is why the move towards meat that does not come from slaughtered animals is worth watching. [A Guardian report](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2019/jun/12/most-meat-in-2040-will-not-come-from-slaughtered-animals-report?CMP=Share_iOSApp_Other) suggests the meat industry is heading clearly in that direction. Trade makes the picture more complicated. China imports 38% of Brazilian beef, and Brazilian beef is linked to the fires used to clear land in the Amazon. That raises the question of how to share responsibility for those emissions between the country that produces the beef and the one that buys it.

Around a third of cropland is used to grow animal feed. Around 17% of all fish caught worldwide never goes straight onto people's plates, and much of it is turned into fish meal for livestock and farmed fish. That is one reason newer food technology is being explored: capturing CO2 to feed bacteria, which can then be grown into protein for animal and fish feed. The idea dates back to the 1970s, but it has only recently started to pay its way, because fish meal (a powder made from fish parts) has become more expensive. [World Watch Institute's analysis of agriculture and livestock as major sources of greenhouse gas emissions](http://www.worldwatch.org/agriculture-and-livestock-remain-major-sources-greenhouse-gas-emissions-0) has more detail.

### Food Mileage

Food mileage, the distance food travels before it reaches your plate, does affect its carbon footprint, but not always in the way people expect. [Food Carbon Footprint](../../sectors/agriculture-food/Food%20Carbon%20FootPrint.md) goes into more detail. A comparison of potatoes grown and transported in different ways found that emissions from growing them were broadly similar, and transport made the real difference. Buying locally feels right, but in pure emissions terms a shared delivery system, such as a vegetable box delivered to the door, can have a smaller total footprint than lots of people each driving to a farm or shop.

## Transport

In the European Union, transport produces 27% of all carbon dioxide emissions, and almost half of that comes from private cars. It is the only sector whose CO2 emissions have risen over the past three decades, by over a quarter.

**Flying**: a comprehensive analysis found that flying's effect on global warming doubled between 2000 and 2018. Overall, flying is responsible for 3.5% of all the warming caused by human activity.

## Did You Know?

Most of the carbon stored by the natural world sits deep in the ocean. A tree can absorb around 20kg of CO2 a year, while a whale can store an average of 33 tonnes over its lifetime. Forests matter, and so do marine ecosystems.
