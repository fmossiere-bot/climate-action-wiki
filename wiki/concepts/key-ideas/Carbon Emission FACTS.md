# Carbon Emission Facts: Agriculture, Transport and Ocean Carbon

## Agriculture

Agriculture is the third largest contributor to carbon emissions, after energy production (coal) and transport, accounting for at least 25% of all greenhouse gas emissions. Livestock account for a substantial share of those emissions: in the three main regions responsible for 80% of total livestock emissions (Asia, South America and Africa), emissions from this source increased 44% between 1990 and 2010. Adding together all emissions related to livestock, they represent more than 80% of agriculture's total emissions, with livestock alone responsible for **14.5% of total global greenhouse gas emissions**, most of it stemming from milk and beef production.

An obvious way to reduce agriculture emissions is for people to reduce their consumption of meat, which is why the shift towards alternatives to slaughtered-animal meat is worth watching: [a Guardian report](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2019/jun/12/most-meat-in-2040-will-not-come-from-slaughtered-animals-report?CMP=Share_iOSApp_Other) points to a clear direction of travel for the meat industry. Trade adds another layer of complexity: 38% of Brazilian beef, production of which is linked to Amazon forest fires used to clear land, is imported by China, raising the question of how responsibility for those emissions should be split between producer and buyer.

Around a third of cropland is used to grow animal feed, and around 17% of all fish caught globally go to commercial fisheries rather than direct human consumption, much of it processed into fish meal for livestock and farmed fish. This is part of why newer food technology, capturing CO2 emissions to feed bacteria that can then be grown into protein for animal and fish feed, is being explored as part of the solution. The idea has existed since the 1970s, but has only recently become economically viable as fish meal, a powder made from fish parts, has grown more expensive. See [World Watch Institute's analysis of agriculture and livestock as major sources of greenhouse gas emissions](http://www.worldwatch.org/agriculture-and-livestock-remain-major-sources-greenhouse-gas-emissions-0) for more detail.

### Food Mileage

Food mileage, the distance food travels before it reaches a plate, matters for its carbon footprint but not always in the way people assume. See [Food Carbon Footprint](../../sectors/agriculture-food/DRAFT%20-%20Food%20Carbon%20FootPrint.md) for more detail. Comparing potatoes grown and transported differently found that production emissions were broadly similar between options, with transport making the real difference. Buying locally makes intuitive sense, but from a pure emissions standpoint, aggregated delivery systems such as vegetable box door deliveries can produce a smaller total footprint than the equivalent number of individual cars travelling to a farm or shop.

## Transport

Within the European Union, transport contributes 27% of overall carbon dioxide emissions, and almost half of that comes from private car use. Transport is the only sector that has seen its CO2 emissions rise over the past three decades, by over a quarter.

**Flying**: a comprehensive analysis found that the effect of flying on global warming doubled between 2000 and 2018. Overall, flying is responsible for 3.5% of the total global warming effect resulting from human activity.

## Did You Know?

Most of the carbon stored by the natural world sits in the depths of the ocean. Where a tree can absorb around 20kg of CO2 a year, a whale can store an average of 33 tonnes over its lifetime, underlining how much marine ecosystems, not just forests, matter for the carbon cycle.
