New York Attorney General Letitia James has turned her attention to JBS, the world's largest beef producer.

James is best known for winning a US$450 million fraud case against former President Donald Trump, and a major corruption case against the National Rifle Association (NRA).

> This case is about something different: greenwashing. ("Net-zero" means that, overall, the company would not add to the heat-trapping gases driving climate change.) Such ads, James says, "in effect, provide environmentally conscious consumers with a ‘license’ to eat beef."  

The problem, according to James, is that JBS makes these claims while knowing it has no realistic way of achieving them.  

JBS already has a troubled record. The company has paid hundreds of millions in fines in the US and Brazil for bribery and price-fixing. More than a dozen US senators tried to stop it listing on the New York Stock Exchange, pointing to its "extensive international corruption record."

So the case turns on the advertising. James argues that JBS promised "bacon, chicken wings and steak with net-zero emissions" without any credible plan to get there, and that the adverts give environmentally conscious shoppers a false licence to keep eating beef.

The science behind her case is hard to get around. Cattle produce methane, a potent greenhouse gas, as they digest their food, a process called enteric fermentation. Attempts to reduce it, such as feeding cows seaweed, have not yet worked at scale. JBS is also the largest buyer of cattle from the Amazon region, which makes it a major driver of deforestation there. In 2021 the company reported emissions of **71 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent**, more than Ireland's entire national emissions that year.

According to a 2023 FAO report, livestock and the land-use change linked to it caused around 12% of all human-caused greenhouse gas emissions in 2015. The lawsuit will ask a court to decide whether any technology or farming system could ever bring those emissions down to zero.

## 2025: a big year for climate litigation

2025 was the tenth anniversary of the Urgenda case, when a Dutch court first ordered a government to cut its emissions faster to protect its citizens from climate change. In the ten years since, climate cases have grown into a global body of law. The Guardian counted at least **13 significant wins in 2025 alone**, covering greenwashing verdicts, unlawful fossil fuel approvals and rulings from international courts.

The cases that changed the rules:

- **Rosebank and Jackdaw (UK)**: two North Sea oil and gas fields were ruled unlawful because their approval did not take into account the scope 3 emissions from burning the oil and gas they would produce.
- **TotalEnergies (France)**: a Paris court found that TotalEnergies's "reinvention" advertising campaign made misleading net zero claims while the company kept expanding fossil fuel production.
- **Apple Watch (Germany)**: a Frankfurt court ruled that Apple could not call its Watch carbon neutral. Apple then dropped the label in other markets too.
- **JBS and Tyson settlements (US)**: JBS paid $1.1 million to New York state, and Tyson agreed to drop its net zero 2050 claims. Both settled after lawsuits challenged false climate marketing in the meat industry.
- **EnergyAustralia (Australia)**: settled with Parents for Climate and publicly accepted that carbon offsets do not prevent or undo greenhouse gas damage. It was the first Australian greenwashing case against an energy utility.
- **Three Norwegian oilfields unlawful**: a Norwegian appeal court declared the licences for three North Sea fields illegal because they did not account for their full climate impact.
- **New South Wales coalmine expansion blocked**: the Mount Pleasant Optimisation project was annulled because it ignored scope 3 emissions (from burning the coal overseas).
- **International Court of Justice advisory opinion**: countries must prevent harm to the climate system, and failing to do so could leave them owing compensation.
- **Inter-American Court of Human Rights**: found that there is a human right to a stable climate, and that states have a duty to protect it.
- **UK forced to publish a tougher climate plan**: after its original net zero strategy was ruled unlawful, the government published a revised carbon budget delivery plan, committing to decarbonise electricity by 2030.

The pattern is clear. Courts are holding governments and companies to account for what they claim as well as for what they emit. Scope 3 emissions, the indirect emissions from using a company's products, used to be left out of environmental assessments. They are now a legal risk.

## July 2026 update: the first big legal challenge against a meat major (JBS)

On 22 July 2026, **Greenpeace International** filed a petition in the Dutch courts. It asks them to make JBS, the world's largest meat company, explain how its $6 billion global expansion plan fits with its responsibilities to the climate, nature and human rights under Dutch duty-of-care law. Greenpeace's lawyers described this as a first step towards formal litigation. Until now, cases like this have targeted fossil fuel companies almost exclusively, so lawyers see it as opening a new front.

### Why this case, why now

- JBS moved its headquarters to the Netherlands in 2025 so it could list on the NYSE. That gave it access to US financial markets, but it also placed it under the same Dutch duty-of-care rules already used against **Shell**.
- The petition targets a **$2.5bn investment in six meat-processing plants in Nigeria**, with 1.2 million hectares of land promised by Niger State. Civil society groups say the plan was decided with no consultation and no public cost-benefit analysis.
- JBS promised to clean up its Amazon supply chain by the end of 2025 and **missed the deadline**. It has also dropped its "net zero by 2040" pledge, and its latest sustainability report leaves out the indirect emissions from cows, which are the biggest part of its climate footprint.
- The case follows a pattern in Dutch and French courts. **TotalEnergies** was ordered in June 2026 to disclose climate risks linked to its products, and the Shell case brought by Milieudefensie is expected to be settled in early 2027.

### What the people involved say

**Richard Brown**, legal counsel for Greenpeace International, explains the reasoning behind bringing the case in the Netherlands: *"We say that if you come to the Netherlands, you have to play by Dutch rules and be held to account to Dutch standards. Ultimately this is about a global conglomerate and how its activities should be limited within safe planetary boundaries."*

**Elujulo Opeyemi**, of the Youth in Agroecology and Restoration Network in Nigeria, speaks for the communities who would live next to the new plants: *"This is the biggest agriculture investment that I have heard of in Nigeria's history but there have been few details and no consultation for civil society. Once JBS has a foothold in Nigeria, they can encroach across Africa."*

### Why it matters beyond JBS

Farming is the biggest driver of deforestation and one of the largest sources of methane. If Dutch courts apply the same duty of care to a meat company that they already apply to fossil fuel firms, the whole farming sector will face the legal test that has already changed how oil and gas companies talk about their climate risk. That could reach every large agribusiness with a European headquarters or a European stock listing.
