# Can Everyone Live a 'Good Life' Within Planetary Limits? Guimarães as a Working Case

## What is it

Two ideas that used to sit in different silos are now converging in the sustainability literature: **planetary boundaries** (there are physical limits within which humanity can safely operate) and **sufficiency** (there is a level of material consumption above which additional wealth stops improving people's lives). Put together, they suggest something the growth-based economy has struggled to name: **a floor of decent living plus a ceiling of maximum consumption — a "consumption corridor" — that everyone can walk along together**. This page uses New Scientist's July 2026 feature on the Portuguese city of **Guimarães** as the working case study.

## Why it matters

Two numbers frame the argument:

- **Humanity has now crossed seven of nine planetary boundaries** (climate change, ocean acidification, biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater-system change, novel entities, nutrient cycles). Only ozone depletion and atmospheric aerosol pollution remain inside safe limits.
- **The growth rate of consumption is four times higher in countries that already have surplus consumption** than in countries below decent living standards (Millward-Hopkins et al., 2025). At current rates, decent living standards will not be fully achieved in lower-income countries until the end of the century.

In other words, the countries that most need more are the ones getting less; the countries that already have more than enough are the ones still growing fastest. The sustainability question is not "how do we grow the world's economy," it is "**how do we lift the floor while lowering the ceiling**."

## Nine boundaries, seven breached

The **planetary boundaries** concept was introduced in a **landmark 2009 paper by Johan Rockström** and colleagues at the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research. They identified nine limits — such as climate change, ocean acidification and land-system change — that if breached will knock humanity out of the stable Holocene conditions of the past 12,000 years.

By 2025, seven of those nine boundaries had been transgressed. Some of the trends behind this are dramatic:

- **Total domestic water use has increased sixfold** over the past 50 years — faster than either agriculture or industry
- **Overall extraction of natural resources and energy use has tripled** over a similar period

## Sufficiency: decent living standards, and "useless overconsumption"

**Joel Millward-Hopkins** (University of Lausanne) coined the working vocabulary that most current sustainability policy discussions now use:

- **Decent living standards**: a basic floor for well-being — nutrition, housing, healthcare, education — plus a buffer to address inequality
- **Useless overconsumption**: additional consumption above that floor whose contribution to well-being is essentially zero
- **Consumption corridor**: the space between the floor and a collectively-agreed ceiling, within which societies can operate without breaching planetary limits

Above a certain level, **additional wealth has little impact on well-being** ([research consistently shows this](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797614568324)). The reasonable-sounding conclusion is that a ceiling on consumption in the wealthiest countries is not a sacrifice — it is a redirection of resources towards the things that actually matter to well-being: healthcare, education, secure housing, time.

## Guimarães as a working example

Guimarães is a small city in northern Portugal, wealthy by Portuguese standards, with a medieval old town. A decade ago **Sara Pires** at the University of Aveiro ran an ecological-footprint assessment of the city: **if everyone in the world lived like a Guimarães resident, we would need the resources of almost 2.3 Earths**. (The global average is ~1.7 Earths.) The result convinced city leaders to sign up as a **"One Planet City"** — a Bioregional/WWF designation committing to reach a one-Earth footprint by 2050.

Since then, the city has:

- Set up the **Landscape Laboratory** in an old textile factory, in partnership with the University of Minho and University of Trás-os-Montes e Alto Douro
- Introduced **pay-as-you-throw waste collection** (charges per kg)
- Run regular **repair fairs** to extend the life of electronics and other goods
- Reached a **waste circularity index 2.5× the Portuguese average**
- **Reduced average waste per person by 18% since 2018**, against global trends
- Restored **95 hectares of green space since 2012**
- Revived a **circular textile industry** (RDD Textiles, Zouri shoes) using recycled materials and natural dyes — over **20 tonnes of textiles recovered annually**
- Built **floodwater retention basins** combining wetlands, meadows and retaining walls — held water during Portugal's deadly January 2026 storms while nearby areas flooded
- Turned **Bairro C** (a UNESCO World Heritage neighbourhood) into a testing ground for zero-carbon policies
- **Codified sustainability commitments into law**, so that a new mayor cannot easily undo them

In 2026, the EU awarded Guimarães the title of **European Green Capital**.

## The two things Guimarães has that others could copy

- **A research-and-action institution** (the Landscape Laboratory) that gives the city a permanent capacity to design, measure and adjust
- **Legal codification** of sustainability commitments, insulating the programme from political reversal. As Sara Pires puts it: *"It's almost impossible for a new mayor to come and destroy that."*

## Social tipping points

**Tim Lenton** at the University of Exeter frames what happens in cities like Guimarães as **social tipping points**: when a threshold of attitude and behaviour change is crossed, wider change becomes rapid and self-sustaining. A vital shift, when it comes to creating a ceiling on consumption, is **what a society counts as a luxury versus a necessity**. The examples from Guimarães suggest some of those shifts can be organic rather than coercive. Sandra Freitas, a local resident: *"We don't need too much food in our freezer or too much money in our pockets, and we don't need to have 10 cars."*

## The awkward finding about wealthy environmentalists

New Scientist notes a recent study finding that **among the wealthiest 30% of people, those who cared most about the environment had an even larger footprint than their peers** — echoing the [high-income carbon paradox](../behavior-change/high-income-carbon-paradox.md) already on the wiki. Millward-Hopkins on why: extreme wealth **insulates** people from the economic pressures that might otherwise change behaviour. When fuel prices rise, poor households cut consumption; the very rich do not.

## The wider vision

In **June 2026, the World Inequality Lab published its Global Justice Report**, which argues that by embracing sufficiency and reshaping economies towards the real sources of human well-being, we can avoid climate breakdown. Its centrepiece is a **global wealth tax on billionaires** to fund the transition to greener energy and a dramatic increase in spending on education and healthcare. The report calculates that **average per-capita income across all countries could rise to around €5,000 per month by the end of the century** while staying within planetary limits.

**Cornelia Mohren**, the Lab's environmental coordinator: *"The climate crisis and inequality are very much intertwined. We think that it's necessary to make those pay who caused the climate crisis, which are the global rich."*

## Key facts

- **Seven of nine planetary boundaries crossed** (2025)
- Consumption growth rate **4× higher in surplus-consumption countries** than in countries below decent living standards
- Guimarães ecological footprint: **~2.3 Earths per resident**
- Guimarães waste circularity index: **2.5× the Portuguese average**
- Guimarães per-capita waste: **–18% since 2018**
- Guimarães green space restored: **95 hectares since 2012**
- Under the Global Justice Report scenario, average per-capita income could reach **~€5,000/month** globally by end of century within planetary limits
