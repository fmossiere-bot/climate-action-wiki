# Can Everyone Live a 'Good Life' Within Planetary Limits? Guimarães as a Working Case

## What is it

Two ideas that used to be discussed separately are now coming together in sustainability research. The first is **planetary boundaries**: there are physical limits within which humanity can safely operate. The second is sufficiency: above a certain level of material consumption, extra wealth stops making people's lives better.

Put together, they point to something a growth-based economy has found hard to name. There is a floor of decent living and a ceiling of maximum consumption, and the space between them is often called a "consumption corridor" that everyone could share. This page uses New Scientist's July 2026 feature on the Portuguese city of Guimarães as a working case study.

## Why it matters

Two numbers frame the argument:

- **Humanity has now crossed seven of nine planetary boundaries**: climate change, ocean acidification, biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater-system change, novel entities and nutrient cycles. Only ozone depletion and atmospheric aerosol pollution remain inside safe limits.
- Consumption is growing **four times faster** in countries that already consume more than they need than in countries below decent living standards (Millward-Hopkins et al., 2025). At current rates, lower-income countries will not fully reach decent living standards until the end of the century.

So the countries that most need more are getting less, and the countries that already have more than enough are still growing fastest. The question then shifts from how to grow the world economy to how to lift the floor while lowering the ceiling.

## Nine boundaries, seven breached

The planetary boundaries idea was introduced in a **2009 paper by Johan Rockström** and colleagues at the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research. They identified nine limits, such as climate change, ocean acidification and land-system change. Breaching them risks pushing humanity out of the stable Holocene conditions of the past 12,000 years.

By 2025, seven of those nine boundaries had been crossed. Some of the trends behind this are large:

- Total domestic water use has increased **sixfold** over the past 50 years, faster than either agriculture or industry
- Overall extraction of natural resources and energy use has **tripled** over a similar period

## Sufficiency: decent living standards, and "useless overconsumption"

**Joel Millward-Hopkins** (University of Lausanne) came up with the working vocabulary that most current policy discussions on sustainability now use:

- **Decent living standards**: a basic floor for well-being (nutrition, housing, healthcare, education), plus a buffer to address inequality
- **Useless overconsumption**: extra consumption above that floor which adds almost nothing to well-being
- **Consumption corridor**: the space between the floor and a ceiling agreed by society, within which people can live without breaching planetary limits

Above a certain level, extra wealth does little for well-being, and [research consistently shows this](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797614568324). It follows that a ceiling on consumption in the wealthiest countries need not feel like a sacrifice. It moves resources towards the things that matter most for well-being: healthcare, education, secure housing and time.

## Guimarães as a working example

Guimarães is a small city in northern Portugal, wealthy by Portuguese standards, with a medieval old town. A decade ago **Sara Pires** at the University of Aveiro measured the city's ecological footprint. If everyone in the world lived like a Guimarães resident, we would need the resources of almost **2.3 Earths**. (The global average is about 1.7 Earths.) That result persuaded city leaders to sign up as a "One Planet City", a Bioregional and WWF designation that commits a city to a one-Earth footprint by 2050.

Since then, the city has:

- Set up the **Landscape Laboratory** in an old textile factory, in partnership with the University of Minho and the University of Trás-os-Montes e Alto Douro
- Introduced pay-as-you-throw waste collection, which charges per kg
- Run regular repair fairs to extend the life of electronics and other goods
- Reached a waste circularity index **2.5×** the Portuguese average
- Cut average waste per person by **18% since 2018**, against global trends
- Restored **95 hectares** of green space since 2012
- Revived a circular textile industry (RDD Textiles, Zouri shoes) using recycled materials and natural dyes, recovering over **20 tonnes** of textiles a year
- Built floodwater retention basins that combine wetlands, meadows and retaining walls. They held water during Portugal's deadly January 2026 storms while nearby areas flooded
- Turned Bairro C, a UNESCO World Heritage neighbourhood, into a testing ground for zero-carbon policies
- Written its sustainability commitments into law, so that a new mayor cannot easily undo them

In 2026, the EU named Guimarães **European Green Capital**.

## What other cities could copy

- **A research-and-action institution.** The Landscape Laboratory gives the city a permanent way to design, measure and adjust its policies.
- **Commitments written into law.** This protects the programme from being reversed when politics changes. As Sara Pires puts it: *"It's almost impossible for a new mayor to come and destroy that."*

## Social tipping points

**Tim Lenton** at the University of Exeter describes what is happening in cities like Guimarães as social tipping points. Once enough people change their attitudes and behaviour, wider change can happen quickly and keep itself going. For a ceiling on consumption, one of the most important shifts is in what a society counts as a luxury and what it counts as a necessity. The Guimarães examples suggest some of those shifts can come about naturally rather than being forced. Sandra Freitas, a local resident, says: *"We don't need too much food in our freezer or too much money in our pockets, and we don't need to have 10 cars."*

## The awkward finding about wealthy environmentalists

New Scientist mentions a recent study which found that **among the wealthiest 30% of people**, those who cared most about the environment had an even larger footprint than their peers. This matches the [high-income carbon paradox](../behavior-change/high-income-carbon-paradox.md) already covered on the wiki. Millward-Hopkins explains why: great wealth shields people from the economic pressures that might otherwise change their behaviour. When fuel prices rise, poorer households cut back. The very rich do not.

## The wider vision

In **June 2026**, the World Inequality Lab published its Global Justice Report. It argues that by embracing sufficiency and reshaping economies around the real sources of human well-being, we can avoid climate breakdown. Its main proposal is a global wealth tax on billionaires, to pay for the switch to greener energy and a large rise in spending on education and healthcare. The report calculates that average income per person across all countries could rise to around **€5,000 per month** by the end of the century while staying within planetary limits.

Cornelia Mohren, the Lab's environmental coordinator, says: *"The climate crisis and inequality are very much intertwined. We think that it's necessary to make those pay who caused the climate crisis, which are the global rich."*

## Key facts

- Seven of nine planetary boundaries crossed (2025)
- Consumption growing **4× faster** in countries with surplus consumption than in countries below decent living standards
- Guimarães ecological footprint: about **2.3 Earths** per resident
- Guimarães waste circularity index: **2.5×** the Portuguese average
- Guimarães waste per person: **down 18%** since 2018
- Guimarães green space restored: **95 hectares** since 2012
- Under the Global Justice Report scenario, average income per person could reach about **€5,000 a month** worldwide by the end of the century, within planetary limits
