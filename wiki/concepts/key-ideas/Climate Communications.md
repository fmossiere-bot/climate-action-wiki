# Climate Communications: How to Talk About Climate Change So People Listen

## What it is
Climate conversations are not easy, but they are vital, and how you communicate about climate change matters as much as what you say. Effective climate communication means listening first, finding common ground with your audience, and only then advocating for action. **There is no single universally correct way to talk about climate change: different approaches work in different settings, and the biggest mistake most people make is leading with facts rather than connecting with the person in front of them first.**

## Why it matters
Facts alone rarely change minds. Climate scientist Dr Katharine Hayhoe, whom The New York Times has called "one of the nation's most effective communicators on climate change," argues that a worthwhile climate conversation depends less on the statistical evidence you present than on finding a shared value with the person you are talking to. Knowing how to do that, whether in a family conversation, a workplace, or with a journalist, is a genuinely learnable skill, and one that matters because the alternative, a purely fact-based or fear-based approach, tends to shut people down rather than move them to act.

## Know your audience before you speak
The starting point for any climate conversation is asking why you want to have it. In almost every case, the underlying goal is the same: **you want people to do something about it**. That raises further questions worth sitting with before the conversation happens: which people specifically do you hope will act, and why them? Are you speaking to someone you already share a connection with, or someone whose actions carry outsized weight? What action do you actually hope they take after hearing you? There is no need to have firm answers to all of these in advance, but they shape how the conversation should go.

A common pitfall is bombarding an audience with a relentless stream of facts. Facts matter, but on their own they are often insufficient to change minds or behaviour. The better starting point is connection, not a lecture. As Hayhoe writes in her 2021 book *Saving Us: A Climate Scientist's Case for Hope and Healing in a Divided World*, "it's nearly impossible to make someone care about climate change for the same reasons I do. But I don't have to, and you don't, either." Having a worthwhile conversation, she argues, hinges less on the strength of your evidence than on bonding over a shared value, which requires knowing, or being willing to learn, something about your audience: what they already care about, how climate change connects to those existing priorities, and the language they actually use. Farmers, for instance, often respond poorly to the term "climate change" itself, so more effective communicators talk instead about long-term trends and climate variability. One of Hayhoe's specific strategies is inviting people to share their own observations, which helps connect the abstraction of climate change to the reality of their own lives: "the 2011 drought in Texas was a game changer," she has said. "Everyone has a story now, where they didn't fifteen years ago."

## Speak your audience's language
The words you choose say a great deal about you and shape how your audience perceives you. A 2019 Columbia University report, *The Psychology of Climate Change Communication*, advises that "when talking to the general public, research shows that communicators should, whenever possible, avoid using jargon, complicated scientific terms, and acronyms," and that where a scientific term truly is necessary, it should be thoroughly defined rather than left to speak for itself, since stringing together too much technical vocabulary can cause an audience to spend its energy decoding words instead of absorbing the point. A simple practical check when editing for a broad audience: read the draft aloud. A sentence so long it leaves you gasping for breath, or a phrase that trips you up every time, is usually a sign that it needs simplifying.

Not everyone in an audience is equally reachable. Yale's "Global Warming's Six Americas" research tracks a spectrum from alarmed and concerned through cautious, disengaged, doubtful, and finally **dismissive**. The share of dismissive and doubtful people has not shifted much over time, but the concerned and alarmed segments have grown, which has translated into more climate action overall relative to the cautious middle. Communications experts generally advise against spending much energy trying to win over outright dismissives and deniers, since research shows they are close to impossible to influence once their minds are made up; political will is better built by focusing on everyone else. This matters because the "everyone else" group is large: the World Risk Poll, surveying 150,000 people across 142 countries, found 69% said climate change is a somewhat or very serious threat, and just 13% said it was not a threat at all.

## Lead with solutions, not just the problem
Messages that dwell on the scale of the crisis while offering little in the way of solutions have a well-documented tendency to shut people down rather than mobilise them, a problem that is not unique to climate coverage but is especially visible there. Journalist Amanda Ripley, reflecting in The Washington Post on why news coverage often alienates people, singled out The New York Times' 2021 multimedia project "Postcards from a World on Fire," which opened with an image of the Earth in flames and the line "cities swallowed by dust, human history drowned by the sea." As Ripley put it: "this was a well-intentioned effort, but it was simply not designed for humans." Of all the climate stories aired on nightly news and Sunday morning shows in the US in 2021, only a third discussed possible solutions at all, according to a study by Media Matters for America. Coverage that does include agency and hope, whether a newspaper feature on concrete solutions or a TikTok creator celebrating incremental environmental wins, tends to land better. Grassroots organising and "power-mapping," identifying which people or groups are worth focusing your effort on, is one practical way to translate this insight into action.

In corporate contexts, the advice converges on the same theme: **keep it simple and clear**, as Vance Merolla, global sustainability director for Colgate-Palmolive, puts it. Merolla favours visuals and checklists that distil complex requirements, such as reducing hundreds of pages of green building guidance into a short, skimmable checklist for engineers and architects, so that colleagues can grasp the essential point without wading through the underlying detail. While he believes companies rarely lie outright about their environmental practices, he cautions that word choice matters enormously and that it is essential to tell the whole story rather than cherry-pick favourable results.

If you find yourself speaking to a journalist, it is worth asking your own questions early: what the story is about, and whether it might involve video or other formats you should know about in advance. It also helps to establish ground rules. "On background" means you can be paraphrased but not quoted verbatim or named; "off the record" means the information should not be repeated at all. The safe default is to assume everything you say is on the record unless a reporter explicitly agrees otherwise. Speaking conversationally, rather than reciting a rehearsed statement, generally produces better quotes, and if you are uneasy speaking to the press, it is entirely reasonable to say "not right now" rather than shutting the door for good.

Simplicity itself is a communication strategy. The IPCC's 2021 Summary for All, a plain-language distillation of its Sixth Assessment Report, runs to just 16 pages and features as many illustrative doodles as charts, proof that even the most technical climate science can be made genuinely accessible.

## Personal conversations and the psychology of change
Talking about climate change with people around you can lead to real change, and doing it well follows a fairly consistent pattern: share your own personal connection to the issue; link it to what already matters to the other person, whether family, the economy, or health; look for common ground in shared values; point to impacts that are already visible now, rather than only distant future ones; make the positive case for clean energy's economic and health benefits; mention the direct cost savings of sustainable choices; convey genuine urgency without descending into fatalism; share concrete examples of climate action that has worked; and talk about how today's choices shape tomorrow.

There is real evidence for why small-scale, personal conversations matter so much. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report suggests that shifting societal norms may require only 10-30% of individuals to be deeply committed, a claim reinforced by researcher Damon Centola's finding that once roughly 25% of a group adopts a new behaviour or belief, the majority is likely to follow, a pattern sometimes called the "25% Revolution."

Several well-established cognitive biases explain why humans generally struggle to act on a threat like climate change, and understanding them can make conversations more effective. **Hyperbolic discounting** is the tendency to value the present far more than the future, a legacy of an evolutionary history where immediate threats mattered more than slow-moving ones. Humans also show a **lack of concern for generations beyond our own immediate family span**, roughly from great-grandparents to great-grandchildren, which makes sacrifices for the more distant future feel less urgent than they should. The **bystander effect** leads people to assume someone else, often "the leaders," must already be handling the crisis, a bias that grows stronger the larger the group involved. And the **sunk-cost fallacy** keeps people and institutions locked into a course of action, such as continued reliance on fossil fuels, simply because of how much has already been invested in it, even against decades of evidence that a transition is both possible and necessary.

Small groups behave differently from large, anonymous ones. Research on "Dunbar's number" finds that an individual can maintain stable relationships with roughly 150 other people; beyond that threshold, trust and the ability to rely on others' cooperation begins to erode. Exposure Labs, the film company behind *Chasing Ice* and *Chasing Coral*, has used this insight deliberately, screening its films in small, mixed groups, for instance in South Carolina, a US state with many prominent climate-denying leaders, bringing together people from agriculture, fisheries and tourism to discuss how climate change affects them personally. This approach draws on two further psychological mechanisms: the **endowment effect**, where people value a solution more highly when they helped come up with it themselves, and **social comparison theory**, where people calibrate their own behaviour by looking at others around them, the same logic behind utility programmes that show households how their energy use compares with their neighbours', which research has found measurably reduces consumption.

Finally, framing matters. People respond better to positively framed messages, such as "a clean energy future will save lives," than to purely negative ones, such as warnings of extinction, meaning how a message is framed shapes whether it produces action or paralysis.
