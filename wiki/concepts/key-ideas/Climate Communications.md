# Climate Communications: How to Talk About Climate Change So People Listen

## What it is
Climate conversations are not easy, but they matter, and how you talk about climate change counts as much as what you say. Good climate communication means listening first, finding common ground, and only then making the case for action. There is no single right way to do it. Different approaches work in different settings. The most common mistake is to **lead with facts** before making a connection with the person in front of you.

## Why it matters
Facts alone rarely change minds. Climate scientist Dr Katharine Hayhoe, whom The New York Times has called "one of the nation's most effective communicators on climate change," argues that a useful climate conversation depends less on the evidence you bring than on finding a value you share with the other person. You can learn to do this, whether in a family chat, at work, or with a journalist. It is worth learning, because the alternatives, a pure stream of facts or a message built on fear, tend to shut people down rather than move them to act.

## Know your audience before you speak
Start by asking yourself why you want to have the conversation. Almost always, the underlying goal is the same: **you want people to do something about it**. That leads to more questions worth thinking about beforehand. Which people do you hope will act, and why them? Are they people you already have a connection with, or people whose actions carry extra weight? What do you hope they will do after hearing you? You don't need firm answers to all of these, but they shape how the conversation should go.

A common pitfall is to bombard people with facts. Facts matter, but on their own they rarely change minds or behaviour. Connection is a better starting point than a lecture. As Hayhoe writes in her 2021 book *Saving Us: A Climate Scientist's Case for Hope and Healing in a Divided World*, "it's nearly impossible to make someone care about climate change for the same reasons I do. But I don't have to, and you don't, either." A good conversation, she argues, rests less on the strength of your evidence than on bonding over a shared value. That means knowing, or being willing to learn, something about your audience: what they already care about, how climate change touches those priorities, and the words they use. Farmers, for example, often react badly to the phrase "climate change" itself, so people who talk with them well speak instead about long-term trends and climate variability. One of Hayhoe's own approaches is to invite people to share what they have noticed themselves, which links an abstract idea to their daily lives: "the 2011 drought in Texas was a game changer," she has said. "Everyone has a story now, where they didn't fifteen years ago."

## Speak your audience's language
The words you choose say a lot about you and shape how people see you. A 2019 Columbia University report, *The Psychology of Climate Change Communication*, advises that "when talking to the general public, research shows that communicators should, whenever possible, avoid using jargon, complicated scientific terms, and acronyms." Where a scientific term really is needed, it should be clearly defined. Too much technical vocabulary makes listeners spend their energy decoding words instead of taking in the point. A simple test when writing for a broad audience is to read the draft aloud. If a sentence leaves you out of breath, or a phrase trips you up every time, it probably needs simplifying.

Not everyone is equally reachable. Yale's "Global Warming's Six Americas" research tracks a range of attitudes: alarmed, concerned, cautious, disengaged, doubtful and, at the far end, **dismissive**. The share of dismissive and doubtful people has hardly moved over time, but the concerned and alarmed groups have grown, which has meant more climate action overall compared with the cautious middle. Communication experts generally advise against spending much energy on outright dismissives and deniers, because research shows they are close to impossible to persuade once their minds are made up. Political will is better built by focusing on everyone else, and that is a large group. The World Risk Poll, which surveyed 150,000 people across 142 countries, found that 69% see climate change as a somewhat or very serious threat, and just 13% see it as no threat at all.

## Lead with solutions, not only the problem
Messages that dwell on the size of the crisis and offer little in the way of solutions tend to shut people down rather than get them moving. This is not unique to climate coverage, but it shows up clearly there. Journalist Amanda Ripley, writing in The Washington Post about why news coverage often puts people off, pointed to The New York Times' 2021 multimedia project "Postcards from a World on Fire." It opened with an image of the Earth in flames and the line "cities swallowed by dust, human history drowned by the sea." As Ripley put it: "this was a well-intentioned effort, but it was simply not designed for humans." According to a study by Media Matters for America, only a third of the climate stories aired on US nightly news and Sunday morning shows in 2021 discussed any possible solutions. Coverage that gives people a sense of agency and hope, whether a newspaper feature on practical solutions or a TikTok creator celebrating small environmental wins, tends to land better. Grassroots organising and "power-mapping", working out which people or groups are worth focusing your effort on, is one practical way to put this into action.

In companies, the advice is much the same: **keep it simple and clear**, as Vance Merolla, global sustainability director for Colgate-Palmolive, puts it. Merolla likes visuals and checklists that boil down complex requirements. One example is turning hundreds of pages of green building guidance into a short checklist that engineers and architects can skim, so colleagues get the main point without wading through the detail. He believes companies rarely lie outright about their environmental practices, but he warns that word choice matters a great deal and that it is essential to tell the whole story rather than cherry-pick the good results.

If you end up speaking to a journalist, ask your own questions early: what the story is about, and whether it might involve video or other formats you should know about. It also helps to agree ground rules. "On background" means you can be paraphrased but not quoted word for word or named. "Off the record" means the information should not be repeated at all. The safe default is to assume everything you say is on the record unless the reporter explicitly agrees otherwise. Speaking naturally, rather than reciting a prepared statement, usually gives better quotes. If you are uneasy talking to the press, it is perfectly reasonable to say "not right now" rather than closing the door for good.

Simplicity is a strategy in itself. The IPCC's 2021 Summary for All, a plain-language version of its Sixth Assessment Report, runs to just 16 pages and has as many hand-drawn doodles as charts. It shows that even very technical climate science can be made easy to follow.

## Personal conversations and the psychology of change
<!-- tag-start: individual-actions -->
Talking about climate change with the people around you can lead to real change, and doing it well tends to follow a similar pattern. Share your own personal connection to the issue. Link it to what already matters to the other person, whether family, the economy or health. Look for common ground in shared values. Point to impacts that are already visible now, not only distant future ones. Make the positive case for the economic and health benefits of clean energy, and mention the direct savings that sustainable choices bring. Convey real urgency without sliding into fatalism. Share concrete examples of climate action that has worked, and talk about how today's choices shape tomorrow.
<!-- tag-end -->

There is good evidence for why small, personal conversations matter so much. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report suggests that shifting a society's norms may need only 10-30% of people to be deeply committed. Researcher Damon Centola's work points the same way: once roughly 25% of a group adopts a new behaviour or belief, the majority is likely to follow. This is sometimes called the "25% Revolution."

Several well-known mental biases help explain why people struggle to act on a threat like climate change, and understanding them can make conversations go better. **Hyperbolic discounting** is the habit of valuing the present far more than the future, a leftover from our evolutionary past, when immediate dangers mattered more than slow ones. People also tend to care little about generations beyond the span of their own close family, roughly from great-grandparents to great-grandchildren, so sacrifices for the more distant future feel less urgent than they should. The bystander effect leads people to assume someone else, often "the leaders", must already be dealing with the crisis, and it gets stronger the larger the group. The sunk-cost fallacy keeps people and institutions stuck on a path, such as continued reliance on fossil fuels, simply because so much has already been invested in it, despite decades of evidence that a transition is both possible and necessary.

Small groups behave differently from large, anonymous ones. Research on **Dunbar's number** finds that a person can keep up stable relationships with roughly 150 other people. Beyond that, trust and the ability to rely on others' cooperation start to weaken. Exposure Labs, the film company behind *Chasing Ice* and *Chasing Coral*, has used this on purpose by screening its films to small, mixed groups. In South Carolina, a US state with many prominent climate-denying leaders, it brought together people from farming, fisheries and tourism to talk about how climate change affects them personally. This draws on two more psychological effects. With the endowment effect, people value a solution more when they helped come up with it. With social comparison theory, people adjust their own behaviour by looking at those around them. The same logic lies behind energy bills that show households how their use compares with their neighbours', which research has found measurably cuts consumption.

Finally, framing matters. People respond better to positive messages, such as "a clean energy future will save lives," than to purely negative ones, such as warnings of extinction. How a message is framed shapes whether it leads to action or to paralysis.

## Summary findings
- Only a third of climate stories on US nightly news and Sunday shows in 2021 discussed possible solutions (Media Matters for America).
- The World Risk Poll found 69% of 150,000 people across 142 countries consider climate change a serious threat, against 13% who see no threat at all.
- The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report suggests shifting a society's norms may need only 10-30% of people to be deeply committed to a cause.
- Damon Centola's research finds that once roughly 25% of a group adopts a new belief, the majority tends to follow.
- "Dunbar's number" puts the limit on a person's stable social relationships at around 150 people, beyond which trust and cooperation weaken.
- The IPCC's 2021 Summary for All boiled its full Sixth Assessment Report down to just 16 pages for general readers.
