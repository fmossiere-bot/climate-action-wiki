# Under the Sky We Make: Book Summary

## What is it

*Under the Sky We Make* is a book by climate scientist **Dr Kimberly Nicholas**, who spent close to fifteen years explaining climate science to the public before she wrote it. Her main argument is that companies and governments carry most of the responsibility for the climate crisis, but individuals can still bring about real and lasting change. That starts with understanding how big the problem is, and how much we can do about it. The book mixes science with her own life, from the carbon cost of visiting family overseas to finding "low-carbon love" online. Nobody will hand us the power to change course, she says. We have to take it.

## Why it matters

This book is one of the sources most often cross-referenced in this wiki (see [Keynote: Dr Chip Fletcher](../../climate-science/Keynote%20Dr%20Chip%20FLETCHER.md), which covers similar ground). It fits a very wide range of climate facts into one readable story, from the physics of warming, through the collapse of ecosystems, to what individuals and whole systems can do. That is why this page goes through it chapter by chapter instead of squeezing it into a handful of themes.

##  It's us

To meet the 1.5C goal, global carbon pollution needs to be **cut at least in half by 2030**, with high emitters cutting more. Some carbon stays in the atmosphere more or less forever, so pollution has to fall all the way to zero for the climate to stabilise. That means switching quickly from fossil fuels to clean energy, and from polluting farming to regenerative farming.

Some of the book's main physical-science facts:

- Global temperature has already risen about **1 degree**. It happened so fast that some Australian temperature charts had to add a new colour, purple, to show it.
- The evidence comes from decades of satellite and ocean buoy records, and centuries of thermometer readings on land.
- Atmospheric **CO2 has increased by 40%**, and as a result ocean acidity has increased by 26%.
- Humans are responsible for **100% of today's warming**. Models show that without human influence, Earth's temperature would probably have gone down over the same period.
- CO2 makes up **75% of heat-trapping emissions**, and methane and nitrous oxide make up most of the rest. Of the carbon emitted over the last decade, 86% came from burning fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas), carbon that had been buried for millions of years. The rest came mostly from cutting down forests and other changes in land use.
- Nature absorbs roughly half of human carbon emissions for free, through plants (photosynthesis) and the ocean, though the ocean pays for it by becoming more acidic. Of what stays in the atmosphere, about a quarter will remain there effectively forever (over 10,000 years). It eventually settles at the bottom of the ocean and comes back through volcanic eruptions over millions of years. Humans are adding carbon to the atmosphere roughly **100,000 times faster** than geological processes can take it out.
- CO2 in the atmosphere stood at around 300ppm (parts per million) in the early 20th century. The safe threshold of **350ppm was passed in 1987**. By 2019 the level had reached roughly 411-419ppm, and it was rising by about 2.3ppm a year.

Countries agreed a framework for working together on climate in **1992**, followed by the **2015 Paris Agreement**, in which they agreed to keep warming below 2C. The book is blunt that **we have already left the safe zone**. Today's climate change is happening around 100 times faster than natural changes in the past. Half of all species have already had to move their range, at just 1 degree of warming. Human-caused climate change has also widened the gap between rich and poor countries by an estimated 25%. The "carbon budget" is the fixed amount of carbon we can still emit while keeping warming in check. Humanity has used close to **90%** of it, burning through 60% in just the last four decades. When our parents were young, roughly 95% of it was still available.

The book compares **1.5C and 2C** of warming. Staying near 1.5C would keep low-lying cities and countries above water, and bring cleaner air and better public health. Even so, only an estimated **10-30% of coral reefs would survive**, because warming kills the algae they depend on. At 2C, half the world's population would face water stress, and the average US resident would live in a climate like one roughly 300 miles further south than today. On current policies, the world is heading for around **3.5C of warming by 2100**.

## We're the asteroid

Humans are using land, water and oceans in ways that are killing much of the life on Earth. The book calls this both morally wrong and self-defeating, because we need working nature to survive. A study led by Frances Moore at the University of California, Davis found that **no one born after 1985 has lived through a year on Earth cooler than the 20th-century average**. Yet people tend to stop commenting on warmer weather after about five years. They seem to accept it as a "new normal", even though nothing about it is normal.

Most of the living matter (biomass) on Earth is plants. Plants make up **82%** of all biomass, single-celled bacteria 13%, fungi 2%, archaea (microbes living in seafloor mud, wetlands and guts) 1.3%, algae and amoebas 0.7%, and animals just **0.4%**, most of them invertebrates. Within that animal share, roughly half is insects and their relatives, a quarter is fish, 8% is worms and soil organisms, 8% is molluscs, and 4% is jellyfish, coral and their relatives. Most of the small remainder is mammals. **Humans make up about 2% of animal biomass**, or roughly 0.01% of all life on Earth when everything is weighed together. Earth has already been through five mass extinctions in its four-billion-year history, each wiping out around three-quarters or more of all species, set off by events such as asteroid impacts and volcanic eruptions. The worst, at the end of the Permian about 250 million years ago, wiped out more than 95% of species (often given as 96%).

Because so much of Earth's biomass is plants, our impact on it is easy to see. **Almost 40% of forests** have been cut down or burned to make room for crops and livestock. Clearing land for food and forestry has released roughly a quarter of the carbon that nature would otherwise have kept locked away. Only **15% of the original biomass of wild land mammals** is left. Populations of vertebrates (animals with a backbone) have fallen by an estimated 60% (2018 report) to 68% (2020 report) since 1970. The book calls this catastrophic, and says it will take millions of years to reverse. An estimated **85% of wetlands** and 50% of coral reefs are already gone.

The book contrasts two ways of thinking. The **Exploitation Mindset** sees producing and consuming more material goods as the path to progress. Under it, nearly 80% of land and 90% of ocean are now dominated by human activity. The Regeneration Mindset puts value, respect and empathy for all living beings at the centre. It treats life as worth something in itself, and sees the Earth as a living thing we have a relationship with, rather than a store of resources.

## Uprooting exploitation, sowing regeneration

Humanity, and above all the wealthiest billion people, is pushing further and further past the physical, chemical and biological limits that keep Earth's life-support systems working. Putting the Regeneration Mindset into practice means caring for people and nature, stopping harm at its source instead of treating symptoms, and building for resilience. To reach real zero emissions, the book argues, all new fossil fuel extraction has to stop now.

If global carbon emissions are cut roughly in half by 2030 and reach zero around 2040, there is a two-in-three chance of holding warming around 1.5C. Cutting CO2 emissions by around 25% by 2030 and reaching zero by 2070 would give a 66% chance of holding it around 2C, the upper limit of the Paris Agreement. The IPCC says that hitting 1.5C needs deep, fast cuts in every sector: energy, land, cities, transport, buildings and industry.

The good news is that **most of the technology we need already exists**. A 2018 study in *Science* found that most of today's energy services are "relatively straightforward to decarbonise". Around a quarter of fossil emissions come from harder sectors such as aviation, iron, steel and cement, where more research is needed to bring costs down. Around **80% of the world's heating and electricity still comes from fossil fuels**. Cutting emissions by 45% means fully decarbonising home heating and electricity, and the book names China, the US and Europe as the worst offenders.

## Sink into your grief

Scientists have known for years what is coming, and that brings its own kind of grief. The book says it needs to be worked through like any other loss, much as the Covid-19 pandemic made loss and disruption real for many people. An estimated **3 billion animals were killed in Australia's 2019 bushfires**, a figure the book says takes time to take in.

On coral reefs, **91% of the Great Barrier Reef bleached in 2016**, and extreme marine heatwaves have only become more common since. A 2007 study in *Science* set out a series of underwater images that, the book says, the real world is now following closely. At 1C of warming: healthy, colourful coral and fish. At 2C: dead, white coral covered in algae. At 3C: a dull, even layer of slime over dead coral and broken shells, in cloudy water.

## Making meaning, facing fear, getting angry

Finding meaning in a time of climate change, the book argues, comes down to personal choices within a global crisis. It draws on Viktor Frankl's idea that meaning comes from three places: what you do, what you feel, and the attitude you choose. A 2017 study found that the choices with the most impact for high emitters wanting to cut their pollution quickly were **car use, meat consumption and flying**. Halving any of these makes a real contribution.

The book is wary of "fix it later" approaches, such as carbon removal or injecting particles into the atmosphere. It sees them as a way to keep the exploitation mindset going while patching up the damage, much like relying on medication while carrying on with an unhealthy lifestyle. Instead, it suggests a **Radical Acceptance** process in five stages: ignorance (or denial), avoidance (or bargaining), doom (or depression), "all the feels" (acceptance), and finally purpose, where acceptance turns into action, one step at a time.

The book also explains why the scientific consensus is so solid. Scientific claims are tested through careful, cautious analysis and peer review, and agreement only builds once consistent evidence becomes overwhelming. The basic physics has been understood since 1896, when Swedish scientist **Svante Arrhenius** (a distant relative of Greta Thunberg) first suggested that burning coal could warm the planet. The human activities behind today's warming break down like this: burning fuel for electricity and heating, 25%; burning fossil fuels for industry, 21%; transport, 14%; buildings, 6%; other energy use, 10%; and land use for food and forestry, 24%.

The book compares fossil fuel companies to the tobacco industry, which spent decades denying that nicotine was harmful. **Exxon Mobil and Shell's core business plan is still to keep extracting and burning carbon, and to expand**, even while their advertising and social media tell a very different story. Oil majors spend around 29% of their lobbying and advertising budgets presenting themselves as low-carbon leaders. That is almost ten times the roughly 3% of their capital budgets they actually invest in clean energy.

## Climate change isn't fair

The book asks readers to recognise "climate privilege". Emissions and their impacts are spread very unevenly between countries, income groups and generations, so what high emitters in rich countries, especially the US, do in the next decade matters most. When pledges under the Paris Agreement were tracked, only **Gambia** was judged on track for a 2030 pledge in line with 1.5C. A handful of countries had pledges in line with 2C, and only Kenya and India were judged to be doing enough to get there. All other major emitters were rated insufficient (Europe), highly insufficient (China) or critically insufficient (Russia, Saudi Arabia and the US).

To work out your own carbon footprint, the book recommends the CoolClimate Calculator, built by researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, as the most evidence-based tool for the US. Sitra's lifestyle test was built for Finland, but its principles apply widely, and it is easier to use and focuses on high-impact actions. The book puts a number on fairness: **everyone's personal climate budget needs to fall to 2.5 tonnes of CO2 per year by 2030**. The US, home to about 5% of humanity, has emitted about 25% of all the carbon released so far. African countries, home to about 15% of the world's people, have emitted only about 4%.

An estimated **72% of global climate pollution comes from household consumption choices**, mainly getting around (cars and planes), diet (meat and dairy) and housing (heating, cooling and electricity). The rest comes from government spending, infrastructure and investment. Income is a strong predictor of pollution. Anyone in the US earning over $38,000 a year is in the top 10% of incomes worldwide, and that top 10% causes half of all household carbon pollution, while the poorest 50% cause just 10%. In a median US household (around $69,000 a year in 2019, roughly what two preschool teachers or one nurse earn), emissions are about **16 tonnes per person a year**. The richest 10% of Americans (household income above roughly $201,000 a year) emit an average of 50 tonnes per person a year. At the very top, Lund University researcher Stefan Gössling estimates that the super-rich, such as Bill Gates and Paris Hilton, emit around 10,000 times more carbon from flying alone than the average person.

Against the 2.5-tonne limit, only about **5% of Europeans** currently stay within it. People in India are already below the 2030 budget per person, and Brazil sits at around 2.8 tonnes. **Kevin Anderson**, professor of energy and climate change, has calculated that if the richest 10% of the world cut their emissions to the level of an average European, which is still a comfortable way to live, global emissions would fall by about a third. That is most of the way to the 50% cut needed by 2030.

On how change spreads, research suggests it takes around **25% of a population** to reach the tipping point where social norms shift. One survey found that 85% of young people aged 12 to 18 were willing to change how they live, especially by travelling less by car and plane.

## Slowing down and staying grounded

<!-- tag-start: individual-actions -->
High emitters need to cut their carbon overconsumption. The main steps are flying less (for people who fly, it is the most intense source of pollution), going car-free, supporting rules and standards to electrify everything with zero-carbon energy, installing solar panels and improving home energy efficiency, and using less heating and cooling.
<!-- tag-end -->

**On flying:** one flight can cancel out the savings from recycling 10,000 plastic bottles, and hour for hour, flying is the fastest way to heat the planet. Aviation's share of global emissions looks small (1.7-2.4%), but very few people fly. Only **11% of the world's population flew in 2018**. More than two-thirds of Germans never fly, and more than half of adults in the US and UK never fly. The top 1% of frequent flyers cause about half of all aviation emissions, and 68% of flights are taken by just 12% of adults, those who fly six or more times a year. One return trip from New York to London uses up roughly eight months of a sustainable carbon budget. Tourism as a whole causes about 8% of global climate pollution, but flying alone can make up about 50% of a wealthy person's household footprint. In Europe, Ryanair has joined the EU's top 10 climate polluters, alongside nine coal-fired power plants. See [Decarbonising Aviation and Shipping](../../solutions/electrification/Decarbonising%20Aviation%20and%20Shipping.md) for more.

**On driving:** transport is the largest source of greenhouse gas emissions in the US, and private cars are the largest source within transport. Rising emissions from SUVs have wiped out the savings from electric cars. If SUVs were a country, they would be the world's seventh-largest emitter. Electric cars are not a perfect fix, and we still need to drive less. But 2017 research found that, across the whole life of the car (building it and generating its electricity), an EV causes roughly half the climate pollution of a typical petrol car. See [Car Life Cycle Emissions: EV vs Combustion](../../sectors/transport/Car%20Life%20Cycle%20Emissions%20-%20EV%20vs%20Combustion.md).

**On technology:** more technology does not automatically mean more efficiency. A UC Davis report found that ride-hailing services such as Uber and Lyft increased car use and traffic, created more trips overall, and cut walking, cycling and public transport use. Self-driving cars might free up commuters' time, but they would still encourage sprawl, traffic, local air pollution and emissions, whoever is at the wheel. Technology alone will not solve this. Behaviour has to change too.

**On electricity:** cleaning up electricity and heat is the first step to decarbonising the whole economy (see [Clean Electricity - Why It Has to Come First](../../sectors/energy/Clean%20Electricity%20-%20Why%20It%20Has%20to%20Come%20First.md)). In Europe, the policies that have cut emissions most so far are rules setting renewable energy targets and efficiency standards, covering how power is generated, transmitted and distributed, and how it is used in appliances and buildings.

## Food shouldn't come from a factory

<!-- tag-start: individual-actions -->
The book's food advice: eat a mostly plant-based diet, and try going vegan for a month. If you eat animal products, keep them to roughly one glass of milk or one ounce of cheese a day, two eggs and two servings of chicken or fish a week, and two burgers a month. Eat more non-tropical fruit, vegetables, pulses and nuts (and less coffee and chocolate). Support reform of farm subsidies, grow food where you can, and support protecting and restoring nature.
<!-- tag-end -->

Livestock causes most of the harm in today's food system. A large 2018 study in *Science* found that animal farming takes up **83% of farmland** and produces 58% of farming's greenhouse gas emissions, 57% of its water pollution and 56% of its air pollution. It also uses more than a third of the fresh water taken for farming. Beef cattle turn only about 3% of the calories and 5% of the protein they eat into food for people. Feeding those crops directly to people could feed another 4 billion people, roughly half as many as are alive today. Another study found that if the whole world ate the average Indian diet, everyone could be fed on less than half of today's cropland.

Farming is the main driver of the loss of wildlife. Humans are causing extinctions **1,000 times faster** than the natural background rate, up to 1 million species are now threatened, and expanding farmland causes 80% of global deforestation. Most deforestation in the Amazon is to feed animals, either directly as pasture or indirectly by growing soy for animal feed.

On water, **87% of the water people consume worldwide goes to irrigated farming**. That is 22 times more than all the households of 7.8 billion people put together. The US Geological Survey shows all of Earth's surface fresh water, from lakes and rivers, as a single bubble just 35 miles across. That is effectively all the fresh water we have. See [IRELAND Water Usage and Water Management](../../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Water%20Usage%20and%20Water%20Management.md) for an example from one country.

Industrial farming also harms health directly. Unhealthy diets caused more than **11 million deaths in 2017**, making them the biggest single risk factor for death. Giving livestock antibiotics routinely, to make them grow faster and prevent disease, adds to antibiotic resistance. The average American eats roughly twice the protein needed for good health.

Subsidies are a big lever. Around **$1 million every minute** is spent worldwide propping up an unsustainable food system, and that money could be redirected. In the EU, more than 80% of farm subsidies go to just 20% of farmers. A 2020 study led by Murray Scown found that nearly half of EU farm subsidies were misspent. They widened income inequality, supported the most polluting farms with the least value for wildlife, and moved rural development money towards cities. The book concludes that we need to eat up to six times less animal produce than we do today.

## Values, costs, and the personal as political

On the economy, the book suggests becoming "growth agnostic": the real purpose of an economy is growth in wellbeing, not GDP. It supports ending fossil fuel subsidies in every form, while protecting vulnerable people during the transition. Markets alone cannot stabilise the climate, it argues. With a target of zero emissions, the right price on carbon is effectively infinite, which amounts to a ban on new fossil exploration, construction, supply, production and financing. The book calls for an immediate stop to all new investment in fossil energy, moving money into clean, local community energy, and preparing to close existing fossil infrastructure early. It recommends divesting personally, by moving away from banks and credit cards linked to fossil fuels towards credit unions. It also suggests supporting divestment campaigns at schools, workplaces, pension funds and city councils, and pushing employers to decarbonise. On carbon offsets, the book is blunt: **offsets don't work**. It recommends giving around 10% of your income (or as much as you can) to groups working for change to the whole system.

Governments worldwide spent almost **$500 billion in 2019** on subsidies that make fossil fuels artificially cheap to produce and use. A 2020 analysis by the International Renewable Energy Agency found that building new solar and wind is now often cheaper than building new fossil power, or even than keeping existing coal plants running. Replacing the most expensive quarter of the world's coal power with solar and wind could cut system costs by up to $23 billion a year.

On politics, the book calls on readers to:

- vote for candidates with strong climate records who have signed the No Fossil Fuel Money Pledge
- get involved in climate organisations and non-violent movements
- demand that business and government leaders deliver credible plans to cut their pollution towards zero, rather than offset it
- support fair ways for citizens to take part in climate decisions
- back a just transition for workers in declining industries
- support independent climate journalism
- support the growing number of legal cases being brought, and won, on climate grounds
