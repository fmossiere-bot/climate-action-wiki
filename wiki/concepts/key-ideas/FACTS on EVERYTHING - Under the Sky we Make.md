# Under the Sky We Make: Book Summary

## What is it

*Under the Sky We Make* is a book by climate scientist **Dr Kimberly Nicholas**, who spent close to fifteen years communicating climate science to the public before writing it. Her central argument, and **the most important claim in the book**, is that while companies and governments carry the largest share of responsibility for the climate crisis, individuals can still drive real, significant and lasting change, and that doing so starts with understanding both the scale of the problem and our own agency within it. The book blends scientific expertise with personal experience, from the carbon cost of visiting family overseas to finding "low-carbon love" online, while making the case that no one will hand us the power to change course: we have to take it.

## Why it matters

This is one of the most frequently cross-referenced sources in this wiki (see [Keynote: Dr Chip Fletcher](../../climate-science/Keynote%20Dr%20Chip%20FLETCHER.md), whose keynote covers similar ground). It packs an unusually wide range of climate facts, from the physical science of warming through ecological collapse to individual and systemic action, into one accessible narrative, which is why this page summarises it chapter by chapter rather than trying to compress it into a handful of themes.

##  It's us

To meet the 1.5C climate goal, global carbon pollution needs to be **cut at least in half by 2030**, with high emitters cutting more. Because some carbon effectively lasts forever in the atmosphere, pollution has to fall all the way to zero to stabilise the climate, meaning a rapid switch from fossil fuels to clean energy and from polluting to regenerative agriculture.

Some of the book's key physical-science facts:

- Global temperature has already risen about **1 degree**, so fast that a new colour, purple, had to be added to some Australian temperature charts to represent it.
- The evidence comes from decades of satellite and ocean buoy records, and centuries of land thermometer readings.
- Atmospheric **CO2 has increased by 40%**, and ocean acidity has increased by **26%** as a result.
- Humans are responsible for **100% of today's warming**; models show that without human influence, Earth's temperature would likely have gone down over the same period.
- **CO2 makes up 75% of heat-trapping emissions**, with methane and nitrous oxide making up most of the rest. Of the carbon emitted over the last decade, **86% came from burning coal and gas**, releasing carbon that had been buried for millions of years.
- Roughly half of human carbon emissions are absorbed for free by nature (plants through photosynthesis, and the ocean, though ocean absorption drives acidification). Of what remains in the atmosphere, about a quarter will stay there effectively forever (over 10,000 years) before eventually settling at the bottom of the ocean and cycling back through volcanic eruptions over millions of years. Humans are adding carbon to the atmosphere roughly **100,000 times faster** than geological processes can remove it.
- Atmospheric CO2 concentration stood at around 300ppm in the early 20th century. The safe threshold of **350ppm was passed in 1987**, and by 2019 the level had reached roughly 411-419ppm, rising by about 2.3ppm a year.

Internationally, nations agreed a framework for climate cooperation in **1992**, followed by the **2015 Paris Agreement**, under which countries agreed to keep warming below 2C. The book is blunt that **we have already passed the safe space**: current climate change is unfolding around 100 times faster than natural variation in the past. Half of all species have already had to shift their range, at just 1 degree of warming, and human-caused climate change has already widened the global income gap by an estimated 25%. On the "carbon budget" (the fixed amount of carbon that can still be emitted while keeping warming in check), humanity has used close to **90%** of the total budget, having burned through 60% of it in just the last four decades, compared with roughly 95% still being available when our parents' generation was young.

At **1.5C versus 2C** of warming: staying near 1.5C would keep low-lying cities and countries above water and mean cleaner air and better public health, but even so, only an estimated **10-30% of coral reefs would survive**, because of warming-driven algae loss. At 2C, half the world's population would be exposed to water stress, and the average US resident would experience a climate shifted roughly 300 miles further south than today. On current policies, the world is on track for around **3.5C of warming by 2100**.

## We're the asteroid

Human exploitation of land, water and oceans is killing much of the life on Earth, something the book frames as both morally wrong and self-defeating, since humans need functioning nature to survive. A study led by Frances Moore at the University of California, Davis found that **no one born after 1985 has lived through a year on Earth cooler than the 20th-century average**, yet people tend to stop remarking on warmer weather after about five years, seeming to accept it as a "new normal" even though nothing about it is normal.

Life on Earth's biomass is overwhelmingly plant-based: plants make up **82%** of all biomass, unicellular bacteria **13%**, fungi **2%**, archaea (microbes living in seafloor sediment, wetlands and guts) **1.3%**, algae and amoebas **0.7%**, and animals just **0.4%**, most of which are invertebrates. Within that animal share, roughly half is insects and their relatives, a quarter is fish, 8% is worms and soil organisms, 8% is molluscs, and 4% is jellyfish, coral and relatives; most of the small remainder is mammals. **Humans make up about 2% of animal biomass**, or roughly 0.01% of all life on Earth on a cosmic weighting scale. Earth has already been through five mass extinctions in its four-billion-year history, each wiping out around 96% of life, triggered by events like asteroid impacts and volcanic eruptions.

Because so much of Earth's biomass is plant life, human impact on it is stark: **almost 40% of forests** have been felled or burned to clear land for crops and livestock, and land conversion for food and forestry has released roughly a quarter of the carbon that nature would otherwise have kept captured. Only **15% of the original biomass of wild land mammals** remains. Global vertebrate populations have declined by an estimated 60% (as of a 2018 report) to 68% (as of a 2020 report) since 1970, a trend the book calls catastrophic and one that will take millions of years to reverse. An estimated **85% of wetlands** and **50% of coral reefs** are already gone.

The book contrasts an **Exploitation Mindset**, which treats producing and consuming more material goods as the path to progress (under which nearly 80% of land and 90% of ocean are now dominated by human activity), with a **Regeneration Mindset**, which places value, respect and empathy for all living beings at the centre of our priorities, treats life as an end in itself, and sees the Earth as a living entity we have a relationship with, not just a store of resources.

## Uprooting exploitation, sowing regeneration

Humanity, and particularly the wealthiest billion people globally, is increasingly exceeding the physical, chemical and biological boundaries that keep Earth's life-support systems functioning. Putting the Regeneration Mindset into practice means respecting and caring for people and nature, reducing harm at its source rather than treating symptoms, and building for resilience. To reach real zero emissions, the book argues, all new fossil fuel exploitation needs to stop now.

If global carbon emissions are cut roughly in half by 2030 and reach zero around 2040, there is a two-in-three chance of stabilising warming around 1.5C. Cutting CO2 emissions by around 25% by 2030 and reaching zero by 2070 would give a 66% chance of stabilising around 2C, the upper limit of the Paris Agreement's temperature goal. The IPCC states that hitting 1.5C requires deep, rapid emissions cuts in every sector: energy, land, urban development, transport, buildings and industry.

Fortunately, **most of the technology needed already exists**. A 2018 study in *Science* concluded that most of today's energy services are "relatively straightforward to decarbonise," while around a quarter of fossil emissions come from technically harder sectors such as aviation, iron, steel and cement, where more research and development is needed to bring down costs. Globally, around **80% of heating and electricity still comes from fossil fuels**; cutting emissions by 45% requires fully decarbonising home heating and electricity, with China, the US and Europe identified as the worst offenders.

## Sink into your grief

Scientists have understood what is coming for years, which creates a particular kind of grief that the book says needs to be processed like any other loss, in the same way the Covid-19 pandemic made loss and disruption newly tangible for many people. An estimated **3 billion animals were killed in Australia's 2019 bushfires**, a figure the book says takes real processing to absorb.

On coral reefs: **91% of the Great Barrier Reef bleached in 2016**, and extreme heatwaves have only become more common since. A 2007 study in *Science* documented a progression of underwater photographs that the book says the real world is now eerily following: thriving, colourful coral and fish at 1C of warming; dead, white coral with algae at 2C; and a dull, uniform slime over dead coral and shell fragments in cloudy water at 3C.

## Making meaning, facing fear, getting angry

Finding meaning under climate change, the book argues, is about personal choices within a global crisis, echoing Viktor Frankl's idea that meaning comes from three routes: what you do, what you feel, and the attitude you choose to take. A 2017 study found that the highest-impact choices for high-emitting individuals looking to quickly cut their climate pollution were **car use, meat consumption and flying**; cutting any of these by half meaningfully contributes to decarbonisation.

The book pushes back on "fix it after the fact" approaches, such as carbon removal or atmospheric particle injection, as ways of preserving an exploitation mindset while trying to patch its consequences, comparing this to relying on medication while continuing an unhealthy lifestyle. It recommends a **Radical Acceptance** process in five stages: ignorance (or denial), avoidance (or bargaining), doom (or depression), "all the feels" (acceptance), and finally purpose, where acceptance turns into action, taken one step at a time.

On the science itself, the book stresses the rigour behind climate consensus: scientific claims are tested through careful, conservative analysis and peer review, with agreement building only once the weight of consistent evidence becomes overwhelming. The basic physics has been understood since 1896, when Swedish scientist **Svante Arrhenius** (a distant relative of Greta Thunberg) first suggested that burning coal could cause global warming. The breakdown of the human activities driving today's warming is: burning fuel for electricity and heating, **25%**; burning fossil fuels for industry, **21%**; transportation, **14%**; buildings, **6%**; other energy use, **10%**; and land use for food and forestry, **24%**.

The book draws a direct parallel between fossil fuel companies and the tobacco industry's decades-long campaign to deny that nicotine was harmful: **Exxon Mobil and Shell's core business plan remains to continue and expand carbon extraction and burning**, even as their advertising and social media present a very different picture. Oil majors spend around **29% of their lobbying and advertising budgets** promoting themselves as low-carbon leaders, a proportion the book notes is almost ten times higher than the roughly **3% of their capital budgets** actually invested in clean energy.

## Climate change isn't fair

The book argues for recognising "climate privilege": what high emitters in rich countries, and especially the US, do in the next decade is critical, given how unevenly emissions and their impacts are distributed across nations, income groups and generations. Tracking pledges under the Paris Agreement, only **Gambia** was judged on track for its 2030 pledge consistent with 1.5C. A handful of countries had pledges consistent with 2C, with only **Kenya and India** judged to have enough action behind them to get there. All other major emitters were judged insufficient (Europe), highly insufficient (China), or critically insufficient (Russia, Saudi Arabia and the US).

To calculate a personal carbon footprint, the book recommends the CoolClimate Calculator from University of California, Berkeley researchers as the most evidence-based tool for the US, or Sitra's lifestyle test (built for Finland, but with generally applicable principles) for a more user-friendly, high-impact-action focus. The book puts a number on fairness: **everyone's personal climate budget needs to fall to 2.5 tonnes of CO2 per year by 2030**. The US, home to about 5% of humanity, has emitted about **25% of cumulative global carbon**; African countries, home to about 15% of the world's people, have emitted only about **4%**.

An estimated **72% of global climate pollution traces back to household consumption decisions**, particularly mobility (cars and planes), diet (meat and dairy) and housing (heating, cooling and electricity); the remainder comes from government spending, infrastructure and capital investment. Income strongly predicts pollution: anyone in the US earning over **$38,000 a year** sits among the top 10% of incomes globally, and that top 10% collectively causes half of household carbon pollution worldwide, while the poorest 50% of people cause just 10%. In a median US household (around $69,000 a year in 2019, roughly the income of two preschool teachers or one nurse), per capita emissions run about **16 tonnes a year**. The richest 10% of Americans (household income above roughly $201,000 a year) emit an average of **50 tonnes per person per year**. At the very top, Lund University researcher Stefan Gössling estimates that the super-rich, such as Bill Gates and Paris Hilton, emit **around 10,000 times more carbon from flying alone** than the average person.

Against the 2.5-tonne personal limit, only about **5% of Europeans** currently manage to stay within it; residents of India are currently below the 2030 per capita budget, and Brazil sits at around 2.8 tonnes. Professor of energy and climate change **Kevin Anderson** has calculated that if the richest 10% of the global population cut their emissions to the level of an average European, a still comfortable standard of living, it would cut global emissions by about a third, most of the way to the 50% cut needed by 2030.

On how change spreads, research estimates it takes around **25% of a population** to reach the critical mass needed to shift social norms. One survey found that **85% of young people aged 12 to 18** were willing to make lifestyle changes, particularly travelling less by car and plane.

## Slowing down and staying grounded

High emitters need to reduce their carbon overconsumption, chiefly by flying less (the single most intense source of pollution for those who fly), going car-free, supporting regulation and standards to electrify everything on zero-carbon energy, installing solar panels and improving home energy efficiency, and cutting back on heating and cooling.

**On flying:** a single flight can cancel out the savings of recycling 10,000 plastic bottles, and hour by hour, flying is the fastest way to heat the planet. Globally, aviation's share of emissions looks small (1.7-2.4%), but that has to be weighed against how few people fly: only **11% of the world's population flew in 2018**, more than two-thirds of Germans never fly, and more than half of US and UK adults never fly. The top 1% of frequent flyers cause about half of all aviation emissions, and **68% of flights are taken by just 12% of adults** who fly six or more times a year. A single return trip from New York to London emits roughly eight months' worth of a sustainable carbon budget. While tourism overall causes about 8% of global climate pollution, aviation alone can make up about 50% of a wealthy individual's household carbon footprint; in Europe, **Ryanair has joined the EU's top 10 climate polluters, alongside nine coal-fired power plants**. See [Decarbonising Aviation and Shipping](../../solutions/electrification/Decarbonising%20Aviation%20and%20Shipping.md) for more.

**On driving:** transportation is the largest source of US greenhouse gas emissions, and private cars are the largest source within transport. Rising SUV emissions have cancelled out the savings from electric vehicle adoption; if SUVs were counted as a nation, they would be the world's seventh-largest emitter. Electric vehicles are not a perfect fix and reducing car use is still necessary, but 2017 research found that, accounting for the full life cycle of vehicle production and electricity use, EVs still cause roughly half as much climate pollution as a typical petrol car. See [Car Life Cycle Emissions: EV vs Combustion](../../sectors/transport/Car%20Life%20Cycle%20Emissions%20-%20EV%20vs%20Combustion.md).

**On technology:** more technology doesn't automatically mean greater efficiency. A UC Davis report found that ride-hailing services like Uber and Lyft increased overall car use and traffic, generated more trips overall, and reduced walking, cycling and public transport use. Self-driving cars might free up commuters' time, but still enable more sprawl, traffic, local air pollution and emissions regardless of whether a human is driving. Technology alone is not the answer; behaviour change matters too.

**On electricity:** decarbonising electricity and heat production is the necessary first step to decarbonising the whole economy (see [Clean Electricity - Why It Has to Come First](../../sectors/energy/Clean%20Electricity%20-%20Why%20It%20Has%20to%20Come%20First.md)). The most effective policies for cutting emissions in Europe to date have been regulations setting renewable energy targets and efficiency standards across generation, transmission, distribution and consumption in appliances and buildings.

## Food shouldn't come from a factory

Dietary recommendations from the book: eat a largely plant-based diet, try going vegan for a month, and if eating animal products, keep them to roughly one glass of milk or one ounce of cheese a day, two eggs and two servings of chicken or fish a week, and two burgers a month; eat more non-tropical fruit, vegetables, legumes and nuts (limiting coffee and chocolate); support reform of farm subsidies; grow food where possible; and support protecting and restoring nature.

Livestock production causes the majority of harm in today's food system. A comprehensive 2018 study in *Science* found that animal agriculture occupies **83% of farmland**, produces **58%** of agriculture's greenhouse gas emissions, **57%** of its water pollution and **56%** of its air pollution, and uses more than a third of the fresh water withdrawn for agriculture. Beef cattle convert only about **3% of the calories and 5% of the protein** they eat into food for people; redirecting that feed directly to humans could feed an additional 4 billion people, roughly half as many as are alive today. Another study found that if the whole world ate the average Indian diet, the world could be fed on less than half of today's cropland.

Agriculture is the main driver of the biodiversity crisis: humans are causing extinctions **1,000 times faster** than the natural background rate, with up to 1 million species now threatened with extinction, and farmland expansion causes **80% of global deforestation**. Most Amazon deforestation is to feed animals, either directly through pasture or indirectly by growing soy for animal feed.

On water: globally, **87% of the water people consume goes to irrigated agriculture**, which uses 22 times more water than the households of 7.8 billion people combined. The US Geological Survey visualises all of Earth's surface fresh water, from lakes and rivers, as a bubble just **35 miles in diameter**; that is effectively all the fresh water available. See [IRELAND Water Usage and Water Management](../../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Water%20Usage%20and%20Water%20Management.md) for a country-level example.

Industrial agriculture also carries direct health costs: unhealthy diets were responsible for more than **11 million deaths in 2017**, the largest mortality risk factor identified, and the routine use of antibiotics on livestock to speed growth and prevent disease contributes to antibiotic resistance. The average American consumes roughly twice the protein recommended for health.

On subsidies: redirecting the money currently propping up an unsustainable food system, which totals around **$1 million every minute globally**, is a key lever. In the EU, more than **80% of farm subsidies go to just 20% of farmers**. A 2020 study led by Murray Scown found that nearly half of EU farm subsidies were misspent, increasing income inequality, supporting the most climate-polluting farms with the lowest biodiversity value, and diverting rural development funds toward cities. The book concludes that animal consumption needs to fall by up to six times from today's levels.

## Values, costs, and the personal as political

On the economy: become a "growth agnostic", recognising that growth in wellbeing rather than GDP is the true purpose of an economy; support ending fossil fuel subsidies in all their forms while protecting vulnerable people from the transition; and recognise that markets alone cannot stabilise the climate, since the correct price on carbon, given a target of zero emissions, is effectively infinite, meaning a ban on new fossil exploration, construction, supply, production and financing. The book calls for stopping all new investment in fossil energy immediately, redirecting money to clean and decentralised community energy, and preparing to shut down existing fossil infrastructure early. It also recommends divesting personally, by moving away from banks and credit cards tied to fossil fuels toward credit unions, and supporting divestment campaigns at schools, workplaces, pension funds and city level, while pressuring employers to decarbonise. On carbon offsets: the book is blunt that **offsets don't work**, and recommends instead donating around 10% of income (or as much as feasible) to groups working for systemic change.

Governments globally spent almost **$500 billion in 2019** on subsidies making fossil fuels artificially cheaper to produce and consume. A 2020 analysis by the International Renewable Energy Agency found it is now often cheaper to build new solar and wind projects than to build new fossil energy capacity, or even to keep running existing coal plants; replacing the costliest quarter of global coal power with solar and wind could cut system costs by up to **$23 billion a year**.

On political engagement, the book calls for voting for candidates with strong climate records who have taken the No Fossil Fuel Money Pledge, getting active in climate organisations and non-violent movements, demanding that corporate and government leaders deliver credible plans to reduce (not just offset) their pollution toward zero, supporting fair citizen involvement in climate decision-making, backing a just transition for workers in declining industries, supporting independent climate journalism, and supporting the growing wave of legal cases being brought, and won, on climate grounds.
