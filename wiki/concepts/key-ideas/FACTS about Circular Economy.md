# FACTS about Circular Economy

## What is it

A **circular economy** tries to keep products, materials and resources in use and holding their value for as long as possible, cutting waste instead of following the traditional "take, make, dispose" model. Earth Overshoot Day, tracked by the organisation of the same name, marks the day each year when humanity has used up all the biological resources the planet can regrow in that year. Everything consumed after that date is ecological overshoot, meaning we are drawing down nature's stock rather than living off what it renews.

## Why it matters

Circularity is one of the few approaches that tackles climate change and resource depletion at the same time, because it cuts the emissions and extraction that go into making new products in the first place. But as the evidence below shows, turning that ambition into measurable progress has proved much harder than policymakers expected.

## EU transition to a circular economy is behind schedule
<!-- tag: eu -->

To drive the shift to a circular economy, the European Commission issued two action plans. The first, in 2015, had 54 specific actions. The second, in 2020, added 35 new actions and set a target of **doubling the EU's "circularity rate"** by 2030. The circularity rate is the share of materials that are recycled and fed back into the economy.

Progress has been slow. An audit by the European Court of Auditors (ECA) found that a large share of the roughly **€10 billion** set aside to encourage green investment and recycling had instead gone on general waste management. As the ECA report put it: "achieving the EU's ambition of recycling twice as many materials during the current decade as during the previous one will be like trying to square the circle." Between 2015 and 2021, the average circularity rate across all 27 EU countries rose by only 0.4 percentage points. Lithuania, Sweden, Romania, Denmark, Luxembourg, Finland and Poland actually went backwards over that period.

## Sharing is on the rise: the Library of Things
<!-- tag: eu -->

A European market study found that community sharing schemes, sometimes called "Libraries of Things", are growing fast. In a single year, the number of sharing stations in Europe rose by **70%**. There are now 1,325 stations serving more than 350,000 users.

## Policy updates

### Right to repair passed by the European Parliament
<!-- tag: eu -->

In November 2023, the European Parliament passed new "right to repair" rules that make it easier for people to have electronic or electrical goods repaired rather than replaced and thrown away. The law requires sellers to repair products unless that would cost more than replacing them, and gives consumers rights that make repairs easier and cheaper once guarantees run out. This matters because electronics are the fastest-growing source of waste in the EU. In 2017, more than **3.5 million tonnes** were collected, and only 40% of that was recycled.

### New EU rules targeting textile and food waste
<!-- tag: eu -->

EU lawmakers reached a provisional agreement on new rules to make a real dent in textile and food waste over five years. The EU produces around **59 million tonnes of food waste** each year, a direct economic loss estimated at €132 billion. It also produces 12.6 million tonnes of textile waste, 41% of it clothing and footwear, a problem made worse by fast fashion.

Under the proposed rules, textile producers and fashion brands would become responsible for their own waste, paying a fee to fund its collection and treatment. Companies that do poorly on circularity, sustainable design and durability would pay higher fees (France has already brought in similar legislation). Smaller companies in the sector would get access to shared waste-treatment facilities. Food manufacturers would need to cut waste by 10% by 2030, while shops, restaurants and households would need to cut food waste by 30% per person. Retailers and restaurants would be encouraged to donate unsold food that is still safe to eat. The agreement still has to be endorsed by all member states through the European Council, and by Parliament, and go through legal and language checks. Once formally adopted, member states would have up to 20 months to update their national laws.

### EU e-waste targets don't reflect Ireland's recycling efforts
<!-- tag: ireland -->

**WEEE Ireland**, the country's largest e-waste non-profit, marked 20 years in operation by pointing out that more than 250 million old and broken electrical items have been kept out of landfill since 2005. Over that period it has helped collect and recycle more than 609,000 tonnes of electrical waste, including 7.6 million large household appliances, 1.8 million fridges, 5.1 million televisions and monitors, 47.5 million lamps, and the equivalent of 725 million used AA batteries.

WEEE Ireland chief executive Leo Donovan argued that EU targets do not properly recognise Ireland's efforts, because they take no account of the longer lifespan of modern technology or of repair and reuse. Under current EU rules, member states must collect at least **65%** of the average weight of electrical and electronic equipment put on the market over the previous three years. This simple weight-based target does not reflect that newer appliances such as solar PV panels and heat pumps last 15 to 20 years, which delays the point at which they can be recycled at all.
