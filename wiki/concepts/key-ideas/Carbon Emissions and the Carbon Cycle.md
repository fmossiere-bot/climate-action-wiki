# Carbon Emissions and the Carbon Cycle

For a deeper look at how greenhouse gases compare and how carbon sinks work, see [Emissions and the Global Carbon Cycle](../../climate-science/Emissions%20and%20the%20Global%20Carbon%20Cycle.md).

Carbon and CO2 emissions are not the same thing. The carbon **stock** is the carbon that builds up in the air from CO2 emissions over time. Emitting 40 gigatonnes (GT) of CO2 adds around 4.9 GT to the carbon stock, once you allow for the roughly 55% that natural sinks take back out. Today's total carbon stock is around 880 GT, which corresponds to an atmospheric concentration of 412 parts per million (ppm).

To keep a 66% chance of staying under 1.5°C of warming, we have a budget of around **420 GT of CO2**, which at today's rate lasts roughly the next 10 years.

A bathtub is a useful way to picture it. Carbon stays in the atmosphere for a very long time, often hundreds of years. So reaching net-zero does not mean removing the carbon already there. It means turning off the tap so we stop adding more, and letting natural sinks slowly drain some of what is already in the tub, which eases the warming.

Not all the extra carbon we emit stays in the air. About a quarter ends up in the ocean, and roughly 30% is absorbed by plants and trees. That leaves around [44%](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/14/4811/2022/#:~:text=trend%2C%20remaining%20at-,around%2044%E2%80%89%25,-%2C%20albeit%20showing%20a) of CO2 emissions building up in the atmosphere, which works out at more than 5 billion tonnes of carbon added every year on average over the last decade. Plants and oceans have actually been absorbing more over time, as there is more carbon in the atmosphere for them to take up.
