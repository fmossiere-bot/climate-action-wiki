# Greenwashing

## What is it

**Greenwashing** is when an organisation makes a false, misleading or untrue claim about how good a company, product or service is for the environment. The term is used everywhere, but there is no firm agreement on what it means in law or regulation, and that makes it hard for regulators to act. European policymakers have gone furthest in trying to build a legal framework around it, yet complications keep turning up. A company might say it is cutting its carbon footprint while quietly leaving out the emissions of its customers or suppliers. An asset manager might overstate the ESG standards it uses to invest clients' money. Claims like these are often easy to spot afterwards, but hard to define in law in advance.

## Why it matters

Greenwashing is now seen as one of the biggest threats to the credibility of ESG investing and of company sustainability claims in general. Knowing how to recognise it protects shoppers and investors from being misled. Following how regulation is catching up, or failing to, shows where the real gaps in accountability still are.

## Examples of greenwashing

<!-- tag-start: company-evaluations -->
- **Coca-Cola's "100% recyclable" packaging claims**: a report found the company had spent millions promoting a trial batch of about 300 sample bottles made with 25% recycled marine plastic (collected from the Mediterranean Sea and beaches), without mentioning that Coca-Cola has been named the world's biggest plastic polluter.
- **Evian's "eco-friendly packaging"**: Evian uses recycled plastic in some of its bottles, but bottled water still has a large environmental impact. Reusable bottles and filtered tap water are the more sustainable choice.
- **"Rainforest Alliance Certified" chocolate**: the label is meant to promote sustainable farming, but critics say a product made mostly from ordinary cocoa can carry it as long as it contains a small share of certified cocoa. In early 2024, the Rainforest Alliance and Hershey's were sued over allegedly false claims about fair labour and sustainability.
- **Bud Light NEXT "Climate Neutral"**: Anheuser-Busch worked with the certifier Climate Neutral to sell its beer as climate neutral. A closer look showed the claim relied on offsetting today's emissions with credits, plus future plans to reduce them that had not been carried out.

### Sitka spruce: greenwashing in Irish schools (2026)
<!-- tag: ireland -->

A children's book, *Sitka Spruce: The Amazing Timber Tree*, was handed out to Irish primary schools. It was funded by Irish commercial forestry interests and had a foreword by the then minister Michael Healy-Rae. It showed Sitka spruce plantations as thriving ecosystems, when these single-species forests are widely seen as ecological dead zones. The Irish Wildlife Trust called for the book to be taken out of schools. The Department of Education said it was not responsible for materials handed out by industry, which shows a gap in oversight. See [IRELAND Land use, Soil and Forestry](../../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Land%20use%2C%20Soil%20and%20Forestry.md).

### TotalEnergies
<!-- tag: france, company-evaluations -->

A French court ruled that TotalEnergies misled consumers with claims that it would be carbon neutral by 2050. It was the first time France's greenwashing law had been used against an energy company. The court ordered the company to remove the misleading statements, set a fine of up to **€10,000 per day** if it did not comply, and required it to pay the NGOs that brought the case.

### Political denial as greenwashing: the "Anti-Woke Davos" (June 2026)
<!-- tag: uk -->

In June 2026 the UK issued red heat warnings for three days in a row, and a wildfire was burning in Derbyshire. At the same time, a conference in London hosted speakers attacking UK climate policy. It was organised by the Alliance for Responsible Citizenship, a group backed by the owner of GB News and a number of fossil fuel companies. Delegates reportedly sweltered inside the Olympia venue in temperatures above **35C** while applauding Trump's energy secretary Chris Wright, a former fossil fuel executive, who called Britain's green transition a "tragic mistake." The goodie bags included fans printed with the slogan "Free speech never felt so cool". The people in the room were denying the very heat they were sitting in.

## The EU anti-greenwashing rule
<!-- tag: eu -->

EU rules that came into force at the start of 2023, and are being phased in gradually, aim to stop businesses misleading consumers into thinking their products or services are more sustainable than they are. The EU's **Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)** tightens the rules on the social and environmental information companies must report. Its arrival worried many companies in Ireland, mainly about how accurate the data they now have to publish would be.
