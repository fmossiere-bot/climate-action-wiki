# Wet-Bulb Temperature Explained: Why Humidity Can Be More Dangerous Than Heat Alone

**Wet-bulb temperature** is measured by wrapping a thermometer bulb in a wet cloth and putting it in moving air, instead of reading the air temperature directly. As the water in the cloth evaporates, it cools the thermometer. How much cooling that achieves depends a great deal on humidity. In dry air, water evaporates quickly and the wet-bulb reading drops well below the actual air temperature. In humid air, evaporation slows down because the air is already nearly full of moisture, so the wet-bulb reading stays much closer to the air temperature. Because it captures heat and humidity together, wet-bulb temperature is a far better guide than air temperature alone to how dangerous a hot day is for the human body.

## Why It Matters for Survival

Humans are warm-blooded and rely on sweating to get rid of excess body heat: sweat evaporates off the skin and takes heat with it. This only works if evaporation is possible, and that is exactly what wet-bulb temperature measures. Once the wet-bulb temperature is high enough, sweat can no longer evaporate properly and the body loses its main way of cooling down, however much water a person drinks or however much shade they find.

Researchers have long treated a wet-bulb temperature of **35°C** as the theoretical upper limit for human survival. Above it, even a healthy, resting, well-hydrated person in the shade would eventually be unable to keep their core temperature under control. More recent research suggests the practical danger point is quite a bit lower, closer to a 31°C wet-bulb reading, especially for people who are not resting in ideal conditions. In the past, wet-bulb temperatures on Earth's surface very rarely went above 31°C anywhere, but such readings have started to appear in parts of South Asia and the Persian Gulf as the planet warms. ## Mapping the Risk

[Probable Futures](https://probablefutures.org/maps/?volume=heat&selected_map=days_above_28c_wet-bulb&map_version=latest&warming_scenario=1.5&map_projection=mercator) has an interactive map showing, for different levels of global warming, how many extra days a year each part of the world can expect above a 28°C wet-bulb temperature. That is well below the outer limit for survival, but it is still dangerous, especially for outdoor workers, older people and anyone without reliable access to cooling. Choosing the 1.5°C warming scenario on the map shows how even fairly modest further warming noticeably widens the areas exposed to these extra days of dangerous heat.
