# Wet-Bulb Temperature Explained: Why Humidity Can Be More Dangerous Than Heat Alone

**Wet-bulb temperature** is measured by wrapping a thermometer bulb in a wet cloth and exposing it to moving air, rather than reading air temperature directly. As the water on the cloth evaporates, it cools the thermometer, and how much cooling that evaporation can achieve depends heavily on humidity: in dry air, evaporation happens quickly and the wet-bulb reading drops well below the actual air temperature; in humid air, evaporation slows because the surrounding air is already close to saturated with moisture, so the wet-bulb reading stays much closer to the actual air temperature. Because it captures the combined effect of heat and humidity, wet-bulb temperature is a far better proxy than air temperature alone for how dangerous a hot day actually is to the human body.

## Why It Matters for Survival

Humans are warm-blooded and rely on sweating to shed excess body heat: sweat evaporates off the skin and carries heat away with it. This mechanism depends entirely on evaporation being possible, which is exactly what wet-bulb temperature measures. When the wet-bulb temperature gets high enough, sweat can no longer evaporate effectively, and the body loses its main tool for cooling itself, regardless of how much water someone drinks or how much shade they find.

Researchers have historically treated a wet-bulb temperature of **35°C** as the theoretical upper limit of human survivability: above it, even a healthy, resting, well-hydrated person in the shade would eventually be unable to keep their core body temperature in check. More recent research suggests the practical danger threshold is considerably lower, closer to a **31°C** wet-bulb reading, well before the theoretical limit, particularly for people who aren't resting in ideal conditions. Historically, wet-bulb temperatures on Earth's surface have very rarely exceeded 31°C anywhere, but instances have begun appearing in parts of South Asia and the Persian Gulf as the planet warms. ## Mapping the Risk

[Probable Futures](https://probablefutures.org/maps/?volume=heat&selected_map=days_above_28c_wet-bulb&map_version=latest&warming_scenario=1.5&map_projection=mercator) offers an interactive map showing, for different global warming scenarios, how many additional days per year different parts of the world can expect to exceed a 28°C wet-bulb temperature, a level well below the outer survivability limit but still dangerous, particularly for outdoor workers, older people, and anyone without reliable access to cooling. Selecting a 1.5°C warming scenario on the map illustrates how even relatively modest further warming meaningfully expands the parts of the world exposed to these extra dangerous-heat days.
