# AMOC Collapse and Carbon Release

## What is it

The **Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC)** is a huge system of ocean currents, including the Gulf Stream, that works like a giant conveyor belt. It carries warm, salty water from the Gulf of Mexico north towards Europe. There the water cools, sinks and flows back south along the ocean floor. Without it, European winters would be far colder, monsoons across Africa and Asia would shift, and sea levels along the US East Coast would rise faster.

Human-caused warming is already slowing the AMOC. Fresh meltwater from the Greenland ice sheet is diluting the salty surface water whose sinking drives the current. The AMOC has already weakened by around 15%. New computer modelling, published in 2026 in *Nature Communications Earth & Environment*, adds a worrying new element. If the AMOC collapses, it could set off a chain reaction in the Southern Ocean that releases up to 640 billion tonnes of CO₂ into the atmosphere, heating the planet by a further 0.2°C on top of all the other effects of a collapse.

## Why it matters

The AMOC is one of the most closely watched potential climate tipping points. A collapse would be a global event with knock-on effects everywhere, far beyond the North Atlantic. The new finding from Potsdam Institute researchers Da Nian and Johan Rockström raises the stakes again. As well as disrupting weather patterns, a collapse would work like a slow-burning carbon bomb, releasing CO₂ from deep ocean water over a thousand years.

The time left to prevent this is shrinking. Atmospheric CO₂ is currently around 430 parts per million. The study found that above 350 ppm, an AMOC collapse cannot be reversed once it starts. We are already past that level.

## The Southern Ocean carbon feedback

The deep waters of the Southern Ocean hold huge amounts of carbon, taken from the atmosphere and from dead plankton sinking over thousands of years. Normally, a layer of fresher water at the surface keeps this deep, carbon-rich water locked underneath.

While the AMOC is running, it sends less salty water south from the North Atlantic, which keeps that layering stable. If the AMOC shuts down, the stabilising effect is lost. Mixing in the Southern Ocean gets stronger, deep water rises to the surface, and much of its stored carbon escapes into the air.

The study modelled this under different future climate scenarios. It found the feedback would play out over more than 1,000 years, but the point at which it becomes locked in could come within the next 25 to 50 years.

## Other AMOC collapse impacts

As well as the carbon feedback, an AMOC collapse would:

- Cool the Arctic by around 7°C, freezing large areas of Canada, Scandinavia and Russia.
- Warm Antarctica by around 6°C, threatening the stability of the East Antarctic Ice Sheet. This is the largest mass of ice on Earth, and its collapse could raise global sea levels by several metres or more over centuries.
- Disrupt monsoons across Africa and Asia, putting food and water supplies for hundreds of millions of people at risk.
- Raise sea levels along the US East Coast faster than the global average.

## Summary findings

- The AMOC has already weakened by ~15% because Greenland meltwater is diluting the salty water that drives it.
- Collapse is projected anywhere from decades to centuries away, and the exact timing is still uncertain.
- At CO₂ concentrations above 350 ppm (currently 430 ppm), collapse appears to be irreversible.
- New 2026 modelling: collapse could release up to 640 billion tonnes of CO₂ from the Southern Ocean.
- Extra warming from that carbon release: 0.2°C, playing out over 1,000+ years.
- An AMOC collapse would cool the Arctic by 7°C and warm Antarctica by 6°C at the same time.
- Possible domino effect: a destabilised East Antarctic Ice Sheet, causing several metres or more of sea level rise over centuries.
- Rockström: "The commitment time may be within the next 25 to 50 years. It's literally now."

## AMOC Monitoring Under Threat

In a 2026 Guardian commentary, oceanographers Penny Holliday, Femke de Jong and Sjoerd Groeskamp warn that the world could lose its ability to track the AMOC just as the stakes are rising. If it collapses, Europe could see climate change happening **up to 10 times faster** than the current rate.

Regular AMOC monitoring only started around two decades ago, pieced together from separately funded national research projects. That fragile base is now under serious pressure. The Trump administration has proposed budget cuts to NASA, NOAA and the National Science Foundation, which between them provide around 50% of all AMOC monitoring funding. The US has also moved to scale back the Ocean Observatories Initiative, which was part of the tracking network.

The European Commission launched the OceanEye initiative in 2026, committing €50 million to ocean observation. OceanEye is not running yet, though, and the research ships that service the current monitoring need stop-gap funding now.

All AMOC monitoring worldwide costs roughly **€25 million per year**, about five cents per EU citizen. The authors urge the EU, the UK and international partners to treat AMOC monitoring as an urgent global priority, before the record is broken and the data is lost for good.
