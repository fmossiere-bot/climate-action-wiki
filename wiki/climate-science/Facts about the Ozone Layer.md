## 1. How the problem was discovered

In the 1970s and 1980s, scientists found that the ozone layer, the part of the atmosphere that shields life on Earth from harmful ultraviolet radiation, was thinning. The most dramatic sign was a large seasonal hole opening up over Antarctica. This was not a slow, natural change. It was linked directly to a group of man-made chemicals that had become common in everyday products.

## 2. The cause: CFC gases

The chemicals responsible were chlorofluorocarbons, known as CFCs. These gases were widely used from the mid-20th century onwards in refrigerators, air conditioning, aerosol sprays and foam packaging. Once released into the atmosphere, CFCs break down ozone molecules and also act as greenhouse gases, adding to the warming of the planet. Because they are so stable, they can remain in the atmosphere for decades.

## 3. The Montreal Protocol

The scale of the risk led to one of the most successful environmental agreements in history: the Montreal Protocol, signed in 1987. Under the agreement, countries committed to phasing out the production of CFCs. This phase-out ran from 1989 to 2010, and it is widely seen as a rare example of the world acting together, quickly, once the science was clear.

## 4. What happened since: a warning sign in the data

For years after the ban, CFC levels in the atmosphere declined steadily, as expected. But scientists later noticed something unusual: levels of CFC-11, the second most common CFC, started rising again from 2013 onwards, reaching a record high in 2020.

Investigations pointed to illegal production, largely in China. Some figures from research reported by New Scientist in April 2023 give a sense of the scale:

- Around 65,000 tonnes of CFCs are estimated to leak into the atmosphere every year.
- China alone appears responsible for around 10,000 tonnes a year, and for 40 to 60 percent of the rise in CFC-11 specifically.
- A small share of this may come as an unintended by-product of manufacturing HFCs, the chemicals developed to replace CFCs. However, this alone does not explain the size of the increase, which suggests a level of illicit or negligent production.

This was treated as an early warning sign, a reminder that even a well-enforced global ban needs ongoing monitoring, since gaps in enforcement can appear even decades after an agreement is signed.

## 5. Where things stand now

Despite this setback, the overall picture remains positive. According to the United Nations, the ozone layer is on track to fully recover, with different timelines depending on the region:

- By 2040 for most of the world.
- By 2045 over the Arctic.
- By 2066 over Antarctica, where the damage was most severe.

Importantly, the unexpected rise in CFC-11 emissions is not expected to seriously delay this recovery. The Montreal Protocol's earlier, sustained success appears to have built in enough of a buffer to absorb this setback.

## 6. The risks that remain

The CFC-11 episode shows that the ozone story is not entirely closed. It points to two ongoing risks. First, illegal production can still happen even under a long-standing international ban, which means monitoring and enforcement need to continue rather than being treated as finished business. Second, because CFCs are also greenhouse gases, any unauthorised production adds to climate warming as well as ozone damage, linking this issue directly to the broader climate agenda.

## Bottom line

The Montreal Protocol remains a genuine success story and a useful example of what coordinated global action can achieve. But the reappearance of illegal CFC-11 production shows that even a strong, long-running agreement needs continued vigilance. The recovery of the ozone layer is on track, but it depends on countries continuing to enforce the rules that made that recovery possible in the first place.
