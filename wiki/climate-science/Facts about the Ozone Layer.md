## 1. How the problem was discovered

In the 1970s and 1980s, scientists found that the ozone layer was thinning. This is the part of the atmosphere that shields life on Earth from harmful ultraviolet radiation. The clearest sign was a large hole that opened up over Antarctica each year. This was not a slow, natural change. It was linked directly to a group of man-made chemicals that had become common in everyday products.

## 2. The cause: CFC gases

The chemicals responsible were chlorofluorocarbons, known as **CFCs**. From the mid-20th century onwards they were widely used in fridges, air conditioning, aerosol sprays and foam packaging. Once released into the atmosphere, CFCs break down ozone molecules. They are also greenhouse gases, so they add to global warming. And because they are so stable, they can stay in the atmosphere for decades.

## 3. The Montreal Protocol

The size of the risk led to one of the most successful environmental agreements ever made: the **Montreal Protocol**, signed in 1987. Under it, countries committed to phasing out the production of CFCs. The phase-out ran from 1989 to 2010, and it is widely seen as a rare case of the world acting together, and quickly, once the science was clear.

## 4. What happened since: a warning sign in the data

For years after the ban, CFC levels in the atmosphere fell steadily, as expected. But scientists later noticed something odd: levels of CFC-11, the second most common CFC, started rising again from 2013, reaching a record high in 2020.

Investigations pointed to illegal production, mostly in China. Research reported by New Scientist in April 2023 gives a sense of the scale:

- Around 65,000 tonnes of CFCs are estimated to leak into the atmosphere every year.
- China alone appears responsible for around 10,000 tonnes a year, and for 40 to 60 percent of the rise in CFC-11 specifically.
- A small share may be an accidental by-product of making HFCs, the chemicals developed to replace CFCs. But this does not explain the size of the increase on its own, which points to some illegal or careless production.

Scientists treated this as an early warning. Even a well-enforced global ban needs ongoing monitoring, because gaps in enforcement can open up decades after an agreement is signed.

## 5. Where things stand now

Despite this setback, the overall picture is still good. According to the United Nations, the ozone layer is on track to recover fully, on different timelines depending on the region:

- By 2040 for most of the world.
- By 2045 over the Arctic.
- By 2066 over Antarctica, where the damage was worst.

The unexpected rise in CFC-11 emissions is not expected to delay this recovery seriously. The Montreal Protocol's long record of success seems to have built in enough of a buffer to absorb it.

## 6. The risks that remain

The CFC-11 episode shows the ozone story is not completely over. It points to two ongoing risks. First, illegal production can still happen under a long-standing international ban, so monitoring and enforcement need to carry on instead of being treated as finished. Second, because CFCs are also greenhouse gases, any unauthorised production adds to global warming as well as to ozone damage, which ties this issue directly to the wider climate agenda.

## In short

The Montreal Protocol is still a real success story and a useful example of what countries can achieve when they act together. But the return of illegal CFC-11 production shows that even a strong, long-running agreement needs constant attention. The ozone layer's recovery is on track, as long as countries keep enforcing the rules that made it possible.
