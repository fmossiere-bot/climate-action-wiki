# How the Jet Stream Makes Long European Heatwaves: Two Patterns Explained

## What is it

Europe's longest and deadliest heatwaves, the ones that last **two weeks or more**, are set up by particular shapes in the jet stream. New research by Duncan Pappert and Olivia Martius at the University of Bern, published in July 2026 in *Environmental Research Letters*, identifies two distinct atmospheric patterns behind these events. Only about 20 long heatwaves are well observed in the daily atmospheric record, which is too few to study properly. So the team ran a state-of-the-art climate model to generate 1,900 plausible hot spells and looked for what they had in common.

Both patterns involve **Rossby waves**, chains of weather systems that carry energy around the globe. These waves bend the polar jet stream, the belt of strong high-altitude winds that sits at roughly 60° north.

## Why it matters

Long heatwaves kill more people per day than short ones. Bodies stay under stress for longer, hydroelectric dams and farms are strained for weeks, and health services can be overwhelmed. The June 2026 heatwave killed an estimated **20,000 people in Europe**. Anything that helps forecasters see long heat spells coming earlier saves lives. Grid operators can ask power companies to get ready for the surge in air-conditioning demand, and farms, dams and hospitals can prepare for the strain.

Tim Woollings at the University of Oxford, commenting on the Bern paper, thinks it will be useful in practice: *"These patterns should help forecasters. This really clearly lays out the structure and evolution of these events, so people know what to look for."*

## Pattern one: the omega block

In the **type I pattern**, the jet stream gets wavier. It kinks up around the UK and Scandinavia and then swings south again, tracing a shape like the Greek letter Ω (omega). A persistent ridge of high pressure forms inside that omega shape, sitting over western Europe.

Normally, storms rolling in from the Atlantic would break up a ridge like this. In the type I pattern they do the opposite. They arrive on the western side of the omega and strengthen the spinning low-pressure system at its base, and that low pulls hot subtropical air north into western Europe.

The ridge is what produces the heat. The wider set-up of the atmosphere is what keeps the ridge in place, sometimes for weeks.

## Pattern two: the northward shift

In the **type II pattern**, the jet stream moves towards the pole. It again runs north of the UK, but in a straighter line rather than a wavy omega. A ridge forms over Europe, and the storm track follows the jet stream north of the continent. The low-pressure systems that would usually bring cooler, wetter Atlantic air are steered away, and warm subtropical air flows in from the south instead.

Pappert explains the difference between the two: *"It's always the ridge that's creating the heat, but how the atmosphere is setting up the situation for the ridge to be maintained, that's the difference."*

## The same feedback loop at ground level

In both patterns, once the ridge is in place, the same chain of events at the surface pushes temperatures higher:

- Air flows outward from the high-pressure ridge towards areas of lower pressure
- Dry air sinks from above to replace it, and is squeezed and heats up as it descends, the same physics that warms a bicycle pump
- No moist air rises, so no clouds form, and unbroken sunshine keeps heating the lower atmosphere
- The heat dries the land, less water evaporates, and a drier surface heats up more each day
- The result is a **positive feedback loop** that outlasts any single hot day

## How this played out in June 2026

The June 2026 heatwave, which killed roughly 20,000 people in Europe, began as a type II and then turned into a type I. Europe's current (July 2026) heatwave mostly resembles a type I, with a trough of low pressure to the west of a high-pressure ridge over Europe.

The two types describe average tendencies, not strict categories. Real events can move from one to the other, as June 2026 showed.

## What scientists don't know yet

Heatwaves are getting hotter, more frequent and longer because global warming is raising background temperatures. That much is settled. What is not settled is whether the particular jet-stream patterns that create long heatwaves are becoming **more common**. Some research suggests that high-pressure blocking linked to the jet stream will become more frequent and longer-lasting over Europe. Other modelling shows no change.

Woollings is frank about the gap: *"It is a concern that we don't know. We don't have a good enough understanding of these events really to know how their duration is changing."*

He also points out that the jet stream is shifting north in general, which could bring drier conditions to southern Europe. If the type I and II patterns did become more common or longer-lasting, *"it would be extremely damaging to economies and human health,"* he says.

## Key facts

- Long heatwaves (2+ weeks) kill more people per day than short ones
- Only about 20 long heatwaves are well observed in the daily atmospheric record, which is why the team simulated **1,900** plausible ones to find patterns
- Type I: a wavy jet stream traces an omega block over western Europe, and Atlantic storms reinforce the set-up
- Type II: a straighter jet stream shifted towards the pole steers the storm track north of the UK
- The June 2026 European heatwave killed about 20,000 people. It started as type II and turned into type I
- The jet stream is shifting north in general, which is likely to dry southern Europe
- Whether blocking patterns are becoming more common is not yet known: some studies find more, some find no change
