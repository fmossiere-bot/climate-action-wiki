# How the Jet Stream Makes Long European Heatwaves — Two Patterns Explained

## What is it

Europe's longest, deadliest heatwaves — the ones that last **two weeks or more** — are set up by very specific configurations of the **jet stream**. New research by **Duncan Pappert** and **Olivia Martius at the University of Bern**, published in July 2026 in *Environmental Research Letters*, identifies **two distinct atmospheric patterns** that lie behind these events. Because there are only about **20 well-observed long heatwaves in the daily atmospheric record**, the team ran a state-of-the-art climate model to generate **1,900 plausible hot spells** and looked for common structures.

Both patterns involve **Rossby waves** — chains of weather systems that transport energy around the globe — altering the **polar jet stream**, the high-altitude belt of strong winds at roughly **60° north**.

## Why it matters

Long heatwaves are **deadlier per day** than short ones, because bodies stay under stress for longer, hydroelectric dams and agriculture come under prolonged strain, and health services can be overwhelmed. The **June 2026 heatwave killed an estimated 20,000 people in Europe**. Anything that helps forecasters and grid operators anticipate long heat spells further in advance saves lives — grid operators can ask power companies to prepare to generate more electricity for the air-conditioning surge; farms, dams and hospitals can prepare for the strain.

Tim Woollings at the University of Oxford, quoted on the Bern paper: *"These patterns should help forecasters. This really clearly lays out the structure and evolution of these events, so people know what to look for."*

## Pattern one: the omega block

In the **type I pattern**, the jet stream gets **wavier**. It kinks up around the UK and Scandinavia and then veers south again, tracing out a shape like the Greek letter **Ω (omega)**. A **persistent high-pressure ridge** forms inside that omega shape, sitting over western Europe.

Normally, Atlantic storms rolling across the ocean would break up such a ridge. In the type I pattern they do the opposite: they arrive on the western side of the omega and **reinforce the low-pressure cyclonic circulation** at its base, and that spinning low **pulls hot subtropical air northwards** into western Europe.

The ridge is the thing generating the heat — but the atmospheric setup is what keeps the ridge in place, sometimes for weeks.

## Pattern two: the northward shift

In the **type II pattern**, the jet stream **shifts polewards** — again running north of the UK, but this time in a **straighter** line rather than a wavy omega. A ridge forms over Europe, and the **storm track follows the jet stream north of the continent**, so low-pressure systems that would normally bring cooler, wetter Atlantic air are diverted away. Warm subtropical air floods in from the south instead.

Pappert: *"It's always the ridge that's creating the heat — but how the atmosphere is setting up the situation for the ridge to be maintained, that's the difference."*

## The same feedback loop at ground level

In both patterns, once the ridge is in place, the same surface-level cascade drives temperatures higher:

- Air flows outward from the high-pressure ridge toward lower-pressure areas
- Dry air descending from above to replace it is **compressed and heats up** — the same physics as pumping air into a bicycle tyre
- No moist air rises, so **no clouds form**; unbroken sunshine keeps heating the lower atmosphere
- The heat dries the land; evaporation drops; a drier surface heats more each day
- The whole thing becomes a **positive feedback loop** that outlasts any single hot day

## How this played out in June 2026

The June 2026 heatwave — the one that killed roughly 20,000 people in Europe — started as a **type II** and then morphed into a **type I**. Europe's current (July 2026) heatwave largely resembles a type I, with a trough of low pressure to the west of a high-pressure ridge over Europe.

The two types are **averaged tendencies, not hard categories**. Real events can shift between them, as June 2026 showed.

## The uncertainty that matters

Heatwaves are **getting hotter, more frequent and longer-lasting** because global warming is raising background temperatures. That much is settled. What is not settled is whether the **specific jet-stream patterns that create long heatwaves are getting more common**. Some research suggests high-pressure blocking related to the jet stream will become more frequent and more persistent over Europe. Other modelling shows no change.

Woollings: *"It is a concern that we don't know. We don't have a good enough understanding of these events really to know how their duration is changing."*

He also notes the jet stream is **shifting northwards in general**, which could bring drier conditions to southern Europe. If the type I and II patterns did become more common or longer-lasting, *"it would be extremely damaging to economies and human health,"* he says.

## Key facts

- **Long heatwaves** (2+ weeks) are deadlier per day than short ones
- Only **~20 long heatwaves** are well-observed in the daily atmospheric record — hence the need to simulate **1,900** plausible ones to find patterns
- **Type I**: wavy jet stream tracing an **omega block** over western Europe; Atlantic storms reinforce the setup
- **Type II**: **poleward-shifted** straighter jet stream diverting storm track north of the UK
- **June 2026** European heatwave killed ~**20,000 people**; started as type II, morphed into type I
- Jet stream is **shifting northwards** in general, likely drying southern Europe
- **Trend in blocking patterns is not yet resolved** — some studies find more, some no change
