# The Work of Climate Science: Observation, Statistics and Models

How do scientists know the climate is changing, and that people are causing it? The answer rests on careful observation, statistical analysis and models built on physics, all working together. This page covers what controls Earth's temperature, how experts measure and model it, which organisations do the leading work, and where to find reliable climate science.

## How Scientists Observe the Climate

Earth's temperature depends on three things: the energy coming in from the sun, [Earth's albedo](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1918770116) (how much of that energy is reflected rather than absorbed, also covered on the [Cool Roofs page](../solutions/energy-efficiency/Cool%20Roofs%20-%20A%20Simple%20Idea%20with%20a%20Surprisingly%20Large%20Impact.md)), and how much energy the atmosphere holds on to through the greenhouse effect. Models built on physics alone are not enough, though. Climate scientists also collect observations and use statistics to find real-world links between the things that matter, such as greenhouse gas levels and temperature.

An international collaboration called the **Global Climate Observing System (GCOS)** tracks 55 "Essential Climate Variables" to describe Earth's climate system. Two of them matter most for public debate about climate change: carbon dioxide and global average surface temperature.

**CO2** is measured at around a hundred sites worldwide, backed up by satellite data. It can be compared with records going back hundreds of thousands of years thanks to ice cores, where trapped air bubbles keep a record of what the atmosphere used to contain. Temperature is measured on land, at sea from ships and buoys, and from satellites. These readings are combined into global datasets that show "anomalies", meaning how far temperatures differ from long-term averages. The datasets handle gaps in the data differently and vary in how much detail they give, but they broadly agree on the trend.

**Aerosols**, tiny particles in the air, make the picture more complicated. Since 1850, aerosols, mostly from burning fossil fuels, have reduced global warming by around 0.4°C or more by reflecting sunlight back into space. That creates an uncomfortable trade-off. Cutting fossil fuel use also removes this hidden cooling, so measured warming will go up even as the underlying cause is being tackled.

## Statistics, Correlation and Causation

Raw observations do not tell us much on their own. To make sense of temperature and CO2 data, and to answer questions such as how fast temperatures are rising, how closely that follows greenhouse gas levels, and how confident we can be in projections, we need statistics.

A common mistake, especially in the media, is to confuse correlation with causation. In the past, the link between CO2 and temperature often ran the other way. Before human activity became a dominant force, natural warming cycles caused more ocean mixing, which released CO2 from the oceans, which then added to the warming. In the deep past, in that sense, rising temperature caused rising CO2.

Today the relationship is understood to run in the other direction: human CO2 emissions are driving temperatures up. That conclusion rests on the physics of how greenhouse gases trap heat, together with statistical analysis of how temperature and CO2 trends relate over time. Observations, physical principles and statistics are combined to build the climate models covered next.

## How Climate Models Work

A climate model is a physics-based simulation of Earth's climate system. Models remain central to climate science, even though there is some evidence they can underestimate certain impacts. This happens especially where feedback loops make effects build on each other faster than expected, which has been suggested for [heat waves](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-022-01520-4), storms and Arctic warming.

**Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs)** go further. They add people, policy and economics to the physics of the climate, and are used more and more to guide policy choices. According to Dr Gavin Schmidt, Director of NASA's Goddard Institute for Space Studies, the biggest source of imprecision in these models is people rather than physics, because human behaviour is far harder to predict than the planet. IAMs have also been criticised for resting on disputed economic assumptions, and for building in unfair biases against the Global South in how they share out the burden of cutting emissions.

### Climate Sensitivity

Climate sensitivity measures how much the planet is expected to warm for a given radiative forcing (the imbalance between energy coming in and energy going out). There are three main versions:

- **Equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS)**: the long-term temperature rise once the effects of doubled CO2 concentration have fully settled
- **Transient climate response (TCR)**: how much warmer Earth is at the moment CO2 concentration actually doubles compared with pre-industrial levels (~280ppm), which is closer to how CO2 is rising in practice
- **Earth system sensitivity (ESS)**: includes very long-term feedbacks such as changes in ice sheets or plant cover

Climate sensitivity has long been one of the biggest uncertainties in climate projections. It is estimated from direct observation, from evidence about Earth's distant past, and from computer models. The IPCC's AR6 report narrowed the uncertainty a good deal. It is *very likely* (9-in-10 odds) that doubling atmospheric CO2 from pre-industrial levels would cause 2-5°C of warming, *likely* (2-in-3 odds) that it would fall between 2.5-4°C with a central estimate of 3°C, and *virtually certain* that it would exceed 1.5°C. AR6 warns, though, that it cannot rule out ECS values above 5°C.

Despite these uncertainties, models have a strong track record. [A study led by climate scientist Zeke Hausfather](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2019GL085378) found that past climate models predicted the following decades of observed warming well. Being unsure of the exact number is not the same as the models getting the overall picture wrong.

To keep results comparable across the many institutions that build climate models, the **Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP)** provides a shared framework, so scientists can analyse, test and improve models in a consistent way.

## The Institutions Behind Climate Science

The **IPCC** (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change) is the United Nations body that assesses climate science. It draws on the work of thousands of scientists worldwide to publish regular Assessment Reports on the physical science, the impacts, and the options for adapting and cutting emissions. Its most recent full cycle, AR6, is the current standard reference for international climate research and policy.

The **UNFCCC** (United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change) is a separate UN body that hosts the yearly Conference of the Parties, or COP. Unlike the IPCC, the UNFCCC does not produce science. It is a forum where national governments negotiate climate policy, using the IPCC's findings as their evidence. COPs have run since 1995, and COP21 in Paris (2015) produced the Paris Agreement, under which countries set their own emissions cuts aimed at keeping warming below 2°C, ideally 1.5°C.

If you want to follow climate science directly, [Carbon Brief](https://www.carbonbrief.org/) and [Skeptical Science](https://skepticalscience.com/) are two widely respected, evidence-based sources, as are individual scientists and communicators such as Zeke Hausfather and Katharine Hayhoe.

Climate scientists in the Global South also face structural barriers, from limited access to supercomputers to basic safety concerns, as [documented by Carbon Brief in 2021](https://www.carbonbrief.org/researchers-the-barriers-to-climate-science-in-the-global-south/). Carbon Brief and the Reuters Institute's Oxford Climate Journalism Network have since launched the [Global South Climate Database](https://www.carbonbrief.org/global-south-climate-database/) to help journalists reach climate experts across Asia, Africa, Latin America, the Caribbean and the Pacific.

## The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report (AR6)

AR6 came out in instalments from August 2021, ending with a [Synthesis Report](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/sixth-assessment-report-cycle/) in March 2023. Its main finding is that, without drastic emissions cuts, global surface temperatures are on course to reach 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels in the early 2030s.

The report is not all bad news. It also records real progress: between 2010 and 2019, unit costs fell 85% for solar energy, 55% for wind energy and 85% for lithium-ion batteries, and use of all three grew a great deal. As climate scientist Joeri Rogelj [told the New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/03/20/climate/global-warming-ipcc-earth.html), "it's not that if we go past 1.5 degrees everything is lost... there's clear evidence that 1.5 is better than 1.6, which is better than 1.7, and so on. The point is we need to do everything we can to keep warming as low as possible."

A major criticism of AR6 is about fairness. Its integrated assessment models look at human and Earth systems together, but they do not take account of which countries carry the most historical responsibility for emissions. Their assumptions arguably rely on developing countries doing a disproportionate share of future work. The wording of IPCC summary reports is also shaped by intense international lobbying. For the 2023 report, [the final draft was largely shaped by rich countries](https://carboncopy.info/ipccs-climate-reports-reveal-an-unequal-science/) that stayed in the negotiations after delegates from many poorer countries had already left. Critics argue that, as a result, the report may understate how much urgent action is really needed.
