# The Work of Climate Science: Observation, Statistics and Models

How do scientists actually know the climate is changing, and that human activity is the cause? It comes down to three things working together: careful observation, statistical analysis, and models built on physics. This page covers what controls Earth's temperature, how experts measure and model it, which organisations do the cutting-edge work, and where to find reliable climate science information.

## How Scientists Observe the Climate

Earth's temperature is controlled by incoming energy from the sun, [Earth's albedo](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1918770116) (how much solar energy is reflected versus absorbed, also covered on the [Cool Roofs page](../solutions/energy-efficiency/Cool%20Roofs%20-%20A%20Simple%20Idea%20with%20a%20Surprisingly%20Large%20Impact.md)), and the amount of energy retained by the atmosphere through the greenhouse effect. But models built on physics alone aren't enough. Climate scientists also gather observations and run statistical analysis to find real-world relationships between the variables that matter, like greenhouse gas levels and temperature.

An international collaboration called the **Global Climate Observing System (GCOS)** tracks 55 "Essential Climate Variables" to characterise the Earth's climate system. Two matter most for the public conversation on climate change: carbon dioxide and global average surface temperature.

**CO2** is measured at around a hundred sites worldwide, backed by satellite data, and can be compared against historical records stretching back hundreds of thousands of years thanks to ice cores, where trapped air bubbles preserve a record of past atmospheric composition. **Temperature** is measured on land, at sea via ships and buoys, and from satellites, then combined into global datasets that reveal "anomalies," meaning temperatures that deviate from long-term averages. Different datasets vary in how they handle sparse data and how much detail they provide, but they broadly agree on the trend.

**Aerosols** complicate the picture. Since 1850, aerosols, mostly from burning fossil fuels, have actually reduced global warming by around 0.4°C or more by reflecting incoming solar radiation. This creates an uncomfortable trade-off: cutting fossil fuel use also removes that masking cooling effect, which will push measured warming higher even as the underlying cause is addressed.

## Statistics, Correlation and Causation

Raw observations aren't useful on their own. Finding meaning in temperature and CO2 data, and answering questions like how fast temperatures are rising, how tightly that's tied to greenhouse gas levels, and how confident we can be in future projections, requires statistics.

A common error, especially in media coverage, is conflating correlation with causation. Historically, the relationship between CO2 and temperature actually ran the other way: before human activity became a dominant force, natural warming cycles caused more ocean mixing and CO2 release from the oceans, which then amplified the warming. In that sense, in the deep past, rising temperature caused rising CO2.

Today's relationship is understood to run the other direction, with anthropogenic emissions of CO2 driving temperature increases. That claim rests on physical understanding of how greenhouse gases trap heat, combined with statistical analysis of how temperature and CO2 trends actually relate over time. Observations, physical principles and statistics are combined to build the climate models covered next.

## How Climate Models Work

A climate model is a physics-based simulation of the Earth's climate system, and models remain central to climate science even though there is some evidence they can underestimate certain impacts, particularly where positive feedback loops cause effects to compound faster than expected (this has been suggested for [heat waves](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-022-01520-4), storms and Arctic warming).

**Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs)** go further, incorporating people, policy and economics alongside physical climate dynamics, and are increasingly used to guide policy choices. According to Dr Gavin Schmidt, Director of NASA's Goddard Institute for Space Studies, the biggest source of imprecision in these models isn't physics, it's people: human behaviour is far less predictable than planetary physics. IAMs have also been criticised for resting on contested economic assumptions and for embedding unjust biases against the Global South in how they allocate the burden of emissions cuts.

### Climate Sensitivity

Climate sensitivity measures how much the planet is expected to warm for a given radiative forcing (the imbalance between incoming and outgoing energy). There are three main versions:

- **Equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS)**: the long-term temperature rise once the effects of doubled CO2 concentration reach a steady state
- **Transient climate response (TCR)**: how much warmer Earth is at the moment CO2 concentration actually doubles relative to pre-industrial levels (~280ppm), which is closer to how CO2 is actually rising in practice
- **Earth system sensitivity (ESS)**: includes very long-term feedbacks such as changes in ice sheets or vegetation cover

Climate sensitivity has long been one of the biggest uncertainties in climate projections, estimated through direct observation, indirect estimates from Earth's distant past, and computer models. The IPCC's AR6 report narrowed this uncertainty significantly: it is *very likely* (9-in-10 odds) that doubling atmospheric CO2 from pre-industrial levels would cause 2-5°C of warming, *likely* (2-in-3 odds) to fall between 2.5-4°C with a central estimate of 3°C, and *virtually certain* to exceed 1.5°C, though AR6 cautions it cannot rule out ECS values above 5°C.

Despite these uncertainties, models have a strong track record: [a study led by climate scientist Zeke Hausfather](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2019GL085378) found that past climate models have predicted subsequent decades of observed warming well. Uncertainty in the exact number is not the same as the models being wrong about the overall picture.

To keep results comparable across the many institutions building climate models, the **Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP)** provides a shared framework so scientists can analyse, validate and improve models systematically.

## The Institutions Behind Climate Science

The **IPCC** (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change) is the United Nations body that assesses climate science, drawing on the work of thousands of scientists worldwide to publish periodic Assessment Reports on the physical science, impacts, and options for adaptation and mitigation. Its most recent full cycle, **AR6**, is the current definitive reference for international climate research and policy.

The **UNFCCC** (United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change) is a separate UN body that hosts the annual Conference of the Parties, or **COP**. Unlike the IPCC, the UNFCCC does not produce science; it is a forum where national governments negotiate climate policy using the IPCC's scientific findings as their evidence base. COPs have run since 1995, and COP21 in Paris (2015) produced the Paris Agreement, committing signatories to self-determined emissions cuts aimed at keeping warming below 2°C, ideally 1.5°C.

For readers who want to follow climate science directly, [Carbon Brief](https://www.carbonbrief.org/) and [Skeptical Science](https://skepticalscience.com/) are two widely respected, evidence-based sources, alongside individual scientists and communicators such as Zeke Hausfather and Katharine Hayhoe.

It's also worth noting the structural barriers facing climate scientists in the Global South, from limited access to supercomputing resources to basic security concerns, [documented by Carbon Brief in 2021](https://www.carbonbrief.org/researchers-the-barriers-to-climate-science-in-the-global-south/). Carbon Brief and the Reuters Institute's Oxford Climate Journalism Network have since launched the [Global South Climate Database](https://www.carbonbrief.org/global-south-climate-database/) to help journalists reach climate experts across Asia, Africa, Latin America, the Caribbean and the Pacific.

## The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report (AR6)

AR6 was published in instalments starting August 2021, concluding with a [Synthesis Report](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/sixth-assessment-report-cycle/) in March 2023. Its headline finding: without drastic emissions cuts, global surface temperatures are on track to reach 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels in the early 2030s.

The report isn't purely bleak. It also notes real progress: between 2010 and 2019, unit costs fell 85% for solar energy, 55% for wind energy, and 85% for lithium-ion batteries, with large increases in deployment across all three. As climate scientist Joeri Rogelj [told the New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/03/20/climate/global-warming-ipcc-earth.html), "it's not that if we go past 1.5 degrees everything is lost... there's clear evidence that 1.5 is better than 1.6, which is better than 1.7, and so on. The point is we need to do everything we can to keep warming as low as possible."

A significant critique of AR6 concerns equity. Its integrated assessment models factor in human and Earth systems together but do not weight which countries bear the most historic responsibility for emissions, and their underlying assumptions arguably lean on developing countries taking disproportionate future action. The wording of IPCC summary reports is also shaped by intense international lobbying; in the 2023 report's case, [the final draft was largely shaped by rich countries](https://carboncopy.info/ipccs-climate-reports-reveal-an-unequal-science/) that remained in negotiations after delegates from many poorer countries had already left. Critics argue this means the report may understate how much urgent action is genuinely needed.
