# Climate Tipping Points — what they are, which are already close, and where the good ones sit

## What is it

A **tipping point** is a moment when a small push produces a change that keeps itself going. Once the balance shifts, the system settles into a new state that is fast to reach, hard to stop and hard to undo. The mechanism behind it is called a **reinforcing feedback**: an effect that amplifies its own cause. Climate scientists use the term for parts of the Earth system that could switch state at a given warming level, called **tipping elements** (a term coined by John Schellnhuber and Tim Lenton in 2008). The same idea also describes rapid changes in society and technology: this page uses **"positive tipping points"** for the good ones.

## Why it matters

Tipping points overturn the standard "warming = damage in proportion" story. A small change in one place — a temperature threshold crossed, a subsidy switched, a technology cost that finally undercuts fossils — can trigger effects that are large, sudden and self-propelling. Understanding which climate systems can tip, at what level, and how positive tipping works in society is the difference between planning for gradual change and planning for abrupt change.

## The 16 climate tipping elements

Lenton originally listed 9 in 2008; the updated list has **16**, and **5 are already in the danger zone at today's ~1.25°C of warming**. Not every big change is a tipping point: not every reinforcing feedback is strong enough to keep itself going.

### The closest ones

- **Greenland ice sheet** — as it melts, the surface drops into warmer air and melts more. Slow but unstoppable past a point. Holds **7 metres of sea-level rise**
- **West Antarctica (Thwaites and Pine Island glaciers)** — sit below sea level; once the ice retreats, it retreats faster. **1.5°C could be enough to lose it all**, giving over **3 metres of sea-level rise** over centuries
- **Winter Arctic sea ice** — the Barents Sea could tip at just over 1.5°C, which would affect Europe's weather; the whole Arctic could tip around 4.5°C
- **AMOC (the big Atlantic "conveyor belt")** — Lenton calls this "the biggest risk of all". It has already slowed about **15% in 50 years**. If it collapses, farming in the UK would end, tropical rains would move south, and the wheat and maize belts would be cut in half worldwide. See [AMOC Collapse and Carbon Release](amoc-collapse-carbon.md) for the specific mechanism
- **Subpolar gyre (North Atlantic)** — a smaller current that fresh meltwater can switch off. Could happen within 10 years, somewhere between 1.1 and 3.8°C. Result: harsher winters and hotter summer heatwaves in Europe. **"Almost nobody has heard of it, but it may be one of the nearest tipping points."**

### Land and forest

- **Permafrost** — holds about **twice the CO2 humans have emitted so far**. Local sudden thaw has already started. Large-scale thaw likely around 1.5°C, full collapse around 4°C, would add about **10% to warming**
- **Northern forests** — could spread north (which warms the planet because dark trees replace white snow) and die back in the south from fires and bark beetles. Soot from fires lands on ice and speeds up melting
- **Amazon** — 18% already cut down; warming plus deforestation could turn around **40% of what is left into dry savanna**. The forest makes about a third of its own rain

### Ocean and ecosystems

- **Coral reefs — 70-90% lost at 1.5°C, almost all at 2°C**. Up to 500 million people depend on them
- **West African monsoon** — could collapse if AMOC weakens; could actually get stronger and green the Sahel if AMOC stays strong (a rare good tipping point in this list)
- **Mountain glaciers** — most are lost past 2°C, cutting water supply for farming in Asia
- **East Antarctica** — mostly safe unless warming passes 5°C; some parts vulnerable above 2°C

### How they connect

Mostly, **one tipping point makes others more likely, like dominoes**. A full chain reaction leading to a "Hothouse Earth" is unlikely but cannot be ruled out.

## What is not a tipping point

Not every threshold counts. **The last dodo being shot, or a party winning an election, has no self-propelling feedback behind it.** Not every reinforcing feedback is strong enough either. Overstating tipping points makes people feel powerless; understating them makes us too optimistic.

## The warning sign: slowing down

A system near a tipping point recovers more slowly from small shocks and swings more widely. "Resilience" means recovering fast; a system loses resilience before it tips. **Scientists have found this early-warning slowdown in Greenland, in the AMOC, and in the Amazon.** It could become a general early-warning system if we can prove the system can tip at all.

## Positive tipping points

Tipping points can also work in the good direction. The same mechanics that build climate risk can build climate solutions, if the right feedbacks are triggered.

### The Norway EV S-curve

Battery cars went from **0.25% of new sales in 2010 to 84% in 2024**, while petrol and diesel fell from 94% to 4%. Growth followed an "S-curve": slow, then very fast, then levelling off. Worldwide, electric car sales double about every year and a half; three more doublings and they are the majority. The three reinforcing feedbacks were:

1. **Learning by doing (Wright's Law):** the more we make something, the better we get. Aircraft labour time fell 20% each time production doubled. Solar and batteries got about 10× cheaper in a decade
2. **Economies of scale:** the more we make, the cheaper it gets
3. **Supporting technology:** more chargers → more EVs → more chargers

Norway's tax policy made EVs the same price as petrol cars, which tipped buyers.

### UK coal

Coal fell from **40% of UK electricity in 2012 to 2% in 2019**. The key was a rising minimum price on carbon from 2013, which made coal plants lose money almost overnight. Wind, solar and biomass filled the gap (not gas), doubling every four years. Almost nobody noticed.

### Wind and solar today

Wind and solar now **double worldwide about every three years**. Solar is forecast to supply over half of the world's electricity by mid-century.

### Money can tip too

When UK coal started losing money, investors pulled out, which made coal weaker, so more investors pulled out. That is a bank-run mechanic working in a good direction.

## Where do tipping points come from historically

Tipping points have happened before in both climate and society, so they can happen again. Some past examples Lenton uses:

- **Dansgaard-Oeschger events** — 25 sudden warmings during the last ice age, when Greenland warmed by up to 10°C in sometimes just ten years, then cooled again. The cause was the AMOC switching between strong and weak states
- **Ice ages** — small changes in Earth's orbit trigger large responses through reinforcing feedbacks inside the climate system
- **Farming (10,000 years ago)** — invented separately at least six to ten times. Once populations grew larger than the wild land could feed, going back meant starving
- **Complex societies** — cities, rulers, writing appeared in Mesopotamia ~7,000 years ago, then Egypt, Indus Valley and China. Joe Tainter's idea: complexity grows to solve hard problems
- **Industrial Revolution (Britain, ~1760-1780)** — the "Malthusian trap" broke as coal, iron and water power, capitalism, stable laws and the Scientific Revolution came together
- **The Great Acceleration (post-WWII)** — fossil-fuelled growth speeded up sharply, damaging climate and nature at global scale

## Bad tipping points, good tipping points

Lenton's argument in one line: **bad tipping points in climate and society are our biggest risk; good tipping points in society, technology and nature are our best hope.** The book's premise is that step-by-step change is now too slow, so only tipping points can move fast enough.

**Simon Sharpe** (Lenton's colleague) argues we must go **"Five Times Faster"** — cutting emissions ~7% a year — which gives good odds of staying below 2°C and maybe 1.7°C if we get there fast. **Ten times faster** would keep 1.5°C alive; the UK power sector already cut 10% a year for a while, so it has been done, but not across every sector at once.
