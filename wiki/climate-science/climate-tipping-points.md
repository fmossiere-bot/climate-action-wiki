# Climate Tipping Points: what they are, which are already close, and where the good ones sit

## What is it

A **tipping point** is a moment when a small push sets off a change that keeps itself going. Once the balance shifts, the system settles into a new state that is quick to reach, hard to stop and hard to undo. What drives it is a reinforcing feedback, an effect that strengthens its own cause. Climate scientists use the term for parts of the Earth system that could switch state at a certain level of warming. These are called tipping elements, a term coined by John Schellnhuber and Tim Lenton in 2008. The same idea also describes fast changes in society and technology, and this page calls the helpful ones "positive tipping points".

## Why it matters

Tipping points upset the usual idea that damage grows in step with warming. A small change in one place, such as a temperature threshold being crossed, a subsidy being switched, or a technology finally becoming cheaper than fossil fuels, can set off effects that are large, sudden and self-sustaining. Knowing which climate systems can tip, at what level, and how positive tipping works in society, decides whether we plan for gradual change or for sudden change.

## The 16 climate tipping elements

Lenton first listed 9 in 2008. The updated list has 16, and **5 are already in the danger zone at today's ~1.25°C of warming**. Not every big change is a tipping point, because not every reinforcing feedback is strong enough to keep itself going.

### The closest ones

- **Greenland ice sheet**: as it melts, its surface sinks into warmer air and melts more. It is slow, but past a certain point it cannot be stopped. It holds 7 metres of sea-level rise
- **West Antarctica (Thwaites and Pine Island glaciers)**: these sit below sea level, so once the ice starts to retreat, it retreats faster. 1.5°C could be enough to lose it all, giving over 3 metres of sea-level rise over centuries
- **Winter Arctic sea ice**: the Barents Sea could tip at just over 1.5°C, which would affect Europe's weather. The whole Arctic could tip at around 4.5°C
- **AMOC (the big Atlantic "conveyor belt")**: Lenton calls this "the biggest risk of all". It has already slowed by about 15% in 50 years. If it collapses, farming in the UK would end, tropical rains would move south, and the world's wheat and maize belts would be cut in half. [AMOC Collapse and Carbon Release](amoc-collapse-carbon.md) explains how it works
- **Subpolar gyre (North Atlantic)**: a smaller current that fresh meltwater can switch off. This could happen within 10 years, somewhere between 1.1 and 3.8°C. The result would be harsher winters and hotter summer heatwaves in Europe. "Almost nobody has heard of it, but it may be one of the nearest tipping points."

### Land and forest

- **Permafrost**: holds about twice the CO2 humans have emitted so far. Sudden local thaw has already started. Large-scale thaw is likely at around 1.5°C and full collapse at around 4°C, which would add about 10% to warming
- **Northern forests**: these could spread north (which warms the planet, because dark trees replace white snow) and die back in the south from fires and bark beetles. Soot from the fires lands on ice and makes it melt faster
- **Amazon**: 18% has already been cut down. Warming plus deforestation could turn around 40% of what is left into dry savanna. The forest makes about a third of its own rain

### Ocean and ecosystems

- **Coral reefs**: 70-90% lost at 1.5°C, and almost all at 2°C. Up to 500 million people depend on them
- **West African monsoon**: could collapse if the AMOC weakens, or could get stronger and turn the Sahel green if the AMOC stays strong (a rare helpful tipping point on this list)
- **Mountain glaciers**: most are lost past 2°C, cutting the water supply for farming in Asia
- **East Antarctica**: mostly safe unless warming passes 5°C, though some parts are vulnerable above 2°C

### How they connect

In most cases, **one tipping point makes others more likely, like dominoes**. A full chain reaction leading to a "Hothouse Earth" is unlikely, but it cannot be ruled out.

## What is not a tipping point

Not every threshold counts. The last dodo being shot, or a party winning an election, has no self-sustaining feedback behind it. Not every reinforcing feedback is strong enough either. Overstating tipping points makes people feel powerless, and understating them makes us too optimistic.

## The warning sign: slowing down

A system close to a tipping point recovers more slowly from small shocks and swings more widely. "Resilience" means bouncing back quickly, and a system loses resilience before it tips. **Scientists have found this early-warning slowdown in Greenland, in the AMOC and in the Amazon.** It could become a general early-warning system, provided we can show that the system is able to tip at all.

## Positive tipping points

Tipping points can also work in a good direction. The same mechanics that build up climate risk can build up climate solutions, if the right feedbacks are set off.

### The Norway EV S-curve

Battery cars went from **0.25% of new sales in 2010 to 84% in 2024**, while petrol and diesel fell from 94% to 4%. Growth followed an "S-curve": slow, then very fast, then levelling off. Worldwide, electric car sales double about every year and a half, so three more doublings would make them the majority. Three reinforcing feedbacks were at work:

1. Learning by doing (Wright's Law): the more we make something, the better we get at it. The labour needed to build an aircraft fell by 20% each time production doubled. Solar and batteries got about 10× cheaper in a decade
2. Economies of scale: the more we make, the cheaper it gets
3. Supporting technology: more chargers → more EVs → more chargers

Norway's tax policy made EVs cost the same as petrol cars, which tipped buyers over.

### UK coal

Coal fell from **40% of UK electricity in 2012 to 2% in 2019**. The turning point was a rising minimum price on carbon from 2013, which made coal plants lose money almost overnight. Wind, solar and biomass filled the gap (not gas), doubling every four years. Hardly anyone noticed.

### Wind and solar today

Wind and solar now **double worldwide about every three years**. Solar is forecast to supply over half of the world's electricity by the middle of the century.

### Money can tip too

When UK coal started losing money, investors pulled out. That made coal weaker, so more investors pulled out. It is the mechanics of a run on a bank, working in a good direction.

## Where do tipping points come from historically

Tipping points have happened before, in both climate and society, so they can happen again. Some past examples Lenton uses:

- **Dansgaard-Oeschger events**: 25 sudden warmings during the last ice age, when Greenland warmed by up to 10°C, sometimes in just ten years, then cooled again. They were caused by the AMOC switching between strong and weak states
- **Ice ages**: small changes in Earth's orbit set off large responses through reinforcing feedbacks inside the climate system
- **Farming (10,000 years ago)**: invented separately at least six to ten times. Once populations grew larger than wild land could feed, going back meant starving
- **Complex societies**: cities, rulers and writing appeared in Mesopotamia ~7,000 years ago, then in Egypt, the Indus Valley and China. Joe Tainter's idea is that complexity grows to solve hard problems
- **Industrial Revolution (Britain, ~1760-1780)**: the "Malthusian trap" broke as coal, iron and water power, capitalism, stable laws and the Scientific Revolution came together
- **The Great Acceleration (post-WWII)**: fossil-fuelled growth sped up sharply, damaging climate and nature on a global scale

## Bad tipping points, good tipping points

Lenton sums up his argument this way: **bad tipping points in climate and society are our biggest risk, and good tipping points in society, technology and nature are our best hope.** The starting point of his book is that step-by-step change is now too slow, so only tipping points can move fast enough.

Simon Sharpe, a colleague of Lenton's, argues we must go "Five Times Faster", cutting emissions by ~7% a year. That gives good odds of staying below 2°C, and maybe 1.7°C if we get there quickly. Ten times faster would keep 1.5°C within reach. The UK power sector did cut emissions by 10% a year for a while, so it has been done, though not across every sector at once.

## September 2026: which positive tipping points are closest?

A follow-up **University of Exeter and Earthshot Prize report** (September 2026, lead author Dr Steve Smith) applies Lenton's framework to 51 priority areas across five environmental goals: protecting nature, cleaning the air, reviving oceans, eliminating waste and tackling the climate crisis.

Its main finding is that **clean electricity is the transition with the most leverage**. Cheaper solar and wind set off a chain of benefits: better economics for EVs, cheaper heat pumps, cleaner industry and less air pollution, all of which then increase demand for more renewables. Solar and wind have already passed positive tipping points in many major markets. Coordinated policies across power, transport and heating could bring each sector's tipping point forward by 2 to 8 years.

Other levers close to their positive tipping points:

- **Global commodity supply chains** (beef, soy, palm oil, timber, cocoa, coffee): a small number of firms, banks and regulators control this trade. Lining up EU, China, UK and US standards could make deforestation-free products the global norm
- **Clean cooking and household energy**: pay-as-you-go and small-scale solar are close to a tipping point for the 2.1 billion people still cooking with solid fuels
- **Methane abatement**: Smith calls it *"one of the fastest levers we have available for limiting peak warming"*, and satellites and drones now reveal leaks that used to be invisible
- **Adaptation itself**: flood defences and resilient infrastructure reduce losses, which lowers insurance and borrowing costs, which attracts more investment

**The problem is where the money goes, more than how much there is.** The report cites the UN State of Finance for Nature 2026: $7.3 trillion per year goes to activities that damage nature, against $220 billion per year for nature-based solutions. That is a ratio of about $33 spent damaging nature for every $1 spent protecting it. As Smith puts it, *"That's a political choice and it's not a lack of finance. It's where it's used."*

For the biggest lever, clean electricity, the obstacle is **where investment goes**: Africa has 60% of the world's best solar resources but attracts under 2% of global clean-energy investment.
