# Local Impact and Extreme Events Attribution: When Climate Change Hits Home

Knowing whether a particular flood, heatwave or drought is linked to climate change is more than a scientific curiosity. It is how climate change stops being a distant, abstract threat and starts to show up in insurance costs, legal liability, and the way cities and regions plan ahead. This page covers the science of extreme event attribution, what the IPCC's AR6 report says about it, and how local climate impacts spread into disasters, food security, migration and infrastructure, followed by several regional case studies.

## The Science of Extreme Event Attribution

Climate scientists cannot say for certain that a single event was "caused" by climate change. What they can do is model the event twice, once in a world with climate change and once in a world without it, and compare the two. Dr Friederike Otto (Imperial College London) helped pioneer this field through [World Weather Attribution](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/about/). She led a study on the July 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome, which broke temperature records in the US and Canada by as much as 5°C. It found climate change made the event [at least 150 times more likely and 2°C more intense](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/without-human-caused-climate-change-temperatures-of-40c-in-the-uk-would-have-been-extremely-unlikely/).

The method has real limits. Otto and colleagues have warned that "all models systematically underestimate the observed trends," so attribution results are almost certainly on the cautious side. Extreme events are rare in any one place by definition, which makes it hard to pick out a climate signal from the noise of natural variation. The many causes behind a single event are also hard to simulate precisely. Some models are too "Gaussian": their statistical spread looks too much like a neat, symmetrical bell curve, so they understate how often real extremes happen. The biggest gap is that [attribution research is heavily biased towards events affecting the Global North](http://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/bams/101/10/bamsD190317.xml), partly because the Global South has fewer historical records and less climate modelling capacity to draw on.

## What AR6 Says

The IPCC's Working Group I contribution to AR6 (August 2021) calls it an "established fact" that human greenhouse gas emissions have made some weather and climate extremes more frequent, more intense, or both, since pre-industrial times. Confidence varies a lot by hazard. The IPCC is fairly confident that European heatwaves have become more likely, but much less confident about a link between climate change and Atlantic hurricanes. The research is also "geographically uneven". A handful of well-studied events (UK extreme rainfall, Australian heatwaves, Hurricane Harvey in Texas) dominate the literature, while many very damaging events in the developing world have never been studied, because of gaps in observations, reliable climate models and scientific capacity. [Carbon Brief keeps a map](https://www.carbonbrief.org/mapped-how-climate-change-affects-extreme-weather-around-the-world/) of which events have been researched.

This has practical consequences. In the US alone, the [frequency of disasters costing over $1 billion is rising](https://www.ncdc.noaa.gov/billions/). Worldwide, 2021 saw 47 separate weather disasters costing a billion dollars or more each, totalling $343 billion in damages ([Yale Climate Connections](https://yaleclimateconnections.org/2022/01/third-costliest-year-on-record-for-weather-disasters-in-2021-343-billion-in-damages/)). That is one reason insurers follow attribution science closely. It matters for climate justice too. Researchers who compared Hurricane Harvey's climate-attributed flood damage with census data on income and background [found in Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-31056-2) that up to half of the buildings flooded would have stayed dry without human-caused climate change. The damage fell disproportionately on poorer, largely Latinx neighbourhoods.

Several tools let anyone look at local climate risk for themselves: the [Climate Impacts Explorer](http://climate-impact-explorer.climateanalytics.org/), the [IPCC's Interactive Atlas](https://interactive-atlas.ipcc.ch/), [Climate Central's coastal flood risk map](https://coastal.climatecentral.org/), the [World Bank's Climate Change Knowledge Portal](https://climateknowledgeportal.worldbank.org/), [Probable Futures](https://probablefutures.org/), and [Cloud to Street](https://www.cloudtostreet.ai/) for flood tracking.

## Disasters Are More Than Weather

A disaster is the combination of three things: a weather event, a population that is vulnerable to it, and how exposed that population is. This matters because, even as extreme weather has increased, disaster deaths worldwide have gone down overall. Vulnerability and exposure shape the outcome as much as the weather does. Blaming every disaster on climate change alone has a real downside. [Researchers have found](https://wires.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/wcc.750) that treating the weather as the only cause can hide the existing weaknesses and inequalities that made a disaster so damaging, and can let governments off the hook for underinvesting in flood or drought prevention.

The 2022 Pakistan floods show this clearly. World Weather Attribution found climate change's fingerprint on the extreme monsoon rain that affected over 33 million people. Otto's team was just as clear that the scale of the devastation also came from homes and infrastructure built close to floodplains, poor infrastructure, an outdated river management system, high poverty, and ongoing political and economic instability.

This links to an idea called **non-linearity**. As climate scientist Andrew Dessler [has put it](https://www.theclimatebrink.com/p/why-are-climate-impacts-escalating), climate impacts do not grow in a straight line. Conditions stay bearable until, quite suddenly, they are not.

## Ripple Effects: Food, Migration and Infrastructure

Disasters that pile on top of each other are becoming normal. In May 2022, Jacobabad in Pakistan hit 50°C, and not long afterwards floods left parts of the city underwater, a sequence one headline called ["from furnace to flood."](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/furnace-flood-worlds-hottest-city-pakistan-now-under-water-2022-08-31/) Emergency services, from fire brigades in California and Australia to flood response teams in India, are more and more stretched by overlapping and back-to-back disasters, in many places around the world at the same time.

**Food security** is a major knock-on concern. [Research published in Nature Climate Change](http://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-021-01000-1) found that climate change has already cut global agricultural productivity by about 21% since 1961, the same as losing seven years of productivity growth. Food prices hit an all-time high during the first eight months of 2022, and a UN report put a record 49 million people across 46 countries at risk of famine or famine-like conditions that year. The picture is mixed rather than uniformly bad. NASA research projects maize yields falling over the next decade, while wheat yields could rise by around 17% as warming lets wheat grow further north. Food security is also a fairness issue. The calories available to people already vary hugely between regions, and climate change could widen that gap, especially where disruption to the monsoon could affect food production for over a billion people across South Asia. Land use matters a great deal as well: one estimate found that replacing all animal-based food in the US with plant-based alternatives could free up enough land to feed 350 million more people from the same area.

**Migration** patterns are already shifting in response to climate-driven disasters. ProPublica's Abrahm Lustgarten has documented this in detail across the US, Russia and the wider world, and it could reshape where people live in unexpected ways.

**Critical infrastructure** is at risk almost everywhere. McKinsey's assessment is blunt: climate risk to infrastructure, from power grids and water treatment to transport and health systems, is both widespread and varied. The flooding of Memorial Medical Center in New Orleans during Hurricane Katrina is still a stark reminder of what happens when vital infrastructure fails in a climate-driven disaster.

## Case Studies: Heat, Drought and Water Stress

### When Heat Passes the Survivability Threshold

As the world warms past 1.5-2°C, large parts of it will start to see heatwaves so extreme that even healthy young people could die within hours if they cannot find relief. The key idea here is **wet-bulb temperature**, which combines heat and humidity to measure how well the body can cool itself by sweating ([Wet-Bulb Explained](Wet-Bulb%20explained.md) covers the physiology). In theory, human survival breaks down at a wet-bulb temperature of around 35°C. Today that is rarely passed anywhere on Earth, and modelling suggests it would take more than 7°C of global warming, which is considered highly unlikely, for large areas to cross it regularly. That is an upper limit, though. Some research puts the practical danger point nearer a 31°C wet-bulb reading, well below the theoretical 35°C, and some tropical regions could approach even that lower threshold fairly soon.

The toll is already real. An estimated 62,000 heat-related deaths occurred across Europe in the summer of 2022 alone, most of them among people over 65 with existing health conditions. Using the more cautious 31°C threshold, researchers estimate that at 1°C of warming (already passed), only about 3% of European weather stations are likely to exceed it more than once per century. That rises to 25% at 2°C of warming. In the US, the equivalent figures are around 20% at 1°C, rising to 28% at 2°C.

### India: Already Crossing the Line

Delhi has broken heatwave records, reaching 49.9°C, and there is growing concern that conditions are nearing the limit of what the human body can bear. With 2°C of warming, Delhi could face 31 to 90 days a year above 45°C. Taking heat and humidity together, large parts of India, Pakistan and Nepal already have several days a year when wet-bulb temperatures come close to the 31-35°C danger zone.

### Sicily and Southern Italy: Crops or Tourism

Sicily and southern Italy are going through some of their worst droughts in decades, made worse by higher temperatures cutting already scarce rainfall. Faced with a hard choice between farming and tourism, the authorities have put tourism first. As Salvatore Cocina, head of Sicily's civil protection agency, put it: "we are forced to sacrifice the damage to agriculture, but we have to try not to damage tourism because it would be even worse." Water has been rationed to essential services, and some small hotels have had to take rooms off the market. There is a further twist: media coverage of the shortages risks scaring tourists away, adding to the economic damage the restrictions were meant to limit. Sardinia faces a similar situation. The Italian government has pledged around €12 billion (roughly $13 billion) for water infrastructure, though experts doubt it will arrive quickly.

### Catalunya: An Aggressive Response to Water Scarcity

In Catalunya, 80% of the region's roughly 6 million residents depend on the Ter-Llobregat river basin, and the region has brought in some of the toughest water restrictions in the area. Reservoir levels fell below 16% in February before recovering to around 25% by May after heavy rain. Back in 2008, the authorities had to spend €500 million shipping drinking water to Barcelona by boat. Current rules cap daily use at 230 litres per person, with a further cut to 160 litres possible if things get worse. Industrial water use has been cut by 25%, cattle farming by 50% and agriculture by 80%, and wine production is expected to fall by around 30% as a result. Guests in luxury hotels in the region use roughly five times as much water as an average resident, which campaigners have called "the injustice in the use of water."

The region's response has five strands: restoring water basins to stop seawater pushing in as river flows drop, cutting water use sector by sector (including limits on filling pools, spas and golf courses), expanding desalination (already around 23% of supply), a major push to fix leaking pipes, and reusing more wastewater, with Israel's reuse rate of roughly 70% held up as the benchmark.
