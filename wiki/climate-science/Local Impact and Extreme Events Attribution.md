# Local Impact and Extreme Events Attribution: When Climate Change Hits Home

Understanding whether a specific flood, heatwave or drought is linked to climate change matters well beyond scientific curiosity. It's how climate change goes from a distant, abstract threat to something that visibly affects insurance costs, legal liability, and how cities and regions plan for the future. This page covers the science of extreme event attribution, what the IPCC's AR6 report says about it, and how local climate impacts ripple out into disasters, food security, migration and infrastructure, with several detailed regional case studies.

## The Science of Extreme Event Attribution

Climate scientists cannot say with certainty that a single event was "caused" by climate change. What they can do is model a version of the event with climate change and a version without it, then compare the two. Dr Friederike Otto (Imperial College London), a pioneer of this field through [World Weather Attribution](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/about/), led a study finding that the July 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome, which shattered temperature records in the US and Canada by as much as 5°C, was made [at least 150 times more likely and 2°C more intense](https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/without-human-caused-climate-change-temperatures-of-40c-in-the-uk-would-have-been-extremely-unlikely/) by climate change.

The method has real limitations. Otto and colleagues have cautioned that "all models systematically underestimate the observed trends," making attribution results almost certainly too conservative. Extreme events are, by definition, rare at any given location, which makes it hard to separate a climate signal from the noise of natural variability, and the complex causes behind any one event are difficult to simulate precisely. Some models are also too "Gaussian," meaning their statistical spread resembles a symmetrical bell curve too closely and understates how often real-world extremes occur. Perhaps most importantly, [attribution research is heavily biased toward events affecting the Global North](http://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/bams/101/10/bamsD190317.xml), partly because the Global South has fewer historical observations and less climate modelling capacity to draw on.

## What AR6 Says

The IPCC's Working Group I contribution to AR6 (August 2021) calls it an "established fact" that human greenhouse gas emissions have led to increased frequency and/or intensity of some weather and climate extremes since pre-industrial times. Confidence varies sharply by hazard: the IPCC has relatively high confidence in an increased likelihood of European heatwaves, but considerably less confidence linking climate change to Atlantic hurricanes. The underlying research is also "geographically uneven": a handful of well-studied events (UK extreme rainfall, Australian heatwaves, Hurricane Harvey in Texas) dominate the literature, while many highly damaging events in the developing world remain unstudied, due to gaps in observational data, reliable climate models and scientific capacity ([Carbon Brief maintains a map](https://www.carbonbrief.org/mapped-how-climate-change-affects-extreme-weather-around-the-world/) of which events have been researched).

This has real practical stakes. In the US alone, the [frequency of disasters costing over $1 billion is rising](https://www.ncdc.noaa.gov/billions/); globally, 2021 saw 47 separate billion-dollar-plus weather disasters, totalling $343 billion in damages ([Yale Climate Connections](https://yaleclimateconnections.org/2022/01/third-costliest-year-on-record-for-weather-disasters-in-2021-343-billion-in-damages/)), which is one reason insurers are paying close attention to attribution science. It also matters for climate justice: researchers who mapped Hurricane Harvey's climate-attributed flood damage against socioeconomic census data [found in Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-31056-2) that up to half of the buildings flooded would have stayed dry without anthropogenic climate change, with the impact falling disproportionately on poorer, largely Latinx neighbourhoods.

A handful of tools let anyone explore local climate risk directly: the [Climate Impacts Explorer](http://climate-impact-explorer.climateanalytics.org/), the [IPCC's Interactive Atlas](https://interactive-atlas.ipcc.ch/), [Climate Central's coastal flood risk map](https://coastal.climatecentral.org/), the [World Bank's Climate Change Knowledge Portal](https://climateknowledgeportal.worldbank.org/), [Probable Futures](https://probablefutures.org/), and [Cloud to Street](https://www.cloudtostreet.ai/) for flood tracking.

## Disasters Are More Than Weather

A disaster isn't just a weather event, it's the combination of a weather event, a vulnerable population, and a level of exposure. That framing matters because, even as extreme weather events have increased, overall disaster deaths have declined globally, underscoring how much underlying vulnerability and exposure shape outcomes. There's a real downside to blaming every disaster on climate change alone: [researchers have found](https://wires.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/wcc.750) that framing weather as the sole cause of disasters can obscure the pre-existing fragilities and inequalities that made a disaster so damaging in the first place, and let governments off the hook for inadequate investment in flood or drought prevention.

The 2022 Pakistan floods illustrate this well. World Weather Attribution traced climate change's fingerprint on the extreme monsoon rainfall that affected over 33 million people, but Otto's team was equally clear that the scale of devastation also reflected the proximity of homes and infrastructure to floodplains, inadequate infrastructure, an outdated river management system, high poverty rates, and ongoing political and economic instability.

This connects to a key concept: **non-linearity**. As climate scientist Andrew Dessler [has put it](https://www.theclimatebrink.com/p/why-are-climate-impacts-escalating), climate impacts don't scale in a straight line; the correct way to understand climate change is that conditions stay tolerable until, quite suddenly, they aren't.

## Ripple Effects: Food, Migration and Infrastructure

Compounding disasters are becoming the norm rather than the exception. In May 2022, Jacobabad, Pakistan hit 50°C, not long before devastating floods left parts of the city underwater, a sequence one headline called ["from furnace to flood."](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/furnace-flood-worlds-hottest-city-pakistan-now-under-water-2022-08-31/) Disaster response services, from Californian and Australian fire departments to Indian flood response teams, are increasingly stretched by overlapping and back-to-back disasters, not just in one place but around the world simultaneously.

**Food security** is a major downstream concern. [Research published in Nature Climate Change](http://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-021-01000-1) found climate change has already reduced global agricultural productivity by about 21% since 1961, equivalent to losing seven years of productivity growth. Food prices hit an all-time high during the first eight months of 2022, and a UN report put a record 49 million people across 46 countries at risk of famine or famine-like conditions that year. The picture isn't uniformly bad, though: NASA research projects maize yields declining over the next decade while wheat yields could grow around 17% as climate change extends wheat's viable growing range further north. Food security is also an equity issue: calorie availability already varies hugely between regions, and climate change threatens to worsen this, particularly where monsoon disruption could affect food production for over a billion people across South Asia. Land-use choices matter enormously too; one estimate found that replacing all animal-based food in the US with plant-based alternatives could free up enough land to feed 350 million additional people from the same footprint.

**Migration** patterns are already shifting in response to climate-fuelled disasters, a trend extensively documented by ProPublica's Abrahm Lustgarten across the US, Russia and globally, with the potential to reshape settlement patterns in unexpected ways.

**Critical infrastructure** faces pervasive risk. McKinsey's assessment is blunt: climate risk to infrastructure, from power grids and water treatment to transport and healthcare systems, is both pervasive and diverse. Hurricane Katrina's flooding of Memorial Medical Center in New Orleans remains a stark historical illustration of what can go wrong when critical infrastructure fails during a climate-fuelled disaster.

## Case Studies: Heat, Drought and Water Stress

### When Heat Passes the Survivability Threshold

As the world warms past 1.5-2°C, large parts of it will start to see heatwaves so extreme that even healthy young people could die within hours without finding relief. The key physical concept here is **wet-bulb temperature**, which factors in humidity alongside heat to measure how effectively the human body can cool itself by sweating (see [Wet-Bulb Explained](Wet-Bulb%20explained.md) for the full physiology). Theoretical human survivability breaks down around a wet-bulb temperature of 35°C; today, that threshold is rarely exceeded anywhere on Earth, and modelling suggests it would take more than 7°C of global warming, considered highly unlikely, for large areas to cross it regularly. But this is an upper bound: some research puts practical danger closer to a 31°C wet-bulb reading, well before the theoretical 35°C limit, and some tropical regions could approach even that lower threshold within a relatively short time.

The toll is already real: an estimated 62,000 heat-related deaths occurred across Europe in the summer of 2022 alone, the large majority among people over 65 with pre-existing health conditions. Using the more conservative 31°C threshold, researchers estimate that at 1°C of warming (already passed), only about 3% of European weather stations are likely to exceed it more than once per century, rising to 25% at 2°C of warming; in the US, the equivalent figures are around 20% at 1°C rising to 28% at 2°C.

### India: Already Crossing the Line

Delhi has broken heatwave records, reaching 49.9°C, with growing concern that conditions are approaching the edge of what the human body can tolerate. Under a 2°C warming scenario, Delhi could face 31 to 90 days a year exceeding 45°C. Looking at combined heat and humidity, large parts of India, Pakistan and Nepal already face several days a year where wet-bulb temperatures approach the 31-35°C danger zone.

### Sicily and Southern Italy: Crops or Tourism

Sicily and southern Italy are experiencing some of their worst droughts in decades, worsened by higher temperatures reducing already-scarce rainfall. Facing a stark choice between agriculture and tourism, authorities have chosen to prioritise tourism: "we are forced to sacrifice the damage to agriculture, but we have to try not to damage tourism because it would be even worse," said Salvatore Cocina, head of Sicily's civil protection agency. Water has been rationed to essential services, some small hotels have had to pull rooms from the market, and there's a further complication: media coverage of water shortages risks scaring tourists away, compounding the economic damage the water restrictions were meant to limit. Sardinia faces a similar dynamic. The Italian government has pledged around €12 billion (roughly $13 billion) for water infrastructure, though experts remain sceptical it will be delivered quickly.

### Catalunya: An Aggressive Response to Water Scarcity

Catalunya, where 80% of the region's roughly 6 million residents depend on the Ter-Llobregat river basin, has adopted some of the most aggressive water-restriction measures in the region. Reservoir levels fell below 16% in February before recovering to around 25% by May after heavy rain; back in 2008, authorities had to spend €500 million shipping drinking water to Barcelona by boat. Current restrictions cap daily consumption at 230 litres per person, with a further cut to 160 litres possible if conditions worsen; industrial water use has been cut 25%, cattle farming 50%, and agriculture 80%, with wine production expected to fall around 30% as a result. Luxury hotel guests in the region use roughly five times as much water as an average resident, a stark illustration of what campaigners have called "the injustice in the use of water."

The region's response spans five strands: regenerating water basins to prevent saltwater intrusion as flows drop, optimising water use sector by sector (including restrictions on filling pools, spas and golf courses), expanding desalination (already around 23% of supply), a major push to fix leaking infrastructure, and expanding wastewater reuse, with Israel's roughly 70% water-reuse rate held up as the benchmark to aim for.
