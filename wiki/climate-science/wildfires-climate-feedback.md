# Wildfires as a Climate Feedback

## What is it

Wildfires are both a result of climate change and a cause of more of it. As the climate warms, the hot, dry, windy weather that fires need becomes more frequent and more extreme. The fires then release carbon that was stored in plants and soils, adding CO₂ to the atmosphere and driving further warming. This is a **climate feedback loop**: warming causes fires, and fires cause more warming.

Two developments make this more worrying than it might first appear. Europe is the world's fastest-warming continent, warming twice as fast as the global average since 1980, and 2025 brought its worst wildfire season on record. And new research on Arctic and boreal (northern forest) fires shows that current climate models are almost certainly underestimating wildfire emissions. These fires are burning recent plant growth and also ancient carbon that has been locked in Arctic soils for hundreds to thousands of years.

## Why it matters

Climate models use wildfire emissions as one of their inputs when they project future warming. If they assume fires burn only recent plant material, they will underestimate today's emissions and how fast the carbon stores in permafrost and peatland are being destabilised. Getting this right matters a great deal for knowing how much time is left to act.

## Europe in 2025: the fastest-warming continent at its limit

Europe went through wildfires and heatwaves on a scale never seen before in 2025. A three-week heatwave reached **30°C inside the Arctic circle**, a region that would normally see between zero and two days of strong heat stress a year. The same heatwave stunted the growth of plants and animals and helped invasive species spread, packing decades of biodiversity change into a single summer.

In August, climate change made the extreme hot, dry and windy conditions behind the wildfires in Portugal and Spain at least 40 times more likely. More than 10,000 sq km burned. Fires came close to Madrid, sections of the Camino de Santiago pilgrimage route were closed, and smoke drifted as far as the UK.

Across Europe, wildfires released a record **47 million tonnes of carbon** in 2025. Spain, the UK, the Netherlands, Germany and Cyprus all broke their previous records for fire emissions. Soils were the driest in 33 years of observations, and more than a third of Europe suffered extreme agricultural drought.

The worst fires followed a sharp swing in the weather. A wet spring made vegetation grow fast, then record summer heat dried it out and left it ready to burn. Scientists warn that this pattern will become more common.

## The Arctic: burning carbon that is thousands of years old

Arctic and boreal soils are more than a forest floor. In the cold, dead plant matter builds up in the soil instead of rotting away, and over centuries and millennia it forms deep layers of **peat**. These soils have worked as a carbon sink, slowly drawing CO₂ out of the atmosphere over very long timescales.

When a wildfire sweeps across the surface, it burns the living plants and often more besides. In many places, the fast burning at the surface sets off a much slower smouldering of old organic matter deep in the soil. That releases carbon that has been locked away for hundreds to thousands of years, along with black carbon (soot).

Meri Ruppel of the Finnish Meteorological Institute studied soil cores from fire-hit Arctic and boreal regions and confirmed this in several different places:

- In Canada's Northwest Territories, fires are burning several centimetres into the soil and releasing carbon stored up to **400 years ago**.
- In Greenland, fires burn 10 cm down on average, releasing carbon that is **1,000 years old**. Some burns reach 15 cm.
- In Quebec's boreal forest, fires in some places have released carbon from **5,000 years ago**.

Current climate models do not include this release of old carbon. They assume fires burn only recent plant growth. As a result, wildfire emissions are **systematically underestimated** in the same models used to project future warming and guide climate policy.

## Black carbon: the hidden double effect

Wildfires also release **black carbon**, fine particles of soot, which warms the planet in two ways. In the air, it absorbs heat from the sun directly. When it settles on snow or ice, it darkens the surface so it reflects less sunlight (lower albedo), and the ice melts faster than it otherwise would. In high-latitude regions that are already losing ice quickly, this adds a significant extra push towards warming.

## June 2026: record heat reaches temperate Europe

The extremes of 2025 carried on into 2026. In the last week of June 2026, the UK Met Office issued red heat warnings for **three days in a row**, the first time this has ever happened in Britain. Temperatures went above 35°C, and night-time lows were high enough to count technically as "tropical." A wildfire burned across moorland in Derbyshire, which is very unusual for a temperate country in early summer. Germany and Poland were preparing for 40°C.

These events were widely reported as remarkable. In fact they are what climate science has been projecting for years. Extreme heat that used to be rare is becoming routine, and it is reaching months of the year and parts of the world that used to be considered safe.

## Summer 2026: an even worse record and the policy response

Summer 2026 broke the European record set in 2025. Up to mid-August 2026:

- **France: 122,000 hectares**, a record for the country
- **Spain: 240,000 hectares**
- **Canada: 4.1 million hectares**
- **United States: 2.9 million hectares**
- Very large fires in Russia and Brazil as well

The **rescEU system**, the EU's shared civil-protection reserve, was used during the Gironde megafire. It now pools water-bombing planes, helicopters and hundreds of expert firefighters stationed in advance. The European Commission is paying for 12 new water-bombing planes to be shared between France, Spain, Portugal, Italy, Greece and Croatia, with the first due in 2028. It has been described as *"the first permanent response to a risk that is now continent-wide."*

To prevent fires, foresters and land managers are moving towards landscapes that resist fire better:

- Mixed tree species rather than rows of a single species
- Restoring wetlands, which act as natural firebreaks and hold moisture
- Wider firebreaks
- Clearing brush around homes, still one of the most effective single measures
- Grazing as a deliberate tool: Andalusia is putting sheep, goats and cattle back onto fire-prone land to keep vegetation down and reduce the fuel available to burn

Looking further ahead, the **FAO and the UN** have floated the idea of an international fire brigade since the early 2000s. It would be able to act anywhere in the world during a major crisis, in the same way that emergency aid works after natural disasters. Scientists support it, but it has not happened. The European rescEU experience shows that permanent cross-border cooperation is possible, and the global toll of the 2026 fires may finally give the idea some weight.

## Summary findings

- Europe is warming twice as fast as the global average. 2025 was the hottest year on record in the UK, Iceland and Norway.
- European wildfires released a record 47 million tonnes of carbon in 2025, and five countries broke their all-time records for fire emissions.
- Climate change made the August 2025 wildfires in Spain and Portugal at least 40 times more likely.
- Arctic and boreal wildfires are burning carbon up to 5,000 years old that had been trapped in peat soils.
- Climate models assume only recent plant growth burns, so they underestimate real wildfire emissions.
- Black carbon from wildfires speeds up the melting of ice and snow at high latitudes.
- The seas around Europe set a new yearly surface temperature record for the fourth year in a row, and 86% of European seas went through marine heatwaves.
