# Keynote: Dr Chip Fletcher on the Greenhouse Effect and Its Consequences

Dr Chip Fletcher is a climate scientist whose work also appears in [FACTS on Everything: Under the Sky We Make](../concepts/key-ideas/FACTS%20on%20EVERYTHING%20-%20Under%20the%20Sky%20we%20Make.md). He has been recognised for his work in hazard risk management and for community service in climate science, and his [Wikipedia page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chip_Fletcher) has a full biography. This page sums up the main points of his [Terra.do keynote lecture](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kxAcm_zCon0&ab_channel=TerradoClimateSchool).

## The Greenhouse Effect

The greenhouse effect is what keeps Earth liveable. Arctic sea ice works like the planet's refrigerator: its bright surface reflects most of the sunlight that reaches it, while the darker open ocean reflects only about 6% and absorbs the rest. So as the ice melts, the Arctic takes in more heat. Dark surfaces absorb sunlight and give it off again as thermal infrared radiation, which is heat. CO2 in the atmosphere sends some of that heat back down towards the surface, warming it further.

Water vapour amplifies this. Warmer air holds more water vapour, which is itself a strong greenhouse gas. Unlike CO2, though, it cycles out of the atmosphere in about 10 days, so we cannot manage it directly the way we can manage emissions. At the same time, the cooling that clouds have usually provided may be weakening.

The pace of change has also sped up. When CO2 monitoring began, concentrations were rising by less than 1ppm (parts per million) a year. Today the rate is roughly 2ppm a year, and the concentration is around 420ppm. Of the CO2 we emit, roughly 40% will still be in the atmosphere a century from now, and around 20% will still be there in 10,000 years. Of the part that is absorbed, plants take up around 29% and the ocean dissolves around 26%.

A newer worry is that, as temperatures rise, plant respiration increases faster than photosynthesis in some ecosystems. Photosynthesis normally stores more carbon than respiration releases. If that balance shifts, some plant systems could turn from a net carbon sink into a net carbon source. It is a slow-moving but serious problem, and recent research has started to document it.

## Heat and Humidity Extremes

Parts of the central Sahara are already too hot for people to survive there for long. As little as six hours' exposure can bring heat illness and organ failure. For now this affects less than 1% of Earth's land surface. On a 3°C warming pathway, models project that roughly a fifth of Earth's land could become just as inhospitable. Even at the roughly 1°C of warming already reached, projections suggest that around a billion people could eventually be displaced by heat and its knock-on effects.

## Water and Food Impacts

Worldwide, roughly 17% more freshwater is taken out than natural systems can replace. China draws about 22% more than its natural replenishment rate, and India around 52% more. This overdraft cannot be paid back, and demand is expected to keep growing, by around 55% by 2050. Roughly a quarter of humanity already faces water shortages every year ([Ireland Water Usage and Water Management](../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Water%20Usage%20and%20Water%20Management.md) gives a country-level example).

Rising CO2 is also linked to falling nutrient levels in major cereal crops, including wheat. This side of food security gets far less attention than harvest sizes.
