# Keynote: Dr Chip Fletcher on the Greenhouse Effect and Its Consequences

Dr Chip Fletcher is a climate scientist whose work is also reflected in [FACTS on Everything: Under the Sky We Make](../concepts/key-ideas/FACTS%20on%20EVERYTHING%20-%20Under%20the%20Sky%20we%20Make.md). He has been recognised professionally for his work in hazard risk management and community service in climate science; see his [Wikipedia page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chip_Fletcher) for a full biography. This page summarises the key points from his [Terra.do keynote lecture](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kxAcm_zCon0&ab_channel=TerradoClimateSchool).

## The Greenhouse Effect

The greenhouse effect exists to keep Earth liveable. Arctic sea ice acts like a refrigerator for the planet: dark surfaces absorb sunlight and re-release it as thermal infrared radiation (heat), and CO2 in the atmosphere re-radiates some of that heat back down toward the surface, warming it further.

Water vapour is a powerful amplifying feedback in this system. Warmer air holds more water vapour, itself a potent greenhouse gas, but unlike CO2 it cycles out of the atmosphere in about 10 days, meaning it isn't something we can directly manage the way we can manage emissions. At the same time, the cooling effect that clouds have traditionally provided may be weakening.

The pace of change has itself accelerated: when CO2 monitoring began, concentrations were rising by less than 1ppm a year; today the rate is roughly 2ppm a year, with atmospheric concentration around 420ppm. Of the CO2 emitted, roughly 40% will still be in the atmosphere a century from now, and around 20% will still be there in 10,000 years. Of what's absorbed, plants take up around 29% and the ocean dissolves around 26%.

One emerging concern: as temperatures rise, plant respiration increases faster than photosynthesis in some ecosystems. Since photosynthesis normally stores more carbon than respiration releases, this shift risks flipping some plant systems from being a net carbon sink into a net carbon source, a slow-moving but serious problem that recent research has begun to document.

## Heat and Humidity Extremes

Parts of the central Sahara are already too hot for sustained human survival, with as little as six hours' exposure risking heat illness and organ failure, though this currently affects less than 1% of Earth's land surface. On a 3°C warming pathway, models project that roughly a fifth of Earth's land surface could become similarly inhospitable. Even at the roughly 1°C of warming already reached, projections suggest on the order of a billion people could ultimately be displaced by heat and its downstream effects.

## Water and Food Impacts

Globally, roughly 17% more freshwater is being extracted than natural systems can regenerate; China draws about 22% more than its natural replenishment rate, and India around 52% more. This overdraft cannot be restored, and demand is projected to keep growing, by around 55% by 2050. Roughly a quarter of humanity already faces water shortages every year (see [Ireland Water Usage and Water Management](../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Water%20Usage%20and%20Water%20Management.md) for a country-level example).

Rising CO2 is also linked to falling nutrient density in major cereal crops, wheat among them, an under-recognised dimension of food security that gets far less attention than yield figures alone.
