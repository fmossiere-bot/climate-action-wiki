# Energy 101: Technical and Quantitative Basics

A few basic energy ideas go a long way: energy versus power, efficiency, load curves, and how the cost of electricity is worked out. With these in hand, it is much easier to judge claims about energy technologies instead of taking the big numbers on trust.

## Energy vs Power

**Energy** is the capacity to do work. It is measured in joules, kilowatt-hours (kWh), Btu or calories.

**Power** is the rate at which energy is used or produced, in other words energy over time, and it is measured in watts, horsepower or Btu per hour. The two are linked by a simple formula: Power (W) = Energy (Wh) / Time (h). A 7W light bulb left on for 4 hours uses 7 x 4 = 28Wh of energy.

Energy analysis also leans on a few related ideas: the energy content of fuels (how much energy a given mass or volume of fuel holds), the carbon intensity of fuels (how much CO2 is released for each unit of energy produced), and a lot of unit conversion between these measures.

## Efficiency and Capacity Factor

**Efficiency** is what a system gets out compared with what goes in. A typical coal power plant, for example, turns only around 36% of the energy in its fuel into usable electricity. Most of the rest is lost as waste heat.

**Capacity factor** is a related but different idea. It is the share of a power plant's theoretical maximum output that it actually produces over a given period. It reflects real-world downtime and maintenance and, for wind and solar especially, how much the wind blows and the sun shines. A plant can have a high rated capacity but a much lower capacity factor once weather and daylight hours are taken into account.

## Load Curves and the "Duck Curve"

A **load curve** plots actual electricity demand in time order, hour by hour, across a day or a season. Adding it up gives the total energy demand for that period. Load curves vary by country and by season.

A **load duration curve (LDC)** uses the same demand data but sorts it from highest to lowest instead of in time order. This helps planners work out how much capacity is needed for peak demand compared with typical demand.

Solar power has visibly changed the load curve on grids with a lot of solar. When solar output peaks around midday, it lowers the demand left for the rest of the system. That demand then climbs again as the sun sets and solar output falls away, just as evening demand rises. The resulting shape, a dip followed by a steep evening climb, is known in the power sector as the **"duck curve."**

Planning a grid with a lot of variable renewable power needs a newer kind of capacity planning model. These models now underpin well over a thousand published studies showing that grids can run reliably and affordably on very high shares of renewable energy. Some of the most influential regional studies are:

- **Europe:** SolarPower Europe and LUT University's [100% Renewable Europe study](https://www.solarpowereurope.org/insights/market-outlooks/100-renewable-europe-study)
- **USA:** Columbia University's [Electrification on the Path to Net Zero](https://www.energypolicy.columbia.edu/research/report/electrification-path-net-zero-comparison-studies-examining-opportunities-and-barriers-united-states), and UC Berkeley's [2035 Report](https://www.2035report.com/)
- **Philippines:** a [2021 study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1364032121002264) finding a 100% renewable energy system achievable by 2050 at a cost comparable to the 2015 system
- **India:** a [2021 report](https://www.wartsila.com/docs/default-source/power-plants-documents/downloads/white-papers/asia-australia-middle-east/a-100-renewable-power-system-across-india-by-2050-whitepaper.pdf) finding India could reach net-zero electricity by 2050 while halving overall electricity costs
- **Australia:** a 2021 [Rewiring Australia study](https://www.rewiringaustralia.org/castles-and-cars) by Saul Griffith arguing that the country's exceptional solar and wind resources make it "the luckiest country" for electrification

## The Cost of Producing Electricity

Building and running any power station involves five separate kinds of cost:

1. **Capital costs**: the fixed cost of building the facility, including land, materials, labour, and regulatory costs such as planning permits and environmental approvals
2. **Fixed operation and maintenance costs**: costs of keeping a plant running whatever its output, such as staff who must always be on site and routine maintenance
3. **Variable operation and maintenance costs**: costs that rise and fall with actual output, including extra labour, maintenance, wastewater management, chemicals and spare parts, but not fuel
4. **Fuel costs**: the cost of the fuel itself, which rises with output for conventional fossil fuel generators
5. **Transmission costs**: these depend heavily on the situation. They include grid upgrades a new facility might need, and the extra complexity of power flowing both ways as rooftop solar feeds electricity back into a grid originally built to send power in one direction only

Comparing five cost categories across different technologies is awkward, so the industry rolls them into one measure: the **levelized cost of energy**, or levelized cost of electricity (LCOE). It is what a facility spends to generate one unit of electricity, averaged over its whole lifetime. When the media quote electricity costs in $/kWh or similar, they are almost always quoting LCOE.

Without subsidies, wind, solar PV and geothermal now compete with fossil fuel power and are often cheaper. Solar paired with storage is cheaper than most coal, gas peaking plants (gas plants that run only at times of high demand) and nuclear power. It is also very competitive with combined-cycle gas plants, even before any subsidies or tax breaks are counted.
