# Global Warming Potential Explained: Why Methane Punches Above Its Weight

**Global Warming Potential (GWP)** brings together two properties of a greenhouse gas: how long it lasts in the atmosphere (its atmospheric lifetime), and how well it absorbs and re-emits infrared radiation (its radiative efficiency). See [Emissions and the Global Carbon Cycle](Emissions%20and%20the%20Global%20Carbon%20Cycle.md) for how GWP is used in practice to compare gases through CO2-equivalent.

## Why Wavelength and Concentration Both Matter

Earth gives off heat as infrared radiation, and different gases absorb different wavelengths of it. Water vapour absorbs a large share. But humans can do little to control the amount of water vapour in the air directly: it cycles out within days, even though a warmer climate does hold more of it through feedback. So climate policy focuses on the gases we can control.

Concentration matters as much as how strongly a gas absorbs. Tonne for tonne, methane traps around 80 times as much heat as CO2 over 20 years and around 30 times over 100 years (its global warming potential). Part of the reason it is so strong is that there is still comparatively little methane in the atmosphere, and adding more of a scarce gas has a proportionally bigger effect. CO2, by comparison, is already so concentrated that its main absorption bands are close to **saturated**. Each extra tonne of CO2 therefore has a slightly smaller effect on warming than the tonne before it, even though CO2's total effect is still far larger because there is so much more of it.

## Interactive Models Worth Trying

The University of Chicago hosts two teaching models that run in your browser and make these ideas easier to see:

- **[MODTRAN](https://climatemodels.uchicago.edu/modtran/)** simulates how infrared radiation is given off and absorbed as it passes through the atmosphere. It plots the theoretical heat spectrum of an ideal body (a "blackbody") at different temperatures next to the actual, jagged spectrum of infrared light leaving the top of the atmosphere. The total energy flow is reported as the Upward IR Heat Flux (in W/m²). Changing the gas concentrations in the model shows directly how greenhouse gases, each absorbing particular wavelengths, reshape the infrared that Earth sends out to space.
- **[Slugulator](https://climatemodels.uchicago.edu/slugulator/)** simulates how the climate responds to a sudden ("spike") release of CO2 or methane. Per gigatonne of carbon released, methane's warming effect is much stronger than CO2's at first, again because CO2's absorption bands are closer to saturated while methane's are not. The two gases also last very different lengths of time. Methane breaks down, mostly into CO2 (which the model accounts for), over roughly a decade. CO2 is removed far more slowly, by dissolving into the ocean and, eventually, through chemical weathering of rocks over much longer timescales. The model shows the temperature response using two heat stores. A shallow ocean layer adjusts to the new warming within about a decade. A deep ocean exchanges heat with it far more slowly, which sets the pace at which the whole planet's temperature catches up, and it builds up the long-term "heat pollution" that remains even after concentrations in the atmosphere have levelled off.
