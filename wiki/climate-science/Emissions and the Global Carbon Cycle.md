# Emissions and the Global Carbon Cycle

How much should we worry about methane compared to CO2 or other greenhouse gases? Is leaky natural gas still cleaner than coal? What does the global carbon cycle tell us about both the causes of climate change and the solutions to it? These questions matter because cutting greenhouse gas emissions quickly and at scale is the core lever for stopping further warming, and there's a lot of confusing or outright wrong information circulating about how to compare and combine different types of emissions.

## Comparing Greenhouse Gases

**Carbon dioxide** behaves differently once it's emitted than most people assume. When you drive a car, more than half of the CO2 from the tailpipe is absorbed by carbon sinks, plants, trees, and the ocean, within about 100 years. The rest stays in the atmosphere for [300 to 1,000+ years](https://climate.nasa.gov/news/2915/the-atmosphere-getting-a-handle-on-carbon-dioxide/), meaning CO2 emitted today keeps affecting the climate for a very long time.

**Not all greenhouse gases persist the same way.** Methane (CH4), by contrast, stays in the atmosphere for an average of just 12 years before being chemically broken down. But it makes up for its short life with potency: while present, methane traps roughly 83 times as much heat as an equivalent mass of CO2 (its "radiative efficiency"). Every greenhouse gas has its own radiative efficiency and atmospheric lifetime.

### CO2-Equivalent: A Common Currency for Greenhouse Gases

Beyond CO2, human activity emits methane (CH4), nitrous oxide (N2O), hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), perfluorocarbons (PFCs), nitrogen trifluoride (NF3) and sulfur hexafluoride (SF6), most of which trap far more heat per kilogram than CO2. To compare them on a common scale, scientists and policymakers use **Global Warming Potential (GWP)**, which expresses each gas's heat-trapping effect in terms of an equivalent mass of CO2, denoted CO2e.

GWP is defined over different time horizons to account for how long each gas actually persists in the atmosphere, most commonly 20, 100 and 500 years. Carbon offset credits are conventionally denominated using 100-year GWPs (GWP-100), the most widely used metric for policy analysis. Because methane is short-lived but potent, its GWP-100 is actually lower than its GWP-20, capturing what climate scientist Zeke Hausfather has summarised as "methane is temporary, while CO2 is forever."

A few specific gases are worth knowing: **halocarbons** like HFC-134a and CFC-11, chiefly used as refrigerants, are emitted in much smaller quantities than CO2 or methane but are extremely potent. Project Drawdown ranks improved refrigerant management among the most impactful climate solutions available, and the international **Kigali Amendment** (155 countries as of writing) is a landmark agreement to phase these gases out. See [Global Warming Potential explained](Global%20Warming%20Potential%20explained.md) for a deeper look at how GWP is calculated and its uncertainties.

### The Methane Question

Fossil fuel operations are among the largest sources of human-caused methane emissions, alongside agriculture. The [IEA estimates](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-methane-tracker-2023) the energy sector accounts for 40% of anthropogenic methane, and that [real emissions run about 70% higher](https://www.iea.org/news/methane-emissions-from-the-energy-sector-are-70-higher-than-official-figures) than governments officially report. Even household gas stoves leak methane when switched off. Detection technology, including infrared cameras and satellite operators like Kairos Aerospace, is increasingly able to pinpoint these leaks, and the IEA has published a [guide to curtailing fossil fuel methane emissions](https://www.iea.org/reports/curtailing-methane-emissions-from-fossil-fuel-operations).

Atmospheric methane concentrations are at record highs, and it isn't only a direct-emissions story: rising temperatures appear to be accelerating methane production from natural sources too, for instance by speeding up microbial activity in wetlands, while more frequent wildfires slow the atmospheric chemistry that normally removes methane. A [2022 study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-31345-w.pdf) found this feedback effect large enough that one author called the results "shocking" [in the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2022/jul/05/global-heating-causes-methane-growth-four-times-faster-than-thought-study). The practical takeaway most climate scientists converge on: cut methane emissions quickly, because it is one of the fastest ways to slow near-term warming, but not at the expense of also cutting CO2, since CO2's effects last far longer.

### Any Amount of CO2 Still Adds Warming

Global annual CO2 emissions have been roughly flat for the last decade, according to [Carbon Brief analysis](https://www.carbonbrief.org/global-co2-emissions-have-been-flat-for-a-decade-new-data-reveals). That sounds like good news, but flat, or even declining, emissions still add to warming, just more slowly, because CO2 persists in the atmosphere for so long that every additional molecule contributes further heating. What matters is the *concentration* of CO2 built up in the atmosphere over time, not just the year-on-year emissions rate. The only way to actually stop the warming trend is to reach **net-zero emissions**: cutting emissions as close to zero as possible and re-absorbing whatever remains.

## The Global Carbon Cycle

Carbon exists in **stocks**, stored in the air, land and water for varying lengths of time, and moves between them through **flows**. A stock that emits carbon into the atmosphere is a "source"; one that removes it is a "sink." Roughly 55% of the carbon emitted each year is absorbed by natural sinks, split roughly between land vegetation (around 30%) and the ocean (around a quarter), leaving about 44% to accumulate in the atmosphere, adding more than 5 billion tonnes of carbon a year on average over the last decade ([Global Carbon Budget, ESSD 2022](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/14/4811/2022/)). The rate at which land and ocean sinks absorb carbon has actually increased over time as atmospheric carbon has grown.

It's worth keeping units straight: carbon (the element, measured in gigatonnes of carbon, GtC) is distinct from carbon dioxide (the emitted gas, typically measured in gigatonnes of CO2 per year) and from atmospheric CO2 *concentration* (measured in parts per million, currently around 424ppm; see [What is Climate Change](What%20is%20Climate%20Change.md) for how that figure has moved over time).

### Carbon Sinks Are Becoming Less Reliable

Several of the natural systems the planet relies on to absorb carbon are showing signs of strain:

- **Wildfires** release carbon that trees had been storing, converting what was a sink back into a source
- There is evidence the **Amazon rainforest** may already be [emitting more CO2 than it absorbs](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2021/jul/14/amazon-rainforest-now-emitting-more-co2-than-it-absorbs)
- California's 2020 wildfire season released enough carbon that a [2022 CarbonPlan analysis](https://carbonplan.org/research/fire-forests-inventories) concluded the state's forests were likely to be a net emissions source rather than a sink, cancelling out years of the state's emissions-reduction efforts
- **Ocean acidification**: as the ocean absorbs more carbon it becomes more acidic, already affecting coral reefs and marine food chains, with further feedbacks that are [hard to predict](https://www.pmel.noaa.gov/co2/story/Ocean+Carbon+Uptake)
- **Permafrost thaw**: frozen polar soils could warm and release large stores of methane currently locked away

## Tracking Emissions in Real Time

Two tools are useful for anyone who wants to see this data directly rather than take it on trust. [Climate TRACE](https://climatetrace.org/map) uses satellite imagery and independent monitoring to track greenhouse gas emissions facility-by-facility worldwide, offering a check on self-reported national inventories. A separate visualisation, the ["climate spiral"](https://openclimatedata.net/climate-spirals/from-emissions-to-global-warming-line-chart/) style chart popularised by climate scientist Ed Hawkins, traces the link between rising emissions, rising atmospheric concentration, and rising temperature over time.
