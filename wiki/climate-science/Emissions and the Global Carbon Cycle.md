# Emissions and the Global Carbon Cycle

How much should we worry about methane compared with CO2 or other greenhouse gases? Is leaky natural gas still cleaner than coal? And what does the global carbon cycle tell us about the causes of climate change and the ways out of it? These questions matter because cutting greenhouse gas emissions quickly and at scale is the main way to stop further warming. There is also a lot of confusing or plainly wrong information around about how to compare and add up different kinds of emissions.

## Comparing Greenhouse Gases

**Carbon dioxide** behaves differently once it is emitted than most people assume. When you drive a car, more than half of the CO2 from the exhaust is absorbed within about 100 years by carbon sinks: plants, trees and the ocean. The rest stays in the atmosphere for [300 to 1,000+ years](https://climate.nasa.gov/news/2915/the-atmosphere-getting-a-handle-on-carbon-dioxide/), so CO2 emitted today keeps affecting the climate for a very long time.

Other greenhouse gases last for different lengths of time. **Methane** (CH4) stays in the atmosphere for an average of just 12 years before it is broken down chemically. What it lacks in lifespan it makes up for in strength: over a 20-year period, methane traps around 80 times as much heat as the same mass of CO2, and around 30 times over 100 years (its global warming potential, explained below). Every greenhouse gas has its own radiative efficiency and its own lifetime in the atmosphere.

### CO2-Equivalent: A Common Currency for Greenhouse Gases

Besides CO2, human activity emits methane (CH4), nitrous oxide (N2O), hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), perfluorocarbons (PFCs), nitrogen trifluoride (NF3) and sulphur hexafluoride (SF6). Most of these trap far more heat per kilogram than CO2. To compare them on one scale, scientists and policymakers use **Global Warming Potential (GWP)**. It expresses each gas's heat-trapping effect as the mass of CO2 that would do the same, written CO2e.

GWP is calculated over different time spans to reflect how long each gas actually lasts in the atmosphere, most often 20, 100 and 500 years. Carbon offset credits are normally counted using 100-year GWPs (GWP-100), the measure most widely used in policy. Because methane is short-lived but strong, its GWP-100 is lower than its GWP-20. The climate scientist Zeke Hausfather sums this up as "methane is temporary, while CO2 is forever."

A few specific gases are worth knowing. **Halocarbons** such as HFC-134a and CFC-11, used mainly as refrigerants, are released in much smaller amounts than CO2 or methane but are extremely strong. Project Drawdown ranks better refrigerant management among the most effective climate solutions, and the international Kigali Amendment (155 countries at the time of writing) is a major agreement to phase these gases out. See [Global Warming Potential explained](Global%20Warming%20Potential%20explained.md) for more on how GWP is calculated and where the uncertainties lie.

### The Methane Question

Fossil fuel operations are among the largest sources of human-caused methane, alongside farming. The [IEA estimates](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-methane-tracker-2023) that the energy sector produces 40% of human-caused methane, and that [real emissions run about 70% higher](https://www.iea.org/news/methane-emissions-from-the-energy-sector-are-70-higher-than-official-figures) than governments officially report. Even household gas cookers leak methane when they are switched off. Detection technology, including infrared cameras and satellite operators such as Kairos Aerospace, is getting better at pinpointing these leaks, and the IEA has published a [guide to curtailing fossil fuel methane emissions](https://www.iea.org/reports/curtailing-methane-emissions-from-fossil-fuel-operations).

Methane levels in the atmosphere are at record highs, and direct emissions are only part of the reason. Rising temperatures seem to be speeding up methane production from natural sources too, for instance by making microbes in wetlands more active. More frequent wildfires also slow down the chemistry that normally removes methane from the air. A [2022 study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-31345-w.pdf) found this feedback so large that one author called the results "shocking" [in the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2022/jul/05/global-heating-causes-methane-growth-four-times-faster-than-thought-study). Most climate scientists land on the same practical advice: cut methane quickly, because it is one of the fastest ways to slow warming in the near term, but keep cutting CO2 as well, because its effects last far longer.

### Any Amount of CO2 Still Adds Warming

According to [Carbon Brief analysis](https://www.carbonbrief.org/global-co2-emissions-have-been-flat-for-a-decade-new-data-reveals), global CO2 emissions have been roughly flat for the last decade. That sounds like good news. But flat, or even falling, emissions still add to warming, only more slowly. CO2 stays in the atmosphere so long that every extra molecule adds more heat. What counts is the total *concentration* of CO2 that has built up in the atmosphere over time, more than the rate of emissions in any one year. The only way to actually stop the warming trend is to reach **net-zero emissions**: cutting emissions as close to zero as possible and taking back out whatever remains.

## The Global Carbon Cycle

Carbon sits in **stocks**, stored in the air, land and water for different lengths of time, and moves between them through **flows**. A stock that releases carbon into the atmosphere is a "source", and one that removes it is a "sink". Roughly 55% of the carbon emitted each year is absorbed by natural sinks, split between land plants (around 30%) and the ocean (around a quarter). That leaves about 44% to build up in the atmosphere, adding more than 5 billion tonnes of carbon a year on average over the last decade ([Global Carbon Budget, ESSD 2022](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/14/4811/2022/)). The rate at which land and ocean sinks absorb carbon has actually risen over time as the amount of carbon in the atmosphere has grown.

It helps to keep the units straight. Carbon, the element, is measured in gigatonnes of carbon (GtC). Carbon dioxide, the gas that is emitted, is usually measured in gigatonnes of CO2 per year. And the *concentration* of CO2 in the atmosphere is measured in parts per million, currently around 424ppm (see [What is Climate Change](What%20is%20Climate%20Change.md) for how that figure has changed over time).

### Carbon Sinks Are Becoming Less Reliable

Several of the natural systems the planet relies on to absorb carbon are showing signs of strain:

- **Wildfires** release carbon that trees had been storing, turning what was a sink back into a source
- There is evidence that the **Amazon rainforest** may already be [emitting more CO2 than it absorbs](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2021/jul/14/amazon-rainforest-now-emitting-more-co2-than-it-absorbs)
- California's 2020 wildfire season released so much carbon that a [2022 CarbonPlan analysis](https://carbonplan.org/research/fire-forests-inventories) concluded the state's forests were likely to be a net source of emissions rather than a sink, cancelling out years of the state's efforts to cut emissions
- **Ocean acidification**: as the ocean absorbs more carbon it becomes more acidic. This is already affecting coral reefs and marine food chains, with further knock-on effects that are [hard to predict](https://www.pmel.noaa.gov/co2/story/Ocean+Carbon+Uptake)
- **Permafrost thaw**: frozen polar soils could warm up and release large stores of methane that are currently locked away

## Tracking Emissions in Real Time

Two tools let you see this data for yourself instead of taking it on trust. [Climate TRACE](https://climatetrace.org/map) uses satellite images and independent monitoring to track greenhouse gas emissions site by site around the world, which provides a check on the figures countries report about themselves. The ["climate spiral"](https://openclimatedata.net/climate-spirals/from-emissions-to-global-warming-line-chart/) style of chart, made popular by the climate scientist Ed Hawkins, traces the link between rising emissions, rising concentrations in the atmosphere and rising temperatures over time.
