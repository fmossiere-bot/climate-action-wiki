# Methane's Role in Global Warming: Sources, Super-Emitters and Solutions

Methane (CH4) is a hydrocarbon and greenhouse gas, emitted both by human activity and natural sources like wetlands, that plays an outsized role in near-term warming. It's released during the production and transport of coal, natural gas and oil, from livestock and other agricultural practices, from land use change, and from the decay of organic waste in landfills.

## Why Methane Matters So Much

Methane is responsible for roughly a quarter to a third of the global heating observed since the industrial revolution, despite atmospheric concentrations being tiny: only about two in every million air molecules are methane. What it lacks in concentration it makes up for in potency: by the most commonly used metric, GWP-100, methane traps about 30 times as much heat as CO2; measured over a 20-year window it traps around 80 times more. By some other measures, methane could be responsible for as much as 50% of anthropogenic warming to date.

The good news is that methane doesn't linger. Around half of any methane released breaks down within about a decade, and almost none remains after a few decades, compared with CO2, which continues affecting the climate for centuries to millennia. That combination of short lifetime and outsized impact makes methane genuine low-hanging fruit for climate mitigation: as former US climate envoy John Kerry put it, "cutting methane is the fastest opportunity to reduce warming."

Roughly 40% of methane emissions come from natural sources, chiefly wetlands, with the remaining 60% from human activity: fossil fuel extraction, livestock farming, and landfill decay foremost among them. China, the United States, Russia, India, Brazil, Indonesia, Nigeria and Mexico together account for nearly half of all anthropogenic methane emissions, though the dominant source varies by country: coal production drives China's methane footprint, oil and gas systems drive Russia's, and in the US, oil and gas systems, livestock digestion, and landfills are the three largest sources.

## Super-Emitters: The Easy Wins

A significant share of methane emissions comes from "super-emitter" point sources, usually leaks that would be straightforward and cheap to fix, by avoiding venting (releasing methane directly rather than the less harmful practice of flaring it) or simply repairing infrastructure. Turkmenistan has become the highest-profile example: [Guardian reporting](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/may/09/mind-boggling-methane-emissions-from-turkmenistan-revealed) documented major leaks running since 2019, with the country's two main oil and gas leak sites alone emitting 4.4 million tonnes of methane in 2022, enough to cause as much warming as the entire UK's carbon emissions that year. The US, under then-climate envoy John Kerry, worked toward [a deal to help Turkmenistan plug these leaks](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2023/jun/02/us-deal-turkmenistan-colossal-methane-emissions).

The US itself may be undercounting its own leaks. The [largest aerial-survey dataset of its kind](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2422110-methane-leaks-from-us-oil-and-gas-are-triple-government-estimates/) found methane leaking from US oil and gas fields at a substantially higher rate than official government estimates, using infrared sensors to survey wells, pipelines and other infrastructure covering 52% of US onshore oil and 29% of gas production sites. The resulting estimate, 6.2 million tonnes of methane a year from the surveyed sites alone, represents roughly $1 billion in lost saleable gas and an estimated $9 billion in associated climate damages.

## The IEA's 2024 Global Methane Tracker

The [IEA's Global Methane Tracker](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-methane-tracker-2024), updated in March 2024, put methane's contribution to warming since the industrial revolution at around 30%, with the energy sector (oil, gas, coal and bioenergy combined) responsible for more than 35% of all human-caused methane emissions. In 2023, fossil fuel production and use generated close to 120 million tonnes of methane, plus a further 10 million tonnes from bioenergy (mostly traditional biomass use). Emissions have held roughly flat since a 2019 record high even as fossil fuel supply kept growing, implying a modest decline in the average methane intensity of production.

Large leak events detected by satellite rose more than 50% in 2023 compared with 2022, with over 5 million tonnes of methane detected from major fossil fuel leaks worldwide, including a single well blowout in Kazakhstan that continued for more than 200 days. Roughly 70% of fossil-fuel methane emissions come from just the top 10 emitting countries.

The IEA frames cutting fossil fuel methane emissions by 75% by 2030 as vital to keeping the 1.5°C target within reach. An estimated 80 million tonnes of annual fossil fuel methane emissions could be avoided using existing technology, often at low or even negative cost, since captured gas can be sold. Around 40% of the 120 million tonnes of fossil fuel methane emitted in 2023 could theoretically be avoided at no net cost at all, a share that's higher for oil and gas operations (50%) than coal (15%), because the market value of the captured gas exceeds the cost of the abatement measures. Full implementation of COP28 pledges and other existing commitments would cut fossil fuel methane emissions by about half, but delivering the full 75% cut requires an estimated $170 billion in spending through 2030. Companies often don't deploy these cost-effective fixes anyway, for reasons ranging from longer payback periods than other investments, to limited awareness of the scale of the opportunity, to infrastructure or institutional barriers that make it hard to capture the financial benefit.

## Measurement Is Catching Up

New satellite-based tools are closing the gap between estimated and actual methane emissions. [Kayrros](https://methanewatch.kayrros.com/map) uses satellite imagery to quantify large emissions daily and estimate country-level oil and gas methane intensity; [GHGSat](https://www.ghgsat.com/en/pulse/) has grown its satellite constellation to 12, including dedicated offshore monitoring; and the UN Environment Programme's Methane Alert and Response System (MARS) uses satellite detection to alert governments and operators to major emission events.

The gap this exposes is stark. If self-reported data submitted to UNEP's Oil & Gas Methane Partnership 2.0 were representative of the global industry, it would imply 2023 global oil and gas methane emissions of around 5 million tonnes, 95% lower than the IEA's measurement-based estimate. National reporting to the UNFCCC suggests around 40 million tonnes, still roughly 50% below the IEA's figure. Closing this gap through more systematic, transparent, measurement-based reporting is essential if methane policy is going to be built on accurate numbers.

## COP28 and the Global Methane Pledge

The **Oil and Gas Decarbonization Charter (OGDC)**, launched at COP28, is an industry initiative in which 52 companies committed to net-zero scope 1 and 2 emissions by 2050 at the latest, ending routine flaring by 2030, and achieving near-zero upstream methane emissions by 2030. Around 30 of the signatories, including many national oil companies, had not previously engaged with other methane initiatives such as the Aiming for Zero Methane Initiative or the Oil & Gas Methane Partnership 2.0.

The **Global Methane Pledge (GMP)**, under which countries commit to cutting methane emissions at least 30% below 2020 levels by 2030, gained five new members at COP28: Angola, Kazakhstan, Kenya, Romania and Turkmenistan, together responsible for close to 10% of 2023 fossil fuel methane emissions. Canada, the Federated States of Micronesia, Germany, Japan and Nigeria joined the EU and US as pledge "Champions," committing to support other members' progress alongside their own. With Azerbaijan's accession in March 2024, the GMP now covers 156 countries responsible for around 55% of global energy-sector methane emissions. If the five COP28 joiners fully captured their abatement potential, it would cut global methane emissions by an estimated 5 million tonnes by 2030.

## A Hidden Source: Methane from Water

Methane also escapes from water bodies in ways that get far less attention than fossil fuel leaks or livestock. It forms naturally wherever carbon-rich organic matter decomposes without oxygen, in tropical swamps, peat bogs and waterlogged soils, and is produced by the same process in manmade settings like wastewater treatment plants and rice paddies. Reservoirs behind dams are a particularly overlooked source, contributing an estimated 22 million tonnes of methane a year globally, a little under two-thirds of what rice cultivation or wastewater treatment each produce (roughly 35 million tonnes apiece). The mechanism is similar to carbonation escaping shaken soda: methane dissolves in large, still bodies of water, and is released when that water is churned, whether by passing through hydroelectric turbines or by agitation at treatment plants. Livestock account for roughly 32% of anthropogenic methane on their own, mostly from cattle digestion.

Start-ups are beginning to treat this as an opportunity rather than just a liability. UK-based Bluemethane, for instance, is developing technology to capture methane bubbling up from reservoirs and treatment works, turning what escapes into a usable energy source. The barrier isn't technical so much as financial and informational: despite causing roughly a third of observed global warming, methane receives only about 2% of global climate finance, and most water utilities, including in the UK where the sector has pledged operational net-zero by 2030, don't yet measure their baseline methane emissions at all.

## Permafrost: An Emerging Wildcard

Thawing permafrost is one of the climate tipping points under active scientific monitoring, carrying the risk of a self-reinforcing carbon feedback loop (see [Global Impact of Climate Change](Global%20Impact%20of%20Climate%20Change.md)). A study led by geologist Thomas Birchall at the University Center in Svalbard found natural gas deposits, richer in methane than previously assumed, trapped in pockets beneath the permafrost, formed where the undulating base of the permafrost layer creates spaces for gas to accumulate from below. Leakage today is very low, but the researchers warn that continued glacial retreat and permafrost thaw could "lift the lid" on these reserves, potentially releasing methane on the order of several million cubic feet at the single site they were able to measure. Because Svalbard shares a similar geological and glacial history with much of the wider Arctic, similar hidden reserves may exist elsewhere across the region. The risk, if the permafrost seal fails, is a vicious cycle: escaping methane accelerates warming, which thaws more permafrost, which releases more methane.
