# Methane's Role in Global Warming: Sources, Super-Emitters and Solutions

Methane (CH4) is a hydrocarbon and a greenhouse gas. It comes both from human activity and from natural sources such as wetlands, and it has an outsized effect on warming in the near term. It is released when coal, natural gas and oil are produced and transported, by livestock and other farming practices, by changes in land use, and by organic waste rotting in landfill.

## Why Methane Matters So Much

Methane is responsible for roughly a quarter to a third of the global heating seen since the industrial revolution, even though there is very little of it in the air: only about two in every million air molecules are methane. It makes up for that with strength. By the most common measure, GWP-100 (its warming effect over 100 years), methane traps about 30 times as much heat as CO2. Over a 20-year window it traps around 80 times more. By some other measures, methane could be behind as much as 50% of human-caused warming so far.

The upside is that methane does not hang around. Around half of any methane released breaks down within about a decade, and almost none is left after a few decades. CO2, by contrast, keeps affecting the climate for centuries to millennia. A short lifetime combined with a large impact makes methane some of the easiest climate action available. As former US climate envoy John Kerry put it, "cutting methane is the fastest opportunity to reduce warming."

Roughly 40% of methane emissions come from natural sources, mainly wetlands. The other 60% come from human activity, led by fossil fuel extraction, livestock farming and rotting landfill waste. China, the United States, Russia, India, Brazil, Indonesia, Nigeria and Mexico together produce nearly half of all human-caused methane emissions. The main source differs by country. In China it is coal production, in Russia it is oil and gas, and in the US the three largest sources are oil and gas systems, livestock digestion and landfill.

## Super-Emitters: The Easy Wins

A large share of methane comes from "super-emitter" sites. These are usually leaks that would be simple and cheap to fix, either by stopping venting (letting methane out directly, rather than flaring it, which is less harmful) or just by repairing equipment. Turkmenistan has become the best-known example. [Guardian reporting](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/may/09/mind-boggling-methane-emissions-from-turkmenistan-revealed) documented major leaks running since 2019. The country's two main oil and gas leak sites alone released 4.4 million tonnes of methane in 2022, enough to cause as much warming as the whole UK's carbon emissions that year. The US, under then climate envoy John Kerry, worked towards [a deal to help Turkmenistan plug these leaks](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2023/jun/02/us-deal-turkmenistan-colossal-methane-emissions).

The US may also be undercounting its own leaks. The [largest aerial-survey dataset of its kind](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2422110-methane-leaks-from-us-oil-and-gas-are-triple-government-estimates/) found methane escaping from US oil and gas fields at a much higher rate than official estimates. It used infrared sensors to survey wells, pipelines and other equipment, covering 52% of US onshore oil and 29% of gas production sites. The result, 6.2 million tonnes of methane a year from the surveyed sites alone, adds up to roughly $1 billion in gas that could have been sold and an estimated $9 billion in climate damage.

## The IEA's 2024 Global Methane Tracker

The [IEA's Global Methane Tracker](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-methane-tracker-2024), updated in March 2024, puts methane's share of warming since the industrial revolution at around 30%. The energy sector (oil, gas, coal and bioenergy combined) is responsible for more than 35% of all human-caused methane emissions. In 2023, producing and using fossil fuels released close to 120 million tonnes of methane, with a further 10 million tonnes from bioenergy (mostly traditional burning of wood and other biomass). Emissions have stayed roughly flat since a record high in 2019, even as fossil fuel supply kept growing, which suggests a small drop in the average amount of methane released per unit produced.

Large leaks spotted by satellite rose by more than 50% in 2023 compared with 2022. Over 5 million tonnes of methane were detected from major fossil fuel leaks worldwide, including a single well blowout in Kazakhstan that went on for more than 200 days. Roughly 70% of fossil fuel methane emissions come from just the top 10 emitting countries.

The IEA says cutting fossil fuel methane emissions by 75% by 2030 is vital to keeping the 1.5°C target within reach. An estimated 80 million tonnes of yearly fossil fuel methane emissions could be avoided with existing technology, often at low or even negative cost, because the captured gas can be sold. Around 40% of the 120 million tonnes of fossil fuel methane emitted in 2023 could in theory be avoided at no net cost at all. That share is higher for oil and gas (50%) than for coal (15%), because the captured gas is worth more than the measures cost. Fully delivering the COP28 pledges and other existing commitments would cut fossil fuel methane emissions by about half. Reaching the full 75% cut would need an estimated $170 billion of spending up to 2030. Companies often still do not make these cost-effective fixes. Reasons range from longer payback times than other investments, to not knowing how big the opportunity is, to practical or institutional barriers that make it hard to cash in on the savings.

## Measurement Is Catching Up

New satellite tools are narrowing the gap between estimated and actual methane emissions. [Kayrros](https://methanewatch.kayrros.com/map) uses satellite images to measure large emissions every day and to estimate how much methane each country's oil and gas industry releases per unit produced. [GHGSat](https://www.ghgsat.com/en/pulse/) has grown its fleet to 12 satellites, including dedicated offshore monitoring. The UN Environment Programme's Methane Alert and Response System (MARS) uses satellite detection to warn governments and operators about major emission events.

The gap these tools reveal is large. If the self-reported data sent to UNEP's Oil & Gas Methane Partnership 2.0 were typical of the whole industry, global oil and gas methane emissions in 2023 would have been around 5 million tonnes, 95% lower than the IEA's measurement-based estimate. National reports to the UNFCCC suggest around 40 million tonnes, still roughly 50% below the IEA's figure. If methane policy is to rest on accurate numbers, reporting needs to become more systematic, transparent and based on actual measurement.

## COP28 and the Global Methane Pledge

The **Oil and Gas Decarbonization Charter (OGDC)**, launched at COP28, is an industry initiative. In it, 52 companies committed to net-zero scope 1 and 2 emissions (those from their own operations and the energy they buy) by 2050 at the latest, to ending routine flaring by 2030, and to near-zero upstream methane emissions by 2030. Around 30 of the signatories, including many national oil companies, had not previously taken part in other methane initiatives such as the Aiming for Zero Methane Initiative or the Oil & Gas Methane Partnership 2.0.

Under the **Global Methane Pledge (GMP)**, countries commit to cutting methane emissions to at least 30% below 2020 levels by 2030. It gained five new members at COP28: Angola, Kazakhstan, Kenya, Romania and Turkmenistan, which together produced close to 10% of 2023 fossil fuel methane emissions. Canada, the Federated States of Micronesia, Germany, Japan and Nigeria joined the EU and US as pledge "Champions", committing to help other members make progress as well as making their own. With Azerbaijan joining in March 2024, the GMP now covers 156 countries responsible for around 55% of global energy-sector methane emissions. If the five COP28 newcomers made every cut available to them, global methane emissions would fall by an estimated 5 million tonnes by 2030.

## A Hidden Source: Methane from Water

Methane also escapes from water in ways that get far less attention than fossil fuel leaks or livestock. It forms naturally wherever carbon-rich organic matter rots without oxygen, in tropical swamps, peat bogs and waterlogged soils. The same process produces it in human-made settings such as wastewater treatment plants and rice paddies. Reservoirs behind dams are a particularly overlooked source, giving off an estimated 22 million tonnes of methane a year worldwide. That is a little under two-thirds of what rice growing or wastewater treatment each produce (roughly 35 million tonnes apiece). It works a bit like the fizz escaping from a shaken fizzy drink: methane dissolves in large, still bodies of water and is released when the water is churned, whether by passing through hydroelectric turbines or by being stirred at treatment plants. Livestock account for roughly 32% of human-caused methane on their own, mostly from cattle digestion.

Start-ups are starting to see this as an opportunity as well as a problem. UK-based Bluemethane, for example, is developing technology to capture methane bubbling up from reservoirs and treatment works and turn it into usable energy. The obstacle is less technical than financial and a matter of information. Methane causes roughly a third of observed global warming, yet it gets only about 2% of global climate finance. Most water companies, including in the UK where the sector has pledged net zero in its operations by 2030, do not yet measure their starting methane emissions at all.

## Permafrost: An Emerging Wildcard

Thawing permafrost is one of the climate tipping points scientists are watching closely, because it could set off a self-reinforcing carbon feedback loop ([Global Impact of Climate Change](Global%20Impact%20of%20Climate%20Change.md) has more). A study led by geologist Thomas Birchall at the University Center in Svalbard found pockets of natural gas trapped beneath the permafrost, richer in methane than previously thought. They form where the uneven base of the permafrost layer leaves spaces for gas rising from below to collect. Very little leaks today. The researchers warn, though, that continued glacier retreat and permafrost thaw could "lift the lid" on these reserves, possibly releasing methane in the order of several million cubic feet at the one site they were able to measure. Svalbard shares a similar geological and glacial history with much of the wider Arctic, so similar hidden reserves may exist across the region. If the permafrost seal fails, the risk is a vicious circle: escaping methane speeds up warming, which thaws more permafrost, which releases more methane.
