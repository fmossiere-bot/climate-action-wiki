# The Forgotten Pollutants: Indirect Greenhouse Gases

## What is it

Most climate policies focus on the three big greenhouse gases: CO₂, methane and nitrous oxide. Yet a sizeable part of the warming we have already caused comes from a different group of pollutants that hardly ever appear in emissions targets. Indirect greenhouse gases, mainly carbon monoxide (CO) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs), have caused approximately **15% of all global warming** since pre-industrial times. That is double the share of nitrous oxide, the third most common greenhouse gas. Black carbon (soot), which is made of tiny particles rather than a gas, is also missing from climate plans. A 2026 study in the journal Science, whose authors include Ilissa Ocko at Spark Climate Solutions, calls for these "forgotten" pollutants to be included in national climate plans.

## Why it matters

These are not greenhouse gases in the usual sense, because they do not trap heat directly. They cause warming through chemical reactions in the atmosphere instead. They break down within hours to a few years (CO₂, by contrast, lasts for centuries), so cutting them would work quickly: their warming effect would start to fade almost straight away. Alex Archibald at the University of Cambridge describes them as "low-hanging fruit" for avoiding catastrophic climate tipping points.

## How indirect greenhouse gases warm the planet

**Carbon monoxide and VOCs** react with other compounds in the lower atmosphere to form ozone. This is different from the protective ozone layer high up in the stratosphere: ozone near the ground traps heat that would otherwise escape to space. At the same time, CO and VOCs use up hydroxyl radicals, which act as the atmosphere's natural "detergent" and break down methane. The more hydroxyl is taken up reacting with CO and VOCs, the less is left to destroy methane, which traps about 80 times more heat than CO₂ in the short term.

**Black carbon** (soot) is not a gas but a pollutant made of tiny particles. It comes mainly from fossil fuels and crops or vegetation that are not burned completely. It absorbs sunlight directly in the atmosphere. When it settles on snow and ice, it darkens the surface, so the surface absorbs more heat and melts faster.

Together with black carbon, these indirect greenhouse gases have caused approximately 0.3°C of warming so far. Some of this has been cancelled out by cooling aerosols such as sulphur dioxide, and by nitrogen oxides, which cool overall because they produce hydroxyl radicals, though they can also warm locally by producing ozone.

## Where these gases come from

The main sources are everyday ones:

- **Carbon monoxide:** incomplete burning in gas boilers, gas cookers, older petrol and diesel vehicles, and the burning of grassland and forest for farming
- **VOCs:** hydrocarbons that evaporate from fossil fuels, paint and cleaning solvents
- **Black carbon:** diesel engines, industrial processes and the burning of wood and other plant material

Air quality rules in countries such as the UK have already cut these gases a good deal, by tightening vehicle emissions standards and limiting VOCs in paint. The reason was health rather than climate, though, and many countries have looser rules. Almost no country includes indirect greenhouse gases in its climate pledges under the Paris Agreement.

## The hydrogen risk

If it is not managed carefully, the move away from fossil fuels could accidentally increase some indirect greenhouse gas emissions. Hydrogen is the smallest molecule, so it leaks, and it is sometimes vented into the air. Once there, it uses up hydroxyl radicals in the same way as CO, which makes methane last longer. Alastair Lewis at the University of York warns that if the most ambitious plans for burning hydrogen (for steel, fertiliser and aviation) go ahead without tight leak controls, the extra warming from hydrogen venting could reach 0.1°C by 2100.

The practical point is that choosing batteries over burning fuel wherever possible avoids this risk altogether. A low-carbon fuel can look carbon-neutral on paper while still adding to warming through these indirect effects.

## Key facts

- Indirect GHGs (mainly CO and VOCs) have caused ~15% of total warming since pre-industrial times, double the contribution of N₂O ([Science, Ocko et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aee5790))
- CO, VOCs and black carbon together have caused approximately 0.3°C of warming
- These gases break down within hours to a few years, so cutting emissions would quickly slow the rate of warming
- Almost no country includes indirect greenhouse gases in its Paris Agreement action plan
- Ambitious plans to use hydrogen as a fuel could add 0.1°C of warming by 2100 from leaks alone
- Lead researcher Ilissa Ocko (Spark Climate Solutions) calls them "a set of forgotten climate pollutants strongly contributing to today's warming"
