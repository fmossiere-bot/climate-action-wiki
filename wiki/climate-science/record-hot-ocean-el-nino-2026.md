# A Record-Hot Ocean, Rising Seas, and the 2026 El Niño

## What is it

The ocean covers **about 71% of the planet** and has absorbed more than 90% of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases since the industrial era. In 2026 that store of heat is warmer than at any point in the observational record, and a strong El Niño is building on top of it. Sea surface temperature reached 21.0°C on 21 June 2026, a record for the time of year in Copernicus Marine data. In the first half of the year, marine heatwaves reached around 82% of the global ocean at some point. The Mediterranean was the worst hit, with strong to extreme marine heat over roughly 80% of it.

This page brings together what that means for weather, ecosystems and policy. It draws on NOAA, WMO, Copernicus, IPCC and peer-reviewed sources, as gathered in a July 2026 Forbes column by Ingmar Rentzhog.

## Why it matters

Air temperature is what we feel, but ocean temperature is what sets the conditions for it. Kevin Trenberth of the National Center for Atmospheric Research calls it *"the clearest single sign the planet is warming: unlike the air, it barely wobbles. It just keeps rising."* Every second, the sea takes in roughly **12 Hiroshima bombs of heat**. That heat does not stay put. Some of it comes back through El Niño events, marine heatwaves and violent autumn rain. Some of it shows up in slow, essentially permanent changes to sea level, oxygen and acidity, which will shape the century that a child born today lives through.

## El Niño 2026: what NOAA and WMO are saying

In June 2026, NOAA declared an El Niño. It put the odds of a **very strong event** (above 2.0°C in the tropical Pacific) at 63% for the November to January peak. That would make it one of the biggest since records began in 1950. In July the World Meteorological Organization said the event was strengthening fast and warned of higher chances of drought, heavy rain and heatwaves around the world.

The WMO outlook for the coming months:

- **Wetter:** southern Europe, the southwestern United States
- **Drier:** Australia, India, the Horn of Africa, Central America, the Caribbean
- **Hurricanes:** a quieter Atlantic season (El Niño usually tears Atlantic storms apart with strong high-level winds), and stronger hurricanes in the central and eastern Pacific
- **US winter:** storms tracking across the southern US, a drier Ohio and Tennessee valley, and a milder north

The climate scientist Daniel Swain has written that California's winter of 2026–27 looks like earlier El Niño years that brought heavy flooding. He sees a real chance of an autumn tropical storm reaching the drought-hit Southwest, and of "atmospheric rivers" hitting the West Coast.

## The deeper link: hotter ocean, stronger El Niños

We usually say that El Niño drives the weather. The newer finding is that the ocean itself is now making the strongest El Niños more likely. A 2024 study in *Nature Communications* found that **deep-ocean warming alone could make extreme El Niños 40 to 80% more common**, and that the effect continues even after emissions stop.

Carlo Buontempo, head of the Copernicus Climate Change Service, calls the current conditions "uncharted territory". No El Niño has ever formed on an ocean this warm, and every El Niño forecast is built on how past events behaved on cooler seas. Two caveats apply. El Niño shifts the odds rather than guaranteeing any particular outcome, and this event is very likely to behave in ways we are less prepared for. Because an El Niño hits hardest late in its run, the **global temperature record** is more likely to be broken in 2027 than in 2026.

## Even without El Niño, a hot sea rewrites the weather

Warmer water evaporates faster, and warmer air holds more moisture. That tilts the odds towards heavier rain and gives extra energy to storms that form over hot water. It also removes the **cool nights** that coastal towns rely on, which was the quiet killer in this year's June heatwave. The Mediterranean showed the pattern clearly. A sea running 5 to 6°C above normal in early summer holds a huge store of moisture, and much of it falls later in the year as sudden, violent autumn rain. Europe saw this in 2023, when a record-hot North Atlantic was followed by brutal heat, deadly floods in Spain and fierce fires around the Mediterranean. The Mediterranean is also warming about 20% faster than the global ocean average, a European speed-up flagged by Anaïs Massé, scientific director at Fondation de la mer, in a June 2026 interview.

### August 2026: atmospheric water vapour record

This link between evaporation and rain now shows up in the records. In August 2026, the atmosphere held **more water vapour than in any month on record**: 27.35 kg/m² according to Copernicus C3S, 0.04 kg/m² above the previous record (July 2024). That extra 0.04 kg/m² equals roughly 20 trillion litres of water in the air, or 41 Sydney Harbours. There were two drivers. August was also the hottest month in NOAA's record (since 1850), and the historic El Niño is pumping huge amounts of moisture into the air from evaporating tropical oceans. As a rule of thumb, the air holds about 7% more water vapour for every °C of warming (the Clausius-Clapeyron relation).

This is a feedback loop. Water vapour is itself a greenhouse gas, so more CO2 means more warming, which means more water in the air, which means more warming. Dr Kimberley Reid of the University of Melbourne puts the cause simply: *"The root cause is the extra CO2 and methane."* Dr Andrew Dessler of Texas A&M said it was *"unimaginable that any month in the last few thousand years, maybe 100,000 years"* had held more water in the atmosphere than August 2026.

Extra water vapour makes rainfall heavier (including in cyclones), gives storms more energy as heat is released when the water condenses, and carries heat towards the poles. Most dangerously for human health, it **stops night-time cooling** during heatwaves. See [water vapour record (source)](../sources/water-vapour-record-copernicus-guardian-2026.md).

## Coral, fisheries, and the permanent bill

The seasonal effects are only the visible edge. The deeper changes are slower and, on human timescales, permanent. Heat sinks into the ocean, and even if we stopped all emissions tomorrow the deep sea would keep warming for centuries. Warm water expands (which raises sea level), holds less oxygen, and becomes more acidic as it soaks up CO₂.

- The world has just come through its **fourth global mass coral bleaching event**. NOAA's Coral Reef Watch says it hit about 84% of the world's reefs across all three oceans between 2023 and mid-2025
- A study in *Coral Reefs* found the heat stress ran without a break from 2018 to 2025, so bleaching is becoming an almost yearly event
- The IPCC finds that 70 to 90% of warm-water corals die at 1.5°C of warming, and over 99% at 2°C. Warming reached 1.37°C in 2025
- Heat pushes fish towards cooler water. During the last strong El Niño, warm seas helped collapse Peru's anchoveta fishery, the world's biggest single-species fishery, and a whole season was cancelled

Roughly **3 billion people** get their protein from the sea, so the cost falls on food supplies and coastal economies.

## Why a hot ocean means rising seas

Many of the human stories on this page (Tuvalu, the Gambia, Kale Island, Miquelon, Dublin Bay) are about the coast. The physical link between ocean heat and sea level is direct, and worth spelling out because it is not obvious:

- **Thermal expansion (~40% of observed rise).** Warm water takes up more space than cold water, so a warmer ocean is a bigger ocean before any ice melts at all. This alone accounts for roughly 40% of the sea-level rise observed since 1900.
- **Ice shelves melting from below (~60%).** The ocean is now warm enough for its heat to reach the underside of Antarctic ice shelves, the floating ice that holds back glaciers on land. Warm water melts the shelves, they hold back less, and the glaciers behind them slide faster into the sea. Pine Island Glacier is the best-known example, and the first study to attribute its retreat to human activity was published in 2026. See [Pine Island Glacier attribution (source)](../sources/pine-island-glacier-attribution-2026.md).
- **Warm air melts land ice from above.** Surface melt in Greenland and the retreat of mountain glaciers add to the rise. See [Glaciers in Retreat](../biodiversity-land/Glaciers%20in%20Retreat%20-%20%20Facts,%20Risks,%20and%20What%20Can%20Be%20Done.md).

The consequence is that **the ocean's store of heat sets sea level**. Even if emissions stopped tomorrow, the deep sea would keep warming for centuries, and sea level would keep rising with it. That is why the speed-up matters (the rate is now about 4.75 mm a year, up from about 2.65 mm a year in the 1990s). The question is how fast the baseline is moving, as well as how much has already happened.

## Two policy stories from 2026

This year showed both what can go right and how easily it can be undone.

On 17 January 2026, after nearly twenty years of negotiation, the **High Seas Treaty** entered into force, with very little public attention. It is the first binding agreement to protect ocean life in the two-thirds of the sea that lies beyond national borders, and more than 80 nations have signed it. For the first time, protected areas can be set up on the high seas. A deal covering half the planet's surface came into force with a fraction of the coverage given to a single interest-rate decision, and that gap says something in itself.

The second story is about monitoring. In May 2026 the US National Science Foundation ordered most of the **Ocean Observatories Initiative** to shut down. It is a network of about 900 deep-sea sensors, built for around $386 million, that had streamed live data on ocean heat, chemistry and currents for more than a decade. After protests from scientists and a rare cross-party revolt in Congress, the decision was reversed in June 2026. The reversal was right, but the episode came just as the ocean was entering conditions never seen before and a very strong El Niño was building. Helen Findlay of Plymouth Marine Laboratory warned that without steady monitoring we would have to *"navigate an increasingly volatile ocean with diminishing visibility."* The same pattern appears in the [AMOC monitoring cuts](../sources/amoc-monitoring-guardian-2026.md) reported in June 2026.

## The sulphate umbrella problem

There is a real complication inside the simple answer of "stop burning fossil fuels". Burning fossil fuels releases **sulphate particles** that reflect sunlight and cool the planet. The IPCC estimates that this "dirty umbrella" has been hiding around 0.4°C of warming. When we clean up the air, which we must do because sulphate pollution kills millions of people a year, some of that hidden heat comes through.

The ocean has already felt this. In 2020 a new rule cut sulphur in ship fuel by about 80%. NASA satellites then saw the bright "ship tracks" over busy shipping lanes drop sharply, and some studies suggest the sudden loss of those particles may have contributed to the faster ocean warming behind the 2023 records.

The answer is not to keep the air dirty. Cleaning it makes fast cuts to methane more urgent, so that we do not remove the umbrella faster than we turn down the heat underneath. Cutting methane and other short-lived climate pollutants could avoid up to **~0.5°C of warming** by mid-century.

## Key facts

- The ocean has absorbed **>90% of the extra heat** from global warming
- Ocean heat content set a record for the ninth year running in 2025, taking in about 12 Hiroshima bombs of heat every second (Trenberth)
- 21 June 2026: global sea surface record of 21.0°C. Marine heatwaves reached about 82% of the global ocean in the first half (H1) of 2026
- NOAA, June 2026: 63% odds of a very strong El Niño (>2.0°C in the Pacific) at the Nov–Jan peak
- Nature Communications 2024: deep-ocean warming alone may make extreme El Niños 40–80% more common
- Buontempo (Copernicus): current conditions are "uncharted territory"
- 4th global mass coral bleaching event, 2023 to mid-2025: about 84% of the world's reefs affected
- IPCC: 70–90% of warm-water corals lost at 1.5°C, >99% at 2°C. Warming in 2025 was 1.37°C
- High Seas Treaty in force from 17 January 2026, the first binding protection for the high seas
- The OOI (~900 deep-sea sensors, $386m) was saved from shutdown by a cross-party revolt in June 2026
- The 2020 shipping sulphur rule cut sulphur in ship fuel by about 80%, which may explain part of the faster ocean warming in 2023
- Clean energy now attracts about 2× as much investment a year as fossil fuels (IEA)
- The ocean accounts for >3% of world GDP and feeds about 3 billion people
- Carrying on as usual would cost **$8 trillion** in total by 2050 (High-Level Panel). Every $1 invested in a healthy ocean returns $5 (WRI)

## WRI's July 2026 update: the three big ocean numbers

Tom Pickerell, Global Director of Ocean at the World Resources Institute, published an overview in July 2026 that brings together the biggest ocean signals with the latest 2025–26 data:

- **Ocean heat**: the upper 2,000 m of the ocean stored more heat than ever before in 2025, the 9th record year in a row. The rate of warming from 2005–2025 was more than double the rate from 1960–2005. WRI expresses Trenberth's "12 Hiroshima bombs a second" as "7.6 billion cups of tea per second."
- **Sea level**: the global average is now about 11 cm higher than in January 1993, and the yearly rate has risen from about 2.65 mm/yr (1993–2011) to about 4.75 mm/yr (2012–2025).
- **Sea ice**: the Arctic hit its lowest yearly maximum on record in March 2025 (~14.3m km²) and matched it in 2026. The Antarctic had its 3rd-lowest yearly maximum in 2025 (~17.8m km²) and its 2nd-lowest summer minimum (~2m km²).
- **Acidification**: the ocean has absorbed about 25% of human CO₂ emissions, and average surface acidity is up about 30% since pre-industrial times.

### Concrete human consequences

- **Tuvalu** and Australia signed the Falepili Union Treaty, which creates a formal migration route as rising seas increasingly flood land that used to stay dry at high tide.
- In **The Gambia**, rising seas are pushing saltwater up the Gambia River into farmland. Some villages have abandoned rice and vegetable fields because the soil has become too salty.
- **Pacific Northwest oyster hatcheries** in Oregon and Washington, an industry once valued at about $111m, came close to collapse in the 2010s when huge numbers of oyster larvae died in acidified seawater low in aragonite (the mineral they need to build shells). Monitoring and water-treatment systems helped the industry partly recover.
- **Kale Island** in the Solomon Islands went under the sea around 2016, one of the first islands anywhere documented as lost to sea-level rise. Its 50,000 m² had been home to a small community. More than 26,000 Solomon Islanders have been relocated since 2008 because of sea encroachment and extreme weather. In July 2026 the artist Jason deCaires Taylor installed "The Solomon Siren" at the site. It is a pH-neutral sculpture of cement and biochar designed to become a base for coral, modelled on the Solomon Islander climate activist Gladys Habu Bartlett, and marked with sea levels for 2006, 2016 and 2026, plus projections for 2036 and 2046. See [Solomon Siren source summary](../sources/solomon-siren-kale-island-smithsonian-2026.md).
- **Miquelon**, part of the French archipelago of Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon off Newfoundland, will have to be moved because of storm and flooding risk, a first for France. Anaïs Massé (Fondation de la mer, June 2026) describes it as the first French village to be relocated because of climate change. See [Anaïs Massé interview (source)](../sources/anais-masse-fondation-mer-ecologie360-2026.md).

### WRI's three policy asks

1. Treat adaptation as essential. The faster rise in sea level should be changing adaptation timetables now, not be treated as a problem for later
2. Invest in healthy ocean ecosystems: mangroves, saltmarshes, seagrasses and coral reefs (blue carbon)
3. Make ocean data visible, by putting ocean figures alongside emissions so that leaders can see climate progress and risk

Pickerell is plain about what is now locked in: "deep ocean warming and interior ocean acidification are effectively irreversible on our timescales." That is not a reason to give up. It means protecting natural buffers, planning coastal infrastructure realistically, and cutting emissions to slow how fast the ocean keeps changing.
