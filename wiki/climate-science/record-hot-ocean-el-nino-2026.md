# A Record-Hot Ocean and the 2026 El Niño: The Climate Story We're Barely Telling

## What is it

The ocean covers **about 71% of the planet** and has absorbed **more than 90% of the excess heat** trapped by greenhouse gases since the industrial era. In 2026 that heat store is running warmer than at any point in the observational record — and a strong El Niño is building on top of it. Sea surface temperature hit **21.0°C on 21 June 2026**, a record for the time of year in Copernicus Marine data. Across the first half of the year, **marine heatwaves touched around 82% of the global ocean at some point**. The Mediterranean was the worst hit, with strong-to-extreme marine heat over roughly 80% of it.

This page pulls together what that means for weather, ecosystems and policy, drawing on **NOAA, WMO, Copernicus, IPCC and peer-reviewed sources** synthesised in a July 2026 Forbes column by Ingmar Rentzhog.

## Why it matters

Air temperature is what we feel. **Ocean temperature is what sets the terms.** Kevin Trenberth of the National Center for Atmospheric Research calls ocean heat *"the clearest single sign the planet is warming: unlike the air, it barely wobbles. It just keeps rising."* Every second the sea takes in roughly **12 Hiroshima bombs of heat**. That heat does not stay put. Some of it comes back through El Niño events, through marine heatwaves, through violent autumn rain, and through the slow, essentially permanent changes in sea level, oxygen and acidity that will shape the century a child born today will live in.

## El Niño 2026: what NOAA and WMO are saying

In **June 2026, NOAA declared an El Niño** and put the odds of a **very strong event (above 2.0°C in the tropical Pacific) at 63%** for the November-to-January peak. That would make it **one of the biggest since records began in 1950**. In July the **World Meteorological Organization** said it was strengthening fast and warned of higher chances of drought, heavy rain and heatwaves worldwide.

The WMO outlook for the coming months:

- **Wetter:** southern Europe, southwestern United States
- **Drier:** Australia, India, the Horn of Africa, Central America, the Caribbean
- **Winter hurricanes:** quieter Atlantic season (El Niño typically shreds Atlantic storms with high winds); stronger hurricanes in the central and eastern Pacific
- **US winter:** storms tracking across the southern US, drier Ohio and Tennessee valley, milder north

Climate scientist **Daniel Swain** has written that the winter of 2026–27 in California is shaping up like earlier flood-heavy El Niño years, with a real chance of an autumn tropical storm reaching the drought-hit Southwest and "atmospheric rivers" hitting the West Coast.

## The deeper link: hotter ocean, stronger El Niños

The headline framing is that El Niño drives weather. The deeper finding is that **the ocean is now making the strongest El Niños more likely**. A 2024 study in *Nature Communications* found that **deep-ocean warming alone could make extreme El Niños 40 to 80% more common**, and that the effect persists even after emissions stop.

**Carlo Buontempo**, head of the Copernicus Climate Change Service, calls the current conditions **"uncharted territory."** No El Niño has ever formed on an ocean this warm. Every El Niño forecast is based on how past El Niños behaved on cooler seas. Two honest caveats matter: El Niño shifts the odds rather than promising a specific outcome, and this event is very likely to behave in ways we are less prepared for. Because an El Niño hits hardest late in its run, the **global temperature record is more likely to fall in 2027 than in 2026**.

## Even without El Niño, a hot sea rewrites the weather

Warmer water evaporates faster, and warmer air holds more moisture. That loads the odds toward heavier rain and hands extra energy to storms that form over hot water. It also takes away the **cool nights** that coastlines rely on — the quiet killer in this year's June heatwave. The Mediterranean showed the pattern clearly: a sea running 5 to 6°C above normal in early summer is a huge store of moisture that often falls later in the year as **sudden, violent autumn rain**. Europe saw this in 2023, when a record-hot North Atlantic was followed by brutal heat, deadly Spanish floods and fierce fires around the Mediterranean.

## Coral, fisheries, and the permanent bill

The seasonal effects are only the visible edge. The deeper changes are slower and, on human timescales, permanent. Ocean heat sinks; even if we stopped all emissions tomorrow the deep sea would go on warming for centuries. Warm water expands (driving sea level rise), holds less oxygen, and acidifies as it soaks up CO₂.

- The world just came through its **fourth global mass coral bleaching event**, which NOAA's Coral Reef Watch says hit about **84% of the world's reefs across all three oceans** between 2023 and mid-2025
- A study in *Coral Reefs* found the heat stress ran **unbroken from 2018 to 2025**; bleaching is becoming near-yearly
- The IPCC finds **70 to 90% of warm-water corals die at 1.5°C** warming, and **over 99% at 2°C**; warming reached **1.37°C in 2025**
- Heat pushes fish toward cooler water; during the last strong El Niño, warm seas helped collapse **Peru's anchoveta fishery** — the world's biggest single-species fishery — and an entire season was cancelled

Roughly **3 billion people get their protein from the sea**. The bill lands on food supplies and coastal economies.

## Two policy stories from 2026

The record shows both what can go right and how fragile it is.

**Good news that almost no one noticed.** On **17 January 2026** — after nearly twenty years of negotiation — the **High Seas Treaty entered into force**. It is the first binding deal to protect ocean life in the two-thirds of the sea that lies beyond national borders, and more than 80 nations have signed on. For the first time, protected areas can be established on the high seas. A deal covering half the planet's surface entered force with a fraction of the attention given to a single interest-rate decision — that gap is part of the story.

**Fragile monitoring.** In May 2026 the US National Science Foundation ordered the shutdown of most of the **Ocean Observatories Initiative**, a network of about 900 deep-sea sensors, built for around $386 million, that had streamed live data on ocean heat, chemistry and currents for over a decade. After scientist outcry and a rare bipartisan revolt in Congress, the decision was **reversed in June 2026**. The reversal was the right call — but it happened while the ocean is entering territory we have never seen and a very strong El Niño is building. **Helen Findlay** (Plymouth Marine Laboratory) put it plainly: strip out steady monitoring and we would be left to *"navigate an increasingly volatile ocean with diminishing visibility."* The same pattern shows up in the [AMOC monitoring cuts](../sources/amoc-monitoring-guardian-2026.md) documented in June 2026.

## The sulphate umbrella problem

There is a real complication buried inside the "just stop burning fossil fuels" answer. Fossil fuels release **sulphate particles that reflect sunlight and cool the planet**. The IPCC reckons this "dirty umbrella" has been hiding **around 0.4°C of warming**. When we clean the air, which we must do because sulphate pollution kills millions of people a year, some of that hidden heat comes through.

The ocean has already felt this. After a **2020 rule cut sulphur in ship fuel by about 80%**, NASA satellites saw the reflective "ship tracks" over busy shipping lanes drop sharply, and some studies suggest the sudden loss of those aerosols **may have contributed to the acceleration of ocean warming behind the 2023 records**.

The answer is not to keep the air dirty. It is that **cleaning it makes fast methane cuts more urgent, not less** — otherwise we tear off the umbrella faster than we turn down the heat underneath. Cutting methane and other short-lived climate pollutants could avoid up to **~0.5°C of warming by mid-century**.

## Key facts

- Ocean has absorbed **>90% of excess heat** from global warming
- **Ocean heat content: ninth record year running in 2025**; ~12 Hiroshima bombs of heat every second (Trenberth)
- **21 June 2026: 21.0°C global sea surface record**; marine heatwaves touched **~82% of global ocean** in H1 2026
- **NOAA June 2026:** 63% odds of a very strong El Niño (>2.0°C Pacific) at Nov–Jan peak
- **Nature Communications 2024:** deep-ocean warming alone may make **extreme El Niños 40–80% more common**
- **Buontempo (Copernicus):** current conditions are **"uncharted territory"**
- **4th global mass coral bleaching event**, 2023–mid-2025: **~84% of world's reefs**
- **IPCC:** 70–90% of warm-water corals lost at 1.5°C, **>99% at 2°C**; 2025 warming = 1.37°C
- **High Seas Treaty in force 17 January 2026** — first binding high-seas protection
- **OOI (~900 deep-sea sensors, $386m)** narrowly saved from shutdown by bipartisan revolt in June 2026
- **2020 shipping sulphur rule** cut ship-fuel sulphur ~80%; may explain part of 2023 ocean warming acceleration
- Clean energy now attracts **~2× as much investment per year as fossil fuels** (IEA)
- **Ocean = >3% of world GDP**; feeds ~3 billion people
- **$8 trillion** cumulative cost of business-as-usual by 2050 (High-Level Panel); **$1 → $5 return** on healthy-ocean investment (WRI)

## WRI's July 2026 update: the three big ocean numbers

The World Resources Institute's Global Director of Ocean, **Tom Pickerell**, published a synthesis in July 2026 that stitches together the three biggest ocean signals with the latest 2025–26 data:

- **Ocean heat**: the upper 2,000 m of the ocean stored more heat than ever before in 2025 — the **9th consecutive record year**. The rate of warming **2005–2025 was more than double** the rate **1960–2005**. Kevin Trenberth's "12 Hiroshima bombs a second" metric is echoed at WRI as "7.6 billion cups of tea per second."
- **Sea level**: the global average is now about **11 cm higher than in January 1993**, and the annual rate has accelerated from **~2.65 mm/yr (1993–2011)** to **~4.75 mm/yr (2012–2025)**.
- **Sea ice**: **Arctic** hit its lowest annual maximum on record in March 2025 (~14.3m km²) and tied it in 2026. **Antarctic** had its 3rd-lowest annual maximum in 2025 (~17.8m km²) and 2nd-lowest summer minimum (~2m km²).
- **Acidification**: the ocean has absorbed ~25% of human CO₂ emissions; average surface acidity is up ~30% since the pre-industrial era.

### Concrete human consequences

- **Tuvalu** and Australia signed the **Falepili Union Treaty**, creating a formal migration pathway as rising seas increasingly flood areas that once remained dry at high tide.
- In **The Gambia**, rising seas are pushing saltwater up the Gambia River into farmland; some villages have abandoned rice and vegetable fields because soils became too salty.
- **Pacific Northwest oyster hatcheries** (Oregon and Washington, industry once valued at ~$111m) came close to collapse in the 2010s due to mass larval die-offs linked to acidified low-aragonite seawater. Monitoring and water-treatment systems partially recovered the industry.

### WRI's three policy asks

1. **Treat adaptation as essential** — the acceleration of sea-level rise should be resetting adaptation timetables today, not treated as a future problem
2. **Invest in healthy ocean ecosystems** — mangroves, saltmarshes, seagrasses, coral reefs (blue carbon)
3. **Make ocean data visible** — put ocean metrics alongside emissions so leaders see climate progress and risk

WRI's Pickerell is blunt about what is now baked in: **"deep ocean warming and interior ocean acidification are effectively irreversible on our timescales."** That does not mean giving up — it means protecting natural buffers, planning coastal infrastructure realistically, and cutting emissions to slow how fast the ocean keeps changing.
