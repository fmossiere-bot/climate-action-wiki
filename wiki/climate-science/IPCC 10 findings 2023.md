## Overview

In March 2023, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) published the final part of its Sixth Assessment Report. It is the most comprehensive scientific review of climate change yet produced. More than 780 scientists worked on it across three working groups, and it runs to nearly 8,000 pages. Its message is serious: things are worse than earlier reports thought, the time left to act is shrinking, and the cost of doing nothing will be severe and, in some cases, permanent.

Below are ten findings worth knowing.

---

## 1. We have already warmed the planet by 1.1 degrees, and the effects are visible everywhere

Human activity has raised the global average temperature by 1.1 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels. That may sound small. It has already caused changes to the climate system not seen for centuries, in some cases millennia. Every region is affected, from rising seas and shrinking Arctic ice to more frequent and more intense extreme weather.

---

## 2. Impacts on people and ecosystems are worse than scientists expected

The damage from climate change is more widespread and more severe than earlier assessments predicted. Ecosystems are being disrupted, people are losing their livelihoods and communities are being split apart. Future risks also climb quickly with every extra fraction of a degree. The gap between 1.5 and 2 degrees is large: for many parts of the world it is the difference between manageable and devastating.

---

## 3. Adaptation works, but it is severely underfunded

At least 170 countries now include adaptation, meaning measures to cope with climate impacts, in their climate policies. Most of these measures are still small, reactive and aimed at near-term risks rather than long-term resilience. The gap between what is being done and what is needed is mostly about money.

> The IPCC estimates that developing countries alone will need 127 billion dollars per year by 2030 and 295 billion dollars per year by 2050 to adapt to climate change. Between 2017 and 2018, adaptation finance reached only 23 to 46 billion dollars, covering just 4 to 8% of what is tracked as climate finance overall.

The encouraging part is that proven, affordable adaptation solutions already exist. With enough funding and political will, they can make communities more resilient and bring wider development benefits at the same time.

---

## 4. For some communities, it is already too late to adapt

Some climate impacts are now so severe that no adaptation can fully prevent the losses. In tropical coastal areas, coral reefs that once fed local communities have been largely destroyed. Low-lying communities have had to move and leave cultural sites behind. This is happening now, at 1.1 degrees of warming, not in some distant future.

The IPCC separates two kinds of limit. "Soft" limits are where solutions exist but money or politics block them. "Hard" limits are where no available strategy can avoid the damage. Both are already being reached in parts of the world.

---

## 5. Emissions must peak before 2025 to stay below 1.5 degrees

The IPCC finds a greater than 50% chance that global warming will reach or pass 1.5 degrees Celsius between 2021 and 2040. In a high-emissions scenario, that could happen sooner, and by the end of the century temperatures could rise by between 3.3 and 5.7 degrees.

To hold warming to 1.5 degrees, emissions need to peak immediately and then fall by 43% by 2030 and 60% by 2035, compared with 2019 levels. For context, the last time global temperatures were more than 2.5 degrees above pre-industrial levels was over three million years ago.

---

## 6. Fossil fuels must be phased out, and quickly

Fossil fuels are the main cause of the climate crisis. The remaining carbon budget for 1.5 degrees, the amount of CO2 we can still emit, is around 510 billion tonnes. Emissions from fossil fuel infrastructure that already exists or is already planned could reach 850 billion tonnes on their own, well over that budget.

The solutions are known: retire existing fossil fuel infrastructure early, cancel new projects, fit carbon capture where needed, and expand renewables. Solar and wind are now cheaper than fossil fuels in many parts of the world. What holds the transition back today is mostly politics and finance, not technology.

---

## 7. Deep cuts are needed across the entire economy

Fossil fuels dominate, but every sector has to cut emissions. Power generation, buildings, industry and transport together account for close to 80% of global emissions. Agriculture, forestry and land use make up the rest. No route to net zero works by changing only one sector.

---

## 8. Carbon removal is now unavoidable

Even with fast emissions cuts everywhere, the IPCC finds that every credible pathway to 1.5 degrees needs some carbon removal. That includes natural approaches, such as restoring forests and building up carbon in soils, and newer technologies that pull carbon dioxide straight out of the air.

How much removal is needed depends on how quickly emissions fall. Estimates range from 5 to 16 billion tonnes of CO2 per year by the middle of the century.

---

## 9. Climate finance must increase dramatically this decade

More money still flows to fossil fuels than to cutting emissions and adapting to climate change. Climate finance has grown by around 60% since the previous IPCC assessment, but it is still far short of what is needed.

For cutting emissions alone, climate finance has to grow between three and six times by 2030. The shortfall is largest where the need is greatest. Investment in Southeast Asia and the Pacific needs to rise at least sixfold by 2030, in Africa fivefold, and in the Middle East fourteenfold. For agriculture and land use, recent funding is 10 to 31 times below what the Paris Agreement requires.

---

## 10. Climate change will deepen inequality unless we act differently

The wealthiest 10% of households are responsible for more than 45% of global greenhouse gas emissions. The poorest 50% are responsible for 15% at most. Yet the impacts fall hardest on the poorest and most vulnerable.

Between 3.3 and 3.6 billion people live in countries that are highly vulnerable to climate impacts. The most exposed regions include the Arctic, Central and South America, small island states, South Asia and much of sub-Saharan Africa. Without a deliberate effort to make the transition fair, climate change will widen existing inequalities rather than narrow them.

---

## Where this leaves us

The Sixth Assessment Report leaves little room for doubt. The science is settled, the risks are growing, and most of the solutions are already known. What is missing is action at the scale and speed required. Every fraction of a degree avoided matters, and every year of delay makes the job harder and more expensive.
