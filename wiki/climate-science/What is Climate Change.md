# What Is Climate Change? Earth's Energy Balance and the Greenhouse Effect

This page covers the basic physics behind why the planet is getting hotter: Earth's "energy balance", the greenhouse effect, and how scientists know human activity is driving today's warming. Once you understand these basics, it becomes much easier to tell solid climate reporting apart from hype, misrepresentation or spin.

## Earth's Energy Balance: A Simple Model

The easiest way into the basic science of climate change is to build a simple model, a stripped-down version of something too complex to observe directly. Here, the model predicts Earth's surface temperature by comparing the energy the planet receives with the energy it loses. To keep things simple, this version leaves out the atmosphere, ocean currents and the effect of water on climate. We add the atmosphere back in below.

**Energy in.** Energy from the Sun arrives as flux, measured in watts per square metre. Only the sunlit half of a sphere receives it at any one time, so the calculation divides the total incoming solar flux by four to spread it evenly over Earth's whole surface.

**Energy out.** Around 30% of the incoming energy is reflected straight back to space, mostly by clouds and pale surfaces. This fraction is called the **albedo**. The other 70% is absorbed, turned into heat and eventually given off again as infrared radiation. How much an object gives off depends on its temperature, following the Stefan-Boltzmann law. In this simple model, Earth is treated as a plain "blackbody", an ideal object that absorbs and emits radiation perfectly.

Setting energy in equal to energy out and solving for temperature gives a very specific prediction, and this is where the model gets interesting.

### Why the simple model gets it wrong

Using real numbers (an albedo of 0.30, a solar flux of about 1,361 W/m², and the Stefan-Boltzmann constant) gives a predicted average surface temperature of **255 K, or about -18°C**. That is roughly 33°C colder than Earth's actual average temperature of around 288 K (15°C).

The gap shows exactly what the simple model left out: the atmosphere, and in particular the **greenhouse effect**. The Moon is the same distance from the Sun as Earth but has almost no atmosphere, so its surface temperature swings wildly between extremes with nothing to even it out. Earth's atmosphere works like a shield. It softens daytime heat and traps some outgoing radiation overnight so it does not get extremely cold. Gases such as CO2, methane and water vapour do this by absorbing outgoing infrared radiation and sending some of it back down. That is what "greenhouse gas" means in practice.

Apart from greenhouse gases, several other things shift Earth's temperature:

- **Aerosols** from volcanoes and human activity, some of which cool the Earth
- **Changes in solar flux**, meaning variation in how much energy the Sun gives out
- **Earth's orbital cycle**, the slow wobbles in Earth's path around the Sun that drive ice ages over tens of thousands of years
- **Land use change**, since forest cover and sea ice both affect how much sunlight the surface reflects (its albedo)

## The Greenhouse Effect and Radiative Forcing

Climate change is explained by two ideas together: the basic physics of Earth's energy balance above, and the part greenhouse gases play in regulating it.

Greenhouse gas concentrations have clearly gone up. CO2 is the main gas of concern, measured in parts per million (ppm). Before industrialisation, CO2 was around 280 ppm. Today it is roughly 424 ppm, over 50% higher, and that rise has happened around 100 times faster than any natural rise in the geological record.

Temperatures have followed. Since pre-industrial times, the planet has warmed by about 1.1°C. According to [NOAA](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/understanding-climate/climate-change-atmospheric-carbon-dioxide), the last time atmospheric CO2 was this high was more than **3 million years ago**, when temperatures were 2-3°C warmer than pre-industrial levels and sea level was 5-25 metres higher. [Research reconstructing temperatures over the past 66 million years](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/abs/10.1146/annurev-earth-082420-063026) supports this. As palaeoecologist Jacquelyn Gill has explained, today's CO2 levels are unlikely to shift temperature and sea level quite that far, mainly because the geography of the planet is different now.

This imbalance between energy coming in and energy going out has a name: **radiative forcing**, sometimes called "climate forcing".

> Radiative forcing = Incoming Energy - Outgoing Energy

When more incoming energy is trapped than can escape, radiative forcing goes up and Earth's average temperature rises. Scientists can break this down further and measure how much each factor, some warming and some cooling, adds to the total. Taking 1750 as the starting point, the overall human contribution is clearly one of net warming, even though the individual estimates carry some uncertainty. A 2021 NASA/NOAA study found that [Earth's energy imbalance has roughly doubled since 2005](https://www.nasa.gov/feature/langley/joint-nasa-noaa-study-finds-earths-energy-imbalance-has-doubled).

## We Are the Cause

Human-caused (anthropogenic) CO2 emissions have risen sharply in every world region since the start of the industrial revolution, as [Our World in Data's emissions-by-region charts](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/annual-co-emissions-by-region) show. CO2 gets most of the attention, but other greenhouse gases, especially methane, matter too.

Total global emissions come to roughly 49.8 GtCO2e. That stands for gigatonnes of CO2-equivalent, a single unit that lets different gases be compared on the same scale. The [World Resources Institute's sector-flow breakdown](https://www.wri.org/insights/4-charts-explain-greenhouse-gas-emissions-countries-and-sectors) traces where those emissions come from: which parts of the economy (such as transport) produce them, which specific uses (such as road transport or aviation) they belong to, and which gas (CO2, methane or others) is responsible.
