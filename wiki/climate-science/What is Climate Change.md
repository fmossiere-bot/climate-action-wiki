# What Is Climate Change? Earth's Energy Balance and the Greenhouse Effect

This page covers the basic physical science behind why the planet is getting hotter: Earth's "energy balance," the greenhouse effect, and how scientists know human activity is driving today's warming. Understanding these fundamentals makes it much easier to tell the difference between solid climate reporting and hype, misrepresentation, or spin.

## Earth's Energy Balance: A Simple Model

The easiest way to understand the basic science of climate change is to build a simple model: a stripped-down representation of something too complex to observe directly. Here, the model predicts the Earth's surface temperature by comparing the energy the planet receives with the energy it loses. To keep things simple, this version ignores the atmosphere, ocean currents, and water's effect on climate. We add the atmosphere back in below.

**Energy in.** Solar energy arrives as flux, measured in watts per square metre. Only the sunlit half of a sphere actually receives that energy, so the calculation divides the total incoming solar flux by four to spread it evenly across Earth's whole surface.

**Energy out.** Around 30% of that incoming energy is reflected straight back to space, mostly by clouds and light-coloured surfaces. This fraction is called the **albedo**. The remaining 70% is absorbed, converted to heat, and eventually re-emitted as infrared radiation, governed by the **Stefan-Boltzmann law**, which describes how much energy any object radiates based on its temperature. For this simple model, Earth is treated as behaving like a plain blackbody.

Setting energy in equal to energy out and solving for temperature gives a very specific prediction, which is where the model gets interesting.

### Why the simple model gets it wrong

Plugging in real numbers (an albedo of 0.30, solar flux of 1,372 W/m², and the Stefan-Boltzmann constant) gives a predicted average surface temperature of **255 K, or about -18°C**. That is roughly 35°C colder than Earth's actual average temperature of around 290 K (17°C).

The gap reveals exactly what the simple model left out: the atmosphere, and specifically the **greenhouse effect**. The Moon sits at the same distance from the Sun as Earth but has no atmosphere to speak of, and its surface temperature swings wildly between extremes with nothing to smooth it out. Earth's atmosphere acts like a shield, moderating daytime heat and trapping some outgoing radiation overnight to prevent extreme cold. Gases like CO2, methane and water vapour do this by absorbing outgoing infrared radiation and re-emitting some of it back down, which is what "greenhouse gas" means in practice.

Beyond greenhouse gases, several other factors shift Earth's temperature:

- **Aerosols** from volcanoes and human activity, some of which actually cool the Earth
- **Changes in solar flux**, i.e. variation in how much energy the Sun is putting out
- **Earth's orbital cycle**, the slow wobbles in Earth's path around the Sun that drive ice ages over tens of thousands of years
- **Land use change**, since forest cover and sea ice both affect how much sunlight the surface reflects (its albedo)

## The Greenhouse Effect and Radiative Forcing

Two ideas combine to explain climate change: the basic physics of Earth's energy balance above, and the role greenhouse gases play in regulating it.

Greenhouse gas concentrations are unambiguously up. CO2 is the main gas of concern, measured in parts per million (ppm). Pre-industrial CO2 sat around 280 ppm; today it is roughly 424 ppm, over 50% higher, and that increase has happened around 100 times faster than any natural increase in the geological record.

Temperatures have followed. Since the pre-industrial era, the planet has warmed by about 1.1°C. According to [NOAA](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/understanding-climate/climate-change-atmospheric-carbon-dioxide), the last time atmospheric CO2 was this high was more than **3 million years ago**, when temperatures were 2-3°C warmer than pre-industrial levels and sea level was 5-25 metres higher, a finding reinforced by [research reconstructing temperatures over the past 66 million years](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/abs/10.1146/annurev-earth-082420-063026). Present-day CO2 levels are unlikely to move temperature and sea level quite that dramatically, mainly because of geographical differences between now and then, as palaeoecologist Jacquelyn Gill has explained.

This imbalance between incoming and outgoing energy has a name: **radiative forcing**, sometimes called "climate forcing."

> Radiative forcing = Incoming Energy - Outgoing Energy

When more incoming energy gets trapped than can escape, radiative forcing increases, and Earth's average temperature rises. Scientists can break this down further, quantifying how individual factors, some warming, some cooling, contribute to the total. Using 1750 as a baseline, the cumulative human contribution is clearly a net warming effect, even though the individual estimates carry some uncertainty. A 2021 NASA/NOAA study found that [Earth's energy imbalance has roughly doubled since 2005](https://www.nasa.gov/feature/langley/joint-nasa-noaa-study-finds-earths-energy-imbalance-has-doubled).

## We Are the Cause

Anthropogenic (human-caused) CO2 emissions have risen sharply by world region since the start of the industrial revolution, a trend well documented in [Our World in Data's emissions-by-region charts](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/annual-co-emissions-by-region). CO2 gets most of the attention, but other greenhouse gases, particularly methane, matter too.

Total global emissions run to roughly 49.8 GtCO2e (gigatonnes of CO2-equivalent, a single unit that allows different gases to be compared on the same scale). The [World Resources Institute's sector-flow breakdown](https://www.wri.org/insights/4-charts-explain-greenhouse-gas-emissions-countries-and-sectors) traces where those emissions come from: which economic sectors (like transport) produce them, which specific end-uses (like road transport or aviation) they fall under, and which gas (CO2, methane, or others) is responsible.
