# Keynote: Dr Kate Marvel on Clouds and Climate Uncertainty

Climate scientist Dr Kate Marvel built her keynote around one of the biggest remaining unknowns in climate projections: clouds. In her words, climate models are in the end stories about what could happen, and which story plays out still depends a great deal on what we do next. She pointed to one feedback loop in particular. As air warms, more moisture evaporates, which forms more cloud and brings heavier rain, and that in turn feeds back into the climate system.

## What About Clouds?

Clouds are still one of the largest sources of uncertainty in how much the planet will warm for a given rise in CO2. If CO2 doubles from pre-industrial levels, warming could be a manageable 1.5°C or something closer to a hellish 4.5°C. Much of that range exists because we still do not fully understand how clouds behave.

That may be starting to change. A major study led by Steven Sherwood (University of New South Wales) [identified the six most important ways](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2019RG000678) that global warming changes clouds. They include less cloud cover overall, mid-latitude clouds moving towards the poles, and more water in icy clouds. Sherwood's team found that, taken together, these feedbacks could do anything from leaving total warming unchanged to raising it substantially. That spread shows how much uncertainty clouds still bring to climate projections.

Experiments are helping too. A cloud chamber at CERN has, for the first time, recreated atmospheric cloud conditions in a laboratory. This gives climate models real measurements where they previously had to rely on a wide range of assumptions. Other researchers are tackling the problem from different angles, studying how ice crystals form or building cloud chambers of their own.

Aerosols, tiny particles in the air, add another layer. Dust, soot from power stations and even airborne fungi are the seeds around which water droplets or ice crystals form clouds in the first place. As atmospheric scientist Graham Feingold has put it, "clouds have a very, very difficult time forming in the absence of these particles." The size, type and number of particles all affect how bright a cloud is, because they change how many droplets or crystals it holds. Current models handle this direct brightness effect reasonably well. They are weaker on the knock-on effects, such as how aerosols change how long a cloud lasts. This matters more and more as air pollution is cut for health reasons: fewer particles means the clouds that depend on them will change too, in ways models do not yet fully capture.
