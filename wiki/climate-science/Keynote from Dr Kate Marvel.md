# Keynote: Dr Kate Marvel on Clouds and Climate Uncertainty

Climate scientist Dr Kate Marvel's keynote centred on one of the biggest remaining sources of uncertainty in climate projections: clouds. As she put it, climate models are ultimately stories about what could happen, and which story plays out still depends heavily on what we do next. One feedback loop she highlighted directly: as air warms it evaporates more moisture, which forms more cloud and drives heavier rainfall, itself a source of further feedback into the climate system.

## What About Clouds?

Clouds remain one of the largest sources of uncertainty in how much the planet will actually warm for a given rise in CO2. Whether doubling CO2 from pre-industrial levels leads to a manageable 1.5°C of warming or something closer to a hellish 4.5°C depends heavily on how clouds respond, and that range of possible outcomes exists largely because cloud behaviour is still not fully understood.

That may be starting to change. A landmark study led by Steven Sherwood (University of New South Wales) [identified the six most important ways](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2019RG000678) that global warming changes clouds, including reduced overall cloud cover, a shift of mid-latitude clouds toward the poles, and more water content in icy clouds. Taken together, Sherwood's team found these feedbacks could range from having no net effect on total warming to substantially increasing it, illustrating just how much uncertainty clouds still introduce into climate projections.

Physical experiments are helping too. A cloud chamber experiment at CERN has, for the first time, been able to recreate atmospheric cloud conditions in a lab setting, offering climate models a much-needed empirical input where they previously had to rely on a wide range of assumptions. Other researchers are approaching the problem from different angles, studying ice crystal formation directly or building their own cloud chambers.

Aerosols add a further layer of complexity. Dust, soot from power plants, and even airborne fungi act as the seeds around which water droplets or ice crystals form clouds in the first place. As atmospheric scientist Graham Feingold has put it, "clouds have a very, very difficult time forming in the absence of these particles." The size, type and number of aerosol particles present all affect how bright a cloud is, by changing how many droplets or crystals it contains. Current climate models handle the direct brightness effect of aerosols reasonably well, but do a poorer job capturing their knock-on effects, such as how they alter a cloud's lifetime. This matters increasingly as air pollution is cut for public health reasons: fewer aerosol particles in the atmosphere means the clouds that depend on them will change too, in ways models don't yet fully capture.
