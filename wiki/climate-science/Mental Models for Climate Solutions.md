## Introduction

Say you had €100 million to put into one or more climate solutions. Which would you choose, and why?

There is no simple answer. Before looking at particular technologies or sectors, it helps to step back and look at how we think about climate solutions in the first place. The frameworks we use to judge options are not neutral. They carry assumptions, priorities and blind spots that decide which solutions get attention and which get ignored.

In this module, climate solutions cover two areas. Mitigation means reducing and eliminating greenhouse gas emissions. Removal means physically taking excess carbon out of the atmosphere and storing it safely. Adaptation matters too, but it is covered elsewhere because it does not tackle the root cause of the problem.

---

## Why mental models matter

A mental model is a way of organising how we think about a problem. With climate solutions, the model you use decides what you measure, what you put first and, in the end, what you recommend.

Even experts often present their assessments of climate solutions as purely objective. In practice, every framework rests on assumptions. Some are stated openly, many are not. Knowing which model is in use, and what it leaves out, is essential for making sound decisions. Some of the models below rule each other out, and others can be used together.

---

## Mental Model 1: Climate Impact and Costs

The most common way to compare climate solutions looks at two things: how much greenhouse gas a solution cuts or removes, and what it costs. Two well-known frameworks are built on this model.

**Project Drawdown** analyses a wide range of technologies and practices. For each, it estimates the potential to cut or remove emissions, the cost of putting it in place, and the money saved or spent compared with carrying on as usual. It is a useful starting point for seeing which solutions could have the biggest impact at scale.

**The Marginal Abatement Cost (MAC) curve**, first popularised by McKinsey in 2007, plots solutions on two axes: how much they can cut emissions, and the cost per tonne of CO2 avoided. It gives a quick visual comparison across many options and has been widely used by governments and businesses.

What makes this model useful is also what limits it. It works well for what can be measured, but it tends to gloss over large uncertainties in both cost and impact estimates. It leaves out local context, and it has nothing to say about areas that cut across everything, such as policy, finance, communications or education. Above all, it is silent on justice and fairness. When whole sectors of the economy are being reshaped, there are always winners and losers, and this model does not help identify who they are.

## Mental Model 2: Growth vs Degrowth

Few debates in climate circles get as heated as this one. The two sides hold very different views on what kind of economic future can go hand in hand with a stable climate.

**The green growth model** holds that rapid decarbonisation is possible without giving up economic growth. It argues that new technology, pushed along by market incentives and policy support, can separate emissions from growth. In practice, this model sits under almost all climate policy today, including major laws such as the US Inflation Reduction Act. Project Drawdown and MAC curves also assume the economy keeps growing.

**The degrowth model** argues that this is not enough. Its supporters, including economist Jason Hickel and journalist George Monbiot, say the growth model is itself a driver of the crisis. More economic activity means more demand for energy, which makes the transition harder and slower. Degrowth would reduce overall consumption and energy use and, with shorter working weeks and living wages, could improve people's lives at the same time.

Both sides have strengths and blind spots. Green growth may underestimate how fast and how far things need to change. Degrowth raises fair questions about whether it is politically possible and who would bear the sacrifices. The debate deserves to be taken seriously, without dismissing either side.

## Mental Model 3: A Systems Lens

A systems approach recognises that climate change does not sit on its own. It is tied up with public health, social inequality, land use, the economy and how we are governed. Solutions that deal with only one of these often cause problems somewhere else.

One practical form of this thinking is **multisolving**: designing actions that tackle several problems at once. Making homes more energy efficient, for example, cuts emissions, lowers energy bills for low-income households, improves indoor air and health, and creates skilled local jobs. The climate benefit is real, but it is one of several results.

**Doughnut Economics**, developed by economist Kate Raworth, is another systems-based framework. It sets two boundaries: a social foundation that no one should fall below, and an ecological ceiling we should not push past. In this model, a good climate solution stays within both.

Tools such as **En-ROADS**, a free online climate simulator from Climate Interactive, let users explore how different mixes of policies and technologies play out across the whole system. It is built on systems thinking and puts the multisolving approach into practice.

The systems lens is harder to apply than a simple cost-and-impact comparison, but it is closer to how the real world works.

---

## Mental Model 4: Masculine and Feminine Framings

This model borrows the idea of complementary forces, sometimes described as Yin and Yang. It asks a different kind of question: as well as what to do, how to do it well.

The outward, action-focused side is about building, scaling up and rolling out solutions. It is the drive to install renewable energy, electrify transport and restore forests as fast as possible.

The inward, reflective side asks whether those actions are being done the right way. It is the voice that asks whether scaling up electric cars is really clean if the lithium supply chain repeats the harms of fossil fuel extraction. It challenges land grabs for solar farms that push communities out. It insists on listening before acting.

Neither side is enough on its own. Action without reflection can cause harm, even with good intentions. Reflection without action can turn into paralysis. The strongest climate solutions keep both in balance: ambitious in scale and careful in how they are carried out.

## Summary

- Mental models shape how we think about, understand and compare climate solutions. "Experts" often present their analyses of solutions as a purely objective exercise. This module shows that experts too work with underlying mental models, and these strongly influence which climate solutions are discussed, promoted or sidelined, and how they are compared. Some of the models rule each other out (growth vs degrowth, for example), while others do not.
    
- _Mental Model 1: Climate Impacts and Costs._ The most common mental model for comparing climate solutions is based on some measure of greenhouse gas cuts and costs. Its strength is that it is practical, measurable and makes comparison easy. Its weakness is that it leaves no room for solutions that cut across areas, such as politics or communications. It also hides or waves away considerable uncertainty, and it leaves no room for questions of justice and fairness.
    
- _Mental Model 2: Growth vs. Degrowth._ The green growth model holds that we can decarbonise quickly without hurting economic growth. The degrowth model argues that to properly deal with our environmental, social and economic problems, western economies have to shrink rather than grow. People feel strongly about this debate one way or the other, and both sides make convincing arguments but also have blind spots.
    
- _Mental model 3: A Systems lens._ This includes frameworks such as multi-solving, where problems are bundled together and solved with a single approach. It also includes Doughnut Economics, which is both a model and a tool for reaching several environmental, social and economic goals at once.
    
- _Mental Model 4: Masculine vs feminine framings._ This approach asks in what ways you are going out to take action and build a fairer, more sustainable world, and how you are making sure you listen to feedback, so that your actions are the right ones and done in the right way.
