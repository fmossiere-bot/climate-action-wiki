# Global Impact of Climate Change: 1.5°C, Tipping Points and What's Already Happening

As the climate warms, the permafrost across Siberia and other northern regions is thawing. It releases water and makes the ground beneath whole towns unstable. More seriously for the planet, it also releases greenhouse gases that had been locked away for thousands of years. It is a good way into the subject of this page: how much warming the world can take before impacts stop growing in a predictable way, what is already changing today, and why thresholds and tipping points matter so much for how bad this gets.

## Why 1.5°C Matters

The planet is currently about 1.1-1.3°C warmer than before the industrial era, and records keep falling. The [World Meteorological Organization](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/2023-shatters-climate-records-major-impacts) confirmed that 2023 was almost certainly the warmest year on record, at 1.4°C above pre-industrial temperatures. On 17 November 2023, the [average global temperature briefly touched 2°C](https://climate.copernicus.eu/global-temperature-exceeds-2degc-above-pre-industrial-average-17-november) above the pre-industrial average for the first time. Warming is uneven: [land is heating up faster than the ocean](https://www.carbonbrief.org/guest-post-why-does-land-warm-up-faster-than-the-oceans), which absorbs most of its extra heat near the surface.

Ice and glaciers are melting so fast that a [November 2023 cryosphere report](https://iccinet.org/statecryo23/) warns that going past 2°C "will trigger irreversible loss to Earth's ice sheets, mountain glaciers and snow, sea ice, permafrost, and polar oceans," with disastrous consequences for millions of people. Sea levels rose 0.2 metres between 1901 and 2018. According to the [IPCC's 2023 synthesis report](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_SYR_SPM.pdf), they will keep rising well beyond 2100 whatever happens next, so the risk to coastal ecosystems, people and infrastructure keeps growing.

The original global pledge, agreed at COP21 in Paris in 2015, was to keep warming below 2°C. The focus moved to the stricter 1.5°C goal partly because island nations argued that 2°C amounted to a "death sentence": projections showed the resulting sea-level rise would put many of them underwater. 1.5°C is less a hard physical threshold than a sobering line scientists agreed on. As MIT research scientist Sergey Paltsev [puts it](https://news.mit.edu/2023/explained-climate-benchmark-rising-temperatures-0827), "there is nothing magical about the 1.5 number, other than that it is an agreed aspirational target. Keeping at 1.4 is better than 1.5, and 1.3 is better than 1.4, and so on." Impacts do not rise in a straight line with temperature, mainly because of tipping points and carbon cycle feedbacks (covered later on this page), and the risk of setting these off rises sharply above 1.5°C.

## Will Warming Stop If We Reach Net-Zero?

Since CO2 can stay in the atmosphere for centuries, it is natural to ask whether the world keeps heating even after emissions stop. As [Carbon Brief explains](https://www.carbonbrief.org/explainer-will-global-warming-stop-as-soon-as-net-zero-emissions-are-reached), it does not: warming is likely to more or less stop once CO2 emissions reach zero. In other words, humanity has real power to choose its climate future.

It helps to keep apart *stabilising* the concentration of CO2 already in the atmosphere and *reducing emissions* to zero or net-zero. If all emitting activity stopped altogether (cars, planes, factories, power plants), the concentration of CO2 in the atmosphere would actually start to fall as natural carbon sinks kept absorbing it, and further warming would stop. If emissions only levelled off, holding the concentration around today's roughly 420+ ppm while still emitting at around 30% of current levels, warming would continue, adding perhaps another 0.5°C over the coming centuries.

That is why the real target is **net-zero**: cutting emissions as close to zero as possible and balancing whatever is left with carbon removal, so that no extra CO2 is added overall. The international scientific consensus is that global net human-caused CO2 emissions need to fall by roughly 45% from 2010 levels by 2030, and reach net-zero around 2050, to avoid the worst damage. Reaching net-zero does not bring temperatures back down to pre-industrial levels. The warming built up along the way is hard to reverse without carbon removal on a large scale. So climate scientists keep coming back to the same message: cutting emissions today is worth more than the same cut years from now, because every year of delay locks in more warming. Other greenhouse gases and aerosols complicate the picture. Global temperatures level off once CO2 emissions hit zero, but the same is not necessarily true for other pollutants.

## Impacts Being Felt Today

The **Arctic** has been warming nearly four times faster than the planet as a whole since 1979, according to a [2022 study in Nature](http://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-022-00498-3). This is called Arctic amplification. Less snow and ice means less sunlight is reflected back to space, while a warmer, wetter atmosphere carries more heat towards the poles. In Siberia, current warming is [unprecedented over the past seven millennia](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-32629-x).

The **ocean** absorbs over 90% of the heat trapped by greenhouse gases. It warms more slowly than land, but its [heat uptake has sped up sharply since the 1990s](http://www.cnn.com/2023/08/04/world/ocean-heat-temperature-record-climate/index.html), nearly doubling through the 2010s. The ocean is also becoming more acidic, according to a [2021 UN World Meteorological Organization report](https://mcusercontent.com/e35fa2254c2a4394f75d43308/files/3d2f5b01-11a6-dee2-a42a-13ad5002dc33/1290_Statement_2021_en_1_.pdf). A [2022 review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/smll.202107407), though, suggests that some species once thought vulnerable, including crabs, octopuses and squid, may cope better than expected.

Extreme **heat and rainfall** are becoming more likely. A [2022 Nature study](http://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-022-01329-1) looked at four decades of observations. It projects that historically extreme rainfall events (above the 99th percentile, meaning the wettest 1% of days) will happen roughly 32±8% more often by the end of the century under a medium-emissions pathway, and 55±13% more often under high emissions. A separate 2022 Nature study found that heat extremes have become much more intense, with extreme events following one another more often.

**Glaciers and ice** are retreating faster, from the Himalayas to the Alps. Antarctica's outer glaciers are now retreating [twice as fast as they did across the whole twentieth century](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01855-6). In Greenland, meltwater is boring networks of holes deep into the ice sheet and weakening its internal structure, as glaciologist Alun Hubbard [describes](https://theconversation.com/meltwater-is-hydro-fracking-greenlands-ice-sheet-through-millions-of-hairline-cracks-destabilizing-its-internal-structure-207468). Together, the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets hold enough ice to raise global sea levels by more than 65 metres, and they have been losing mass faster and faster since the 1990s. Mountain regions where snow turns to rain face greater risk of floods and landslides, which could affect a quarter of the world's population. Rainfall at high altitudes is projected to rise by around 15% for every 1°C of warming.

At the poles, **sea ice** keeps hitting new lows. Arctic sea ice has shrunk markedly, and a [2023 Nature study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38511-8) projects that the Arctic could see an [ice-free September within one to two decades](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2023/jun/06/too-late-now-to-save-arctic-summer-ice-climate-scientists-find). Antarctica is a separate and more surprising story. After decades of relative stability, Antarctic sea ice started falling sharply in 2016, and February 2023 set a new record low, the third in seven years. Natural variability does not explain the shift. The speed and scale of the decline has taken scientists by surprise and started a serious debate about whether a tipping point has already been crossed there (more on Antarctica's role below).

**Sea-level rise** is speeding up. [NASA satellite data](https://sealevel.nasa.gov/news/259/nasa-uses-30-year-satellite-record-to-track-and-project-rising-seas/) shows that sea levels rose 0.27cm from 2021 to 2022 alone. That is like adding a million Olympic swimming pools of water to the ocean every day for a year. It is part of an average rise of 9.1cm over the last three decades, during which the yearly rate of rise has roughly doubled. Heat takes centuries to work its way through the deep ocean, and ice sheets take hundreds to thousands of years to settle into a new balance. So some sea-level rise is now locked in whatever happens next, as physicist Jonathan Bamber [explained to the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2023/jun/26/its-absolutely-guaranteed-the-best-and-worst-case-scenarios-for-sea-level-rise). Coastal countries from Bangladesh to the Netherlands, and megacities including Lagos, Mumbai, Shanghai, London and New York, all face serious impacts. Even if warming is held to 1.5°C, the IPCC's synthesis report still projects 28-55cm of sea-level rise by 2100.

**Ocean currents** may be slowing too. The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) carries warm water from the equator to the North Atlantic. There it cools, becomes denser and sinks, then flows south again. More fresh water from melting Greenland ice and heavier rainfall dilutes that water, making it less dense and slowing the circulation. Some studies suggest [it is at its weakest in over a millennium](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2021/feb/25/atlantic-ocean-circulation-at-weakest-in-a-millennium-say-scientists), though [other research questions](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-022-01342-4) how confidently this can be put down to climate change. The IPCC's AR6 synthesis report is careful here. It has medium confidence that the AMOC will not collapse abruptly before 2100. But if it did, it would very likely cause sudden shifts in regional weather and large impacts on ecosystems and human activity, possibly including drying in Europe and disrupted monsoons. Oceanographer Stefan Rahmstorf has argued that the risk this century is "far greater" than 10%, and "even rather worrying for the next few decades." See [AMOC Collapse and Carbon](amoc-collapse-carbon.md) for more on this risk.

## The Remaining Carbon Budget

Scientists can estimate fairly precisely how much more CO2 can be emitted before the world passes 1.5°C. The IPCC's 2021 assessment put this **carbon budget** at 400GtCO2 for a 67% chance of staying under 1.5°C, roughly ten years at the emission rates of the time (around 40GtCO2 a year). More recent work, counting other greenhouse gases more precisely, suggests the real budget may be smaller, closer to 300Gt. A [2023 update](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01848-5) found that for just a 50% chance of staying under 1.5°C, the budget is around 250GtCO2, about six years of current emissions, with a lot of uncertainty around that estimate.

What actually happens to emissions depends on how populations, economies and efforts to decarbonise develop. Climate scientists, economists and energy modellers have worked together on five scenarios, called the [Shared Socioeconomic Pathways](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959378016300681) (SSPs). Each one describes a plausible mix of population, economic and technological change over the coming century. They feed into Integrated Assessment Models, which underpin both the IPCC's 2023 Synthesis Report and the science behind the Paris Agreement.

Only the most ambitious SSPs keep warming to 1.5°C by 2100, and they need deep, sustained emissions cuts starting now. A [UNEP report](http://wedocs.unep.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.11822/43923/EGR2023_ESEN.pdf) puts the odds of actually achieving this at just 14%. The more realistic path being discussed is "1.5°C with overshoot": going above 1.5°C for a while, then bringing temperatures back down by removing carbon dioxide on a large scale. This path is technically demanding, and whether it is possible is disputed. On current policies, the [Climate Action Tracker](https://climateactiontracker.org/global/temperatures/) projects that the world is heading for 2.6-2.9°C of warming, although ongoing policy action is also pulling the trend in a better direction.

## Tipping Points and Carbon Cycle Feedbacks

A **tipping point** is a threshold beyond which a small extra change can push a system into a very different state, often irreversible and usually catastrophic. Tipping points are less a precisely measurable line than a way of thinking about sources of severe, uncertain risk in the climate system. [Carbon Brief has catalogued nine of them](https://www.carbonbrief.org/explainer-nine-tipping-points-that-could-be-triggered-by-climate-change/). The IPCC's AR6 report notes that so far, Earth's systems have responded roughly in proportion to recent warming. That may not hold once a tipping point is crossed: past sudden reorganisations, such as at the end of ice ages, show these systems can and do shift quickly. A [late-2023 assessment](https://global-tipping-points.org/summary-report/key-messages/) found that five of the identified tipping points are already at risk of being crossed at current warming levels, with three more becoming threats as the world nears 1.5°C. This could disrupt huge areas of farmland, along with governments and economies.

Two systems show what is at stake.

The **Antarctic ice sheet** holds nearly 70% of the planet's fresh water across about 14 million km², making it the largest single mass of ice on Earth. If it melted completely, global sea levels would rise by roughly 58 metres. Today it already adds around 0.4mm a year to sea-level rise, and that rate is growing. Its bright surface reflects up to 80% of incoming sunlight (the albedo effect). As ice melts and exposes darker ocean and rock, more heat is absorbed, which drives further warming in a self-reinforcing loop. Antarctic waters also drive the global thermohaline circulation, the "ocean conveyor belt". The Southern Ocean takes up around 40% of all the CO2 the ocean absorbs, making it one of the planet's most important carbon sinks. As melting ice dilutes that water with fresh water, it risks disrupting both ocean circulation and the storage of carbon in the deep ocean, with knock-on effects for species such as Antarctic krill that depend on seasonal sea ice. For decades Antarctic sea ice held roughly steady despite global warming. Then came a sharp decline from 2016, still not fully explained, ending in a record low in February 2023, the third in seven years. Scientists are now actively debating whether the region has already crossed a tipping point.

**Warm-water coral reefs** cover only about a tenth of a percent of the ocean's surface, around 350,000km², yet they support a third of all marine species. They depend on a close partnership with algae called zooxanthellae, which also makes them very sensitive to even small temperature changes. Scientists have concluded that a widespread dieback of warm-water corals, under way since the global bleaching event that began in 2023, is now effectively irreversible (Global Tipping Points Report, October 2025). They describe it as the first major global climate tipping point to have actually been triggered. It has affected more than 80% of the world's coral reefs, compared with 8% in a major bleaching event in 1998. Central academic estimates put the heat limit for warm-water corals at around 1.2°C of warming above pre-industrial levels, with an upper threshold of 1.5°C. The long-term global average reached roughly 1.3°C by 2024, and a temperature surge that year briefly pushed the world past 1.5°C for the first time, taking coral reefs clearly beyond their estimated tipping point. Reefs are estimated to be worth close to $10 trillion to the global economy.

**Carbon cycle feedbacks** are closely tied to tipping-point risk. The carbon cycle is the constant exchange of carbon between the atmosphere, land, ocean and living things, and a feedback is a change in those exchanges caused by warming itself. Feedbacks can be negative, such as the CO2 fertilisation effect, where more CO2 in the air boosts plant growth and the plants then absorb more CO2. Or they can be positive, such as thawing permafrost releasing methane, which speeds up warming. Positive feedbacks raise the odds of crossing a tipping point, and crossing one can in turn set off further feedbacks. As climate scientist Andrew Dessler [has written](https://www.theclimatebrink.com/p/a-truly-worst-case-climate-scenario), "the exact threshold at which carbon-cycle feedbacks become significant remains uncertain and we really don't know how close we are to reaching it."

Human-made **aerosols**, tiny sulphur or nitrogen particles that reflect sunlight back to space, add one more layer of uncertainty. They cool the planet, though by how much is uncertain, and they only last a short time in the atmosphere. So if the activities that produce them (mostly burning fossil fuels) stop, the cooling disappears quickly. That could unmask extra warming that fossil fuels had been holding back by accident.
