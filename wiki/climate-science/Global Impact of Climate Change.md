# Global Impact of Climate Change: 1.5°C, Tipping Points and What's Already Happening

The permafrost across Siberia and other northern regions is thawing as the climate warms, releasing water, destabilising the ground beneath entire towns, and, more importantly for the planet, releasing greenhouse gases that had been locked away for millennia. It's a useful entry point into this page's subject: how much warming the world can afford before impacts stop scaling predictably, what's already changing today, and why individual thresholds and tipping points matter so much for how bad this gets.

## Why 1.5°C Matters

The planet is currently about 1.1-1.3°C warmer than pre-industrial levels, and the record-breaking keeps coming: the [World Meteorological Organization](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/2023-shatters-climate-records-major-impacts) confirmed 2023 as virtually certain to be the warmest year on record, at 1.4°C above pre-industrial temperatures, and on 17 November 2023 the [average global temperature briefly touched 2°C](https://climate.copernicus.eu/global-temperature-exceeds-2degc-above-pre-industrial-average-17-november) above the pre-industrial average for the first time. Warming isn't even: [land is heating up faster than the ocean](https://www.carbonbrief.org/guest-post-why-does-land-warm-up-faster-than-the-oceans), which absorbs most of its extra heat near the surface.

Ice and glaciers are melting fast enough that a [November 2023 cryosphere report](https://iccinet.org/statecryo23/) warns that continuing past 2°C "will trigger irreversible loss to Earth's ice sheets, mountain glaciers and snow, sea ice, permafrost, and polar oceans," with disastrous consequences for millions of people. Sea levels rose 0.2 metres between 1901 and 2018 and, per the [IPCC's 2023 synthesis report](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_SYR_SPM.pdf), will keep rising well beyond 2100 regardless of what happens next, meaning risk to coastal ecosystems, people and infrastructure keeps growing.

The original global pledge, agreed at COP21 in Paris in 2015, was to keep warming below 2°C. Momentum shifted toward the stricter 1.5°C goal partly because island nations argued 2°C amounted to a "death sentence," given projections that the resulting sea-level rise would put many of them underwater. 1.5°C isn't a hard physical threshold so much as a sobering line scientists settled on: as MIT research scientist Sergey Paltsev [puts it](https://news.mit.edu/2023/explained-climate-benchmark-rising-temperatures-0827), "there is nothing magical about the 1.5 number, other than that it is an agreed aspirational target. Keeping at 1.4 is better than 1.5, and 1.3 is better than 1.4, and so on." Impacts don't scale in a straight line with temperature, largely because of tipping points and carbon cycle feedbacks, covered later on this page, and the risk of triggering them rises sharply above 1.5°C.

## Will Warming Stop If We Reach Net-Zero?

Given that CO2 can persist in the atmosphere for centuries, a natural question is whether the world keeps heating even after emissions stop. The answer, as [Carbon Brief explains](https://www.carbonbrief.org/explainer-will-global-warming-stop-as-soon-as-net-zero-emissions-are-reached), is no: warming is likely to more or less stop once CO2 emissions reach zero, meaning humanity effectively has the power to choose its climate future.

It helps to separate two different things: *stabilising* the concentration of CO2 already in the atmosphere, versus *reducing emissions* to zero or net-zero. If all emitting activity, cars, planes, factories, power plants, stopped altogether, atmospheric CO2 concentration would actually start falling as natural carbon sinks kept absorbing it, and further warming would stop. If instead emissions merely flatten, holding concentration around today's roughly 420+ ppm while still emitting at around 30% of current levels, warming would continue, adding perhaps another 0.5°C over the coming centuries.

This is why the actual target is **net-zero**: cutting emissions as close to zero as feasible and offsetting whatever remains through carbon removal, so no net CO2 is added. International scientific consensus holds that global net human-caused CO2 emissions need to fall roughly 45% from 2010 levels by 2030, reaching net-zero around 2050, to avoid the worst damage. Crucially, reaching net-zero does not bring temperatures back down to pre-industrial levels; the warming already locked in in the meantime is difficult to reverse without large-scale carbon removal. Because of this, the core message climate scientists keep returning to is that **cutting emissions today is more valuable than an equivalent cut years from now**, since every year of delay locks in additional warming. Non-CO2 greenhouse gases and aerosols complicate the picture further, since global temperatures stabilise once CO2 emissions hit zero, but the same isn't necessarily true for other pollutants.

## Impacts Being Felt Today

**The Arctic is warming nearly four times faster than the planet as a whole** since 1979, a phenomenon called Arctic amplification, [according to a 2022 study in Nature](http://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-022-00498-3). Less snow and ice means less sunlight reflected back to space, while a warmer, moisture-laden atmosphere transports more heat toward the poles. In Siberia specifically, current warming is [unprecedented over the past seven millennia](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-32629-x).

**The ocean absorbs over 90% of the heat trapped by greenhouse gases** and, while it warms more slowly than land, that [heat uptake has accelerated dramatically since the 1990s](http://www.cnn.com/2023/08/04/world/ocean-heat-temperature-record-climate/index.html), nearly doubling through the 2010s. The ocean is also becoming more acidic, per a [2021 UN World Meteorological Organization report](https://mcusercontent.com/e35fa2254c2a4394f75d43308/files/3d2f5b01-11a6-dee2-a42a-13ad5002dc33/1290_Statement_2021_en_1_.pdf), though a [2022 review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/smll.202107407) suggests some species once thought vulnerable, including crabs, octopuses and squid, may be more resilient than expected.

**Heat and rainfall extremes are becoming more likely.** A [2022 Nature study](http://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-022-01329-1) analysing four decades of observations projects historically extreme precipitation events (above the 99th percentile) will occur roughly 32±8% more often by end of century under a medium-emissions pathway, and 55±13% more often under high emissions. A separate 2022 Nature study found the magnitude of heat extremes has significantly increased, with successive extreme events becoming more common.

**Glaciers and ice are retreating faster**, from the Himalayas to the Alps. Antarctica's peripheral glaciers are now retreating [twice as fast as they did across the whole twentieth century](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01855-6). In Greenland, meltwater is boring extensive networks of holes deep into the ice sheet, destabilising its internal structure, as glaciologist Alun Hubbard [describes](https://theconversation.com/meltwater-is-hydro-fracking-greenlands-ice-sheet-through-millions-of-hairline-cracks-destabilizing-its-internal-structure-207468): the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets together lock up more than 65 metres of equivalent global sea-level rise, and their mass loss has been accelerating since the 1990s. Mountain regions that shift from snowfall to rainfall face greater flood and landslide risk, potentially affecting a quarter of the world's population, with rainfall at high elevations projected to rise around 15% for every 1°C of warming.

**At the poles, sea ice keeps hitting new lows.** Arctic sea ice has shrunk markedly, and a [2023 Nature study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-38511-8) projects the Arctic could see an [ice-free September within one to two decades](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2023/jun/06/too-late-now-to-save-arctic-summer-ice-climate-scientists-find). Antarctica is a separate and more surprising story: after decades of relative stability, Antarctic sea ice extent fell sharply starting in 2016, and February 2023 set a new record minimum, the third in seven years. Natural variability doesn't explain the shift, and the speed and scale of the decline has taken scientists by surprise, prompting serious debate over whether a tipping point has already been crossed there (more on Antarctica's role below).

**Sea-level rise is accelerating.** [NASA satellite data](https://sealevel.nasa.gov/news/259/nasa-uses-30-year-satellite-record-to-track-and-project-rising-seas/) shows sea levels rose 0.27cm from 2021 to 2022 alone, equivalent to adding a million Olympic swimming pools of water to the ocean every day for a year, part of an average rise of 9.1cm over the last three decades, with the annual rate of rise roughly doubling over that period. Because heat takes centuries to work through the deep ocean and ice sheets take hundreds to thousands of years to reach a new equilibrium, some sea-level rise is now locked in regardless of what happens next, as physicist Jonathan Bamber [explained to the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2023/jun/26/its-absolutely-guaranteed-the-best-and-worst-case-scenarios-for-sea-level-rise). Coastal countries from Bangladesh to the Netherlands, and megacities including Lagos, Mumbai, Shanghai, London and New York, all face serious impacts. Even if warming is held to 1.5°C, the IPCC's synthesis report still projects 28-55cm of sea-level rise by 2100.

**Ocean currents may be slowing too.** The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) carries warm water from the equator to the North Atlantic, where it cools, becomes denser and sinks before flowing south again. Rising freshwater input from melting Greenland ice and increased rainfall dilutes that water, reducing its density and slowing the circulation; some studies suggest [it is at its weakest in over a millennium](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2021/feb/25/atlantic-ocean-circulation-at-weakest-in-a-millennium-say-scientists), though [other research questions](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-022-01342-4) how confidently this can be attributed to climate change. The IPCC's AR6 synthesis report puts it carefully: there is medium confidence AMOC will not collapse abruptly before 2100, but if it did, it would very likely cause abrupt shifts in regional weather and large impacts on ecosystems and human activity, potentially including drying in Europe and disrupted monsoon patterns. Oceanographer Stefan Rahmstorf has argued the risk this century is "far greater" than 10%, and "even rather worrying for the next few decades." See [AMOC Collapse and Carbon](amoc-collapse-carbon.md) for more detail on this specific risk.

## The Remaining Carbon Budget

Scientists can estimate fairly precisely how much more CO2 can be emitted before crossing 1.5°C. The IPCC's 2021 assessment put the budget at 400GtCO2 for a 67% chance of staying under 1.5°C, roughly eight years at current emission rates. More recent work accounting more precisely for non-CO2 gases suggests the true budget may be smaller, closer to 300Gt, and a [2023 update](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01848-5) found that for just a 50% chance of staying under 1.5°C, the budget is around 250GtCO2, about six years of current emissions, with substantial uncertainty attached to that estimate.

What actually happens to emissions depends on how populations, economies and decarbonisation efforts evolve. Climate scientists, economists and energy modellers have collaborated on five scenarios, known as the [Shared Socioeconomic Pathways](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959378016300681) (SSPs), capturing plausible combinations of demographic, economic and technological change over the coming century. These feed into Integrated Assessment Models, which underpin both the IPCC's 2023 Synthesis Report and the scientific case behind the Paris Agreement.

Only the most ambitious SSPs keep warming to 1.5°C by 2100, and they require deep, sustained emissions cuts starting now; a [UNEP report](http://wedocs.unep.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.11822/43923/EGR2023_ESEN.pdf) puts the odds of actually achieving this at just 14%. The more realistic path under discussion is "1.5°C with overshoot": temporarily exceeding 1.5°C, then bringing temperatures back down through large-scale carbon dioxide removal, though this path remains technically demanding and its feasibility is contested. On current policy, the [Climate Action Tracker](https://climateactiontracker.org/global/temperatures/) projects the world is heading for 2.6-2.9°C of warming, though the trend is also being pulled in a better direction by ongoing policy action.

## Tipping Points and Carbon Cycle Feedbacks

A **tipping point** is a threshold beyond which a small additional change can push a system into a fundamentally different, often irreversible and typically catastrophic state. Tipping points aren't a precisely measurable scientific line so much as a conceptual framework for understanding sources of severe, uncertain risk in the climate system. [Carbon Brief has catalogued nine such tipping points](https://www.carbonbrief.org/explainer-nine-tipping-points-that-could-be-triggered-by-climate-change/). So far, the IPCC's AR6 report notes, Earth's systems have responded roughly in proportion to the rate of recent warming, but that may not hold if a tipping point is crossed, since past abrupt reorganisations (such as at the end of ice ages) show these systems can and do shift suddenly. A [late-2023 assessment](https://global-tipping-points.org/summary-report/key-messages/) found five of the identified tipping points are already at risk of being crossed at current warming levels, with three more becoming threats as the world approaches 1.5°C, potentially disrupting huge areas of cropland along with governments and economic systems.

Two systems illustrate what's at stake:

**The Antarctic ice sheet** holds nearly 70% of the planet's freshwater across about 14 million km², the largest single mass of ice on Earth. If it fully melted, it would raise global sea levels by roughly 58 metres; today it already contributes around 0.4mm a year to sea-level rise, and that rate is accelerating. Its bright surface reflects up to 80% of incoming solar radiation (the albedo effect); as ice melts and exposes darker ocean and rock, more heat is absorbed, reinforcing further warming in a positive feedback loop. Antarctic waters also drive the global thermohaline circulation, the "ocean conveyor belt," and the Southern Ocean absorbs around 40% of the ocean's total CO2 uptake, making it one of the planet's most important carbon sinks; as melting ice dilutes that water with fresh water, it risks disrupting both ocean circulation and deep-water carbon storage, with knock-on effects for species like Antarctic krill that depend on seasonal sea ice. For decades Antarctic sea ice held roughly steady despite global warming, until a sharp and still not fully explained decline beginning in 2016, culminating in a record low in February 2023, the third in seven years, fuelling active debate over whether the region has already crossed a tipping point.

**Warm-water coral reefs** cover only about a tenth of a percent of the ocean's surface, around 350,000km², yet support a third of all marine species, sustained by a close symbiotic relationship with algae called zooxanthellae, which also makes them highly sensitive to even small temperature changes. Scientists have concluded that a widespread dieback of warm-water corals over the past two years is now effectively irreversible, describing it as the first major global climate tipping point to have actually been triggered, affecting more than 80% of the world's coral reefs, up from a major bleaching event in 1998 that affected 8%. Central academic estimates put the thermal limit for warm-water corals at around 1.2°C of warming above pre-industrial levels, with an upper threshold of 1.5°C; the long-term global average reached roughly 1.3°C by 2024, and a temperature surge that year briefly pushed the world past the 1.5°C mark for the first time, placing coral reefs squarely past their estimated tipping point. Economically, reefs are estimated to be worth close to $10 trillion to the global economy.

**Carbon cycle feedbacks** connect directly to tipping-point risk. The carbon cycle describes the constant exchange of carbon between atmosphere, land, ocean and living organisms; a feedback is a change in those exchanges triggered by warming itself. Feedbacks can be negative, such as the CO2 fertilisation effect, where more atmospheric CO2 boosts plant growth, which then absorbs more CO2, or positive, such as permafrost thaw releasing methane, which accelerates further warming. Positive feedbacks raise the odds of crossing a tipping point, and crossing one can in turn trigger further feedbacks. As climate scientist Andrew Dessler [has written](https://www.theclimatebrink.com/p/a-truly-worst-case-climate-scenario), "the exact threshold at which carbon-cycle feedbacks become significant remains uncertain and we really don't know how close we are to reaching it."

Human-emitted aerosols, tiny sulfur or nitrogen particles that reflect sunlight back to space, add one more layer of uncertainty: they have a real cooling effect on the planet, though its exact size is uncertain, and a short atmospheric lifetime, meaning that if aerosol-producing activity (largely fossil fuel combustion) stops, that cooling effect disappears quickly, potentially unmasking additional warming that fossil fuels had inadvertently been suppressing.
