# Fossil Fuel Banking

## What is it

Every year since the Paris Agreement, the world's largest banks have been tracked by the **Banking on Climate Chaos** report, a coalition of environmental groups. The picture is consistently alarming: rather than cutting fossil fuel lending in line with climate commitments, the world's 65 largest banks have collectively increased their financing year after year.

> In 2025, those banks committed **$906 billion** to fossil fuel companies. That is an increase of $64 billion, nearly 8%, on 2024. Since 2015, the total has reached **$8.7 trillion** in new fossil fuel financing, even as the same institutions made voluntary pledges to align their lending with net zero by 2050.

## Why it matters

Banks do not drill for oil or burn coal themselves, but the money they lend and the bonds they underwrite make new fossil fuel projects viable. Without access to cheap capital, expansion of coal mines, gas fields, and LNG terminals would stall. The scale of fossil fuel financing is therefore a direct measure of how seriously the financial system is treating the climate emergency.

The 2025 report found that the world's largest banks are "making decisions incompatible with international agreements to restrain rising global temperatures." The UN's own Net-Zero Banking Alliance, designed to change this, effectively collapsed in 2025 after a wave of departures, many driven by political pressure in the United States under the Trump administration.

## Who is lending what

JPMorgan Chase is the world's leading fossil fuel financier, committing $58 billion in 2025, up 13% from 2024. The top five lenders are all American or Japanese: JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, MUFG, Mizuho Financial, and Citigroup. Barclays is the highest-placed British bank, at eighth position.

A "dirty dozen" of the largest institutions account for 40% of all fossil fuel funding globally. Almost all financing originates from six jurisdictions: the United States, Canada, Japan, China, the United Kingdom, and the European Union.

Not all banks are moving in the same direction. Twenty-six of the 65 tracked banks reduced their fossil fuel financing in 2025. European banks led the reductions: BNP Paribas, UBS, and La Caixa cut their fossil fuel lending the most.

## Where the money goes

In 2025, $508 billion was pledged specifically for the expansion of existing fossil fuel sites, a 27% increase on 2024. The three largest single recipients of fossil fuel lending were US-based operators: Venture Global (LNG export), Enbridge (pipelines), and Energy Transfer (midstream gas). Big oil's record profits, boosted in 2025 by surging energy prices following US and Israeli military action against Iran, have made fossil fuel companies attractive lending clients.

## May 2026: French banks fund EPH's gas expansion

A specific example of how the pattern plays out in practice. On 21 May 2026, all four French banking majors — **BNP Paribas, Crédit Agricole, Société Générale, and BPCE** — participated in a **€2.2 billion revolving credit facility (RCF)** to **Energetický a průmyslový holding (EPH)**, a Czech coal-and-gas group controlled by billionaire Daniel Křetínský. EPH's parent EP Group is **Europe's largest coal producer** and second-largest coal-power operator. EPH itself operates **11.1 GW of fossil gas across 8 European countries and plans to add 5.5 GW more**. It has just formed a joint venture, TTEP, with TotalEnergies.

Two features of the deal are notable. First, EPH has moved coal assets to a new subsidiary, **EP Energy Transition (EPETr)** — also owned by Křetínský — to claim a "coal exit" by 2030 without actually closing plants. Reclaim Finance calls this "a sleight of hand." Second, one participating bank characterised the RCF as a **"green loan"** under the LMA Green Loan Principles; the transaction was later removed from the database provider's records "for confidentiality reasons." S&P's own second-party opinion notes EPH's Green Finance Framework "includes significant investments in fossil fuel-based assets and infrastructure."

BNP Paribas is the same bank that led European reductions in fossil fuel financing in 2025. The EPH RCF illustrates that reductions in the aggregated headline number can coexist with major new deals for individual polluters. ## Accountability parallels beyond climate

The same pattern of banks refusing to act on findings from voluntary accountability mechanisms also plays out outside climate. In **August 2026**, the Swiss National Contact Point for Responsible Business Conduct issued a Follow-up Statement finding that **UBS had failed to act on two of three recommendations** it had received in a private-prisons complaint filed by Worth Rises and BankTrack. UBS had also refused to enter NCP mediation — a first among European commercial banks. The mechanism (OECD Guidelines / NCP process) and the underlying issue (passive-portfolio due diligence) directly parallel the climate accountability gap: voluntary rules with no enforcement produce voluntary compliance. See [UBS private prisons source summary](../sources/ubs-private-prisons-swiss-ncp-2026.md).

## The collapse of voluntary commitments

Many banks had previously announced net zero lending targets and joined voluntary initiatives. The dissolution of the Net-Zero Banking Alliance in 2025 is a sign that voluntary commitments have not delivered change at the required scale. As one of the report's editors put it: "The era of voluntary commitments has not worked at the scale that we need and so this points to a much more active role for financial regulators, legislators and policymakers, especially in those big six financial centres."

## Key facts

- $906bn committed by 65 largest banks to fossil fuels in 2025, up 8% on 2024.
- $8.7 trillion total since the Paris Agreement (2015–2025).
- JPMorgan Chase: $58bn in 2025, up 13% from 2024.
- $508bn specifically for fossil fuel site expansion in 2025, up 27%.
- "Dirty dozen" banks: 40% of all industry funding.
- 26 of 65 banks reduced fossil fuel financing; BNP Paribas led reductions.
- Net-Zero Banking Alliance disbanded 2025 after mass departures.
- **May 2026:** €2.2bn RCF to Czech coal-and-gas group EPH from all four French majors, plus ING, Lloyds, CaixaBank — labelled a "green loan" under LMA principles.
