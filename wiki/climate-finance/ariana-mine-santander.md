# Ariana Mine and Santander: When a $100m Loan Meets Lima's Water Supply

## What is it

Ariana is a planned underground **copper and zinc mine** in the high Andes of central Peru, in the Marcapomacocha district of Junín region. The site sits directly on top of the water system that supplies around 60% of the dry-season drinking water for Lima and Callao, a metropolitan area of more than 10 million people. The Peruvian company Alpayana bought the project in 2025 and is trying to bring it into operation. In August 2025, Banco Santander lent Alpayana US$100 million through a refinancing facility, which campaigners say makes construction realistic. The mine has not started operating yet, largely because of a constitutional case brought by Lima residents that has been running for years.

## Why it matters

Ariana is a small, concrete example of a much bigger question: **should a European bank finance a mine that threatens the drinking water of 10 million people?** Peru's water utility, SEDAPAL, has flagged four ways the mine could cause harm. It could reduce groundwater flows. It could contaminate water with heavy metals. Its vibrations could damage a decades-old trans-Andean water tunnel that runs a hundred metres from the planned tailings dam (the dam that holds the mine's liquid waste). And that dam could itself fail in an earthquake. Peru has already recorded 18 tailings dam ruptures since 1952. Santander has an [Environmental, Social and Climate Change Risk Management Policy](https://www.banktrack.org/download/environmental_social_climate_change_risk_management_policy) on paper. Ariana will show whether that policy actually holds the bank to anything in practice.

## How the project got here

The mine's Environmental Impact Study (EIA) was approved in 2016, and the mine was meant to open in 2019. That year a group of Lima citizens filed a constitutional appeal, and the project has been legally frozen ever since. In 2025, after six years of proceedings, the **Constitutional Chamber of Lima** ruled that Ariana does pose "a certain and imminent threat" to the fundamental right to drinking water and a suitable environment. Instead of overturning the EIA, though, the court ordered a Complementary Environmental Impact Study. The same year, Alpayana bought the project from Southern Peaks for US$200 million, advised by LXG Capital, a clear sign that it plans to press ahead. A few months later, Santander stepped in with the US$100 million loan.

## The water system at stake

The **Marcapomacocha water system** is more than a set of lakes. It combines natural high-altitude wetlands, known locally as *bofedales* (Andean peat bogs), with lagoons, canals and reservoirs. It also includes the Cuevas-Milloc Trans-Andean Tunnel, built in the 1960s to carry water from the eastern slopes of the Andes to the dry Pacific coast where Lima sits. Lima is one of the largest desert cities in the world, and this system is its lifeline in the dry season. The proposed tailings dam would sit just 100 metres from the tunnel, in an active earthquake zone. If either failed, the backup water supply for a metropolitan region of 10+ million people would be limited at best.

## The campaign and the transition-minerals defence

Copper and zinc appear on many "critical minerals" lists for the [energy transition](../solutions/energy-transition/santa-marta-fossil-fuel-conference-2026.md). Copper goes into electricity grids, wiring, EVs and heat pumps, and zinc into batteries and galvanising. Alpayana's supporters have presented Ariana in those terms. The Ekō petition aimed at Santander's Executive Chairman, **Ana Botín**, rejects that argument head on: it points out that people using the "metals for green growth" line rarely offer up their own drinking water in exchange. The campaign asks Santander to use its influence as lender to persuade Alpayana to drop the project. It cites cases where similar public pressure pushed banks away from the [Carmichael coal mine in Australia](https://www.stopadani.com/) and the Pebble Mine in Alaska.

## Key facts

- Ariana would sit inside the water system that supplies **around 60% of Lima's dry-season drinking water**
- Peru's water utility SEDAPAL flagged four ways the mine could cause harm: reduced groundwater flows, heavy metals contamination, vibration damage to the Trans-Andean Tunnel, and possible collapse of the tailings dam just 100 m from the tunnel
- Peru has had **18 tailings dam ruptures since 1952**, mostly caused by earthquakes
- Constitutional Chamber of Lima (2025): the project is "a certain and imminent threat" to the right to drinking water, and a Complementary EIA is required
- Alpayana bought the project in **March 2025 for US$200 million**, advised by LXG Capital
- Banco Santander provided a **US$100 million refinancing facility** to Alpayana in **August 2025**
- No construction has started yet, so this is the window in which public and financial pressure can still make a difference
