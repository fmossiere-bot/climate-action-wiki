## Is Finance the Problem, the Solution, or Both?

When people talk about tackling climate change, the conversation usually turns to technology, policy or personal behaviour. Another part of the story gets far less attention: **money**. Where it comes from, where it goes, and who decides.

> Finance is not neutral. The banking system, investment funds, government budgets and private capital markets all play a direct part in speeding up or slowing down climate change. The same institutions that have the tools to pay for a clean energy transition have also spent decades financing the fossil fuel industry that caused the problem.

This article looks at that tension. It covers why finance matters for the climate, what is happening with public and private climate investment, where the gaps and risks are, and what you can do with your own money.

---

## Why Does Finance Matter for Climate?

Tackling climate change at the scale and speed needed is very expensive.

> According to McKinsey, **reaching net zero by 2050 will require around 9.2 trillion dollars every year** between now and then.

For a sense of where that money would go, the IPCC estimates that staying below 1.5 degrees of warming needs roughly:
- 1 to 2 trillion dollars per year to clean up the power sector
- 1.8 trillion dollars per year in buildings
- 1.7 trillion dollars per year in industry
- At least 2.7 trillion dollars per year in transport between 2015 and 2035
- Around 300 to 350 billion dollars per year in land use and agriculture

These figures leave out adaptation, the spending needed to help communities cope with climate impacts that are already locked in.

No government can pay for this alone, so private finance has to be part of it. Finance is also a lever that can move quickly, even when politics is slow. And financial institutions have never been bystanders. They helped shape the energy system we have today, often by sending money to its most carbon-heavy parts.

There is a strong economic case for climate investment, too. The World Bank estimates that investing in resilient infrastructure in developing countries could deliver 4.2 trillion dollars in benefits over the lifetime of that infrastructure.

> Every dollar invested in resilience brings, on average, four dollars in benefits. A shift to low-carbon economies could also create over 65 million net new jobs worldwide by 2030.

## The Current State of Climate Finance

### Public and private finance are both growing, but not fast enough

Climate finance comes from two main sources:
- Public finance is money from governments and public institutions such as development banks.
- Private finance comes from businesses, investment funds and individuals.

According to the Climate Policy Initiative, **both grew by around 7% a year** in the decade from 2011 to 2020. Today they are roughly equal in size. Public money often goes first, taking on the early risk, and private money follows. In blended finance, public money is used to lower the risk of an investment enough for private investors to come in.

Even with this growth, there is still a large funding gap. Current investment falls well short of what a 1.5 degree pathway needs.

### Fossil fuel finance is still very large

The harder fact is that while climate finance grows, fossil fuel finance is not shrinking fast enough. In the five years after the Paris Agreement (2016 to 2020), the world's 60 largest banks provided 3.8 trillion dollars to fossil fuel companies, according to the 2021 Banking on Climate Chaos report. The 2026 edition puts the total since 2015 at 8.7 trillion dollars, from the 65 largest banks. In 2020 and 2021 alone, the 30 largest financial institutions provided more than 740 billion dollars to the fossil fuel industry. Of those 30 institutions, 29 had publicly committed to net zero.

Governments play a part as well. The International Monetary Fund calculated that global fossil fuel subsidies reached 5.9 trillion dollars in 2020, nearly 7% of global GDP. That figure includes direct subsidies and the wider costs, such as pollution and health damage, that are not reflected in the price of fossil fuels.

There is some progress. At least 53 countries reformed fossil fuel subsidies between 2015 and 2020. But change is slow compared with the size of the problem.

## Public Finance: Key Instruments and Examples

### Government spending and green investment

Governments are putting more public money into the climate transition. In the United States, the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 committed 370 billion dollars over ten years to clean energy and climate solutions. It came alongside a 1.2 trillion dollar infrastructure deal with sizeable climate components. A national green bank was also set up to direct 27 billion dollars to clean energy projects.


### Debt-for-climate swaps

One inventive tool spreading among developing countries is the **debt-for-climate swap**. Many low-income countries carry very high debts, with yearly repayments sometimes eating up 30% or more of their total export earnings. That leaves little room to invest in cutting emissions or adapting to climate impacts.

In a swap, a country agrees to spend money it would have paid on foreign debt on climate projects at home instead. Belize, for example, reduced its debt in exchange for protecting 30% of its marine areas and funding marine conservation for twenty years. Costa Rica used similar deals to plant over 60,000 trees and reverse deforestation. In principle the same model could pay for renewable energy, flood defences or other climate infrastructure.

### The Green Climate Fund

The Green Climate Fund was set up under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change. It is the world's largest fund dedicated to helping developing countries cut emissions and adapt to climate impacts. It was established in 2010 and approved its first projects in 2015. It has funded more than 100 projects since then, but it remains underfunded compared with what is needed.

### Criticisms of aid-based climate finance

Not every assessment of public climate finance is positive. Oxfam's analysis shows that the real value of climate finance flowing from wealthy to developing countries is much lower than donors report. Help with adaptation has been especially thin. And instead of coming on top of existing development aid, most climate finance seems to be replacing it.

The money also often goes to the wrong places. Some of the most useful measures, such as cooling systems in tropical regions, receive a tiny share of total climate finance, even though cooling could account for 13% of global emissions by 2030.

## Private Finance: ESG, Green Bonds, and Venture Capital

### ESG investing

**ESG** stands for Environmental, Social and Governance. ESG investing looks at a company's environmental and social record alongside its financial returns, and it has grown enormously over the past two decades. In 2021 Bloomberg Intelligence projected that ESG assets would pass 53 trillion dollars worldwide by 2025, more than a third of the 140.5 trillion dollars of professionally managed assets expected by then. Europe is still the largest market.

ESG investing has serious problems, though.

The most basic is that ESG ratings were mostly designed to measure risk to the company, not risk to the world. A company can score well because climate change poses little financial risk to its own business, even if that business is damaging the environment. As one financial journalist put it: ESG ratings are about corporate profits, not planetary health.

There are no consistent standards either. Rating agencies use different methods, which makes it very hard to compare companies or funds. This has produced some well-known oddities. In 2022, Tesla was removed from the S&P ESG index over concerns about workplace conditions and racial discrimination, not for environmental reasons. At the same time, Exxon stayed on the index and another oil company was added.

### Greenwashing

Closely linked to ESG is **greenwashing**: environmental claims that are misleading or not backed by evidence. Regulators are starting to act. The European Union has passed laws aimed at greenwashing in investment products. In the United Kingdom, HSBC had adverts banned for misleading climate claims. In Germany, Deutsche Bank's asset management arm faced legal challenges over similar issues. In the United States, the SEC set up a Climate and ESG Enforcement Task Force, and BNY Mellon paid a 1.5 million dollar fine for misleading ESG claims.

### Green bonds

**Green bonds** are loans raised from investors where the money is committed to projects that help the environment. The market had grown to around 2 trillion dollars by late 2022, and demand still outstrips supply. For issuers, bonds are usually a cheaper way to borrow than bank loans. For investors, they offer fairly stable returns while supporting climate goals.

Green bonds have their own problems. Standards and accountability have often been weak, which leaves room for greenwashing. In one well-known case from 2015, a US college building authority issued a 94 million dollar "green bond" that paid for, among other things, a car park. Standards are improving, helped by groups such as the Climate Bonds Initiative, which certifies bonds against a set standard.

### Climate tech venture capital

Venture capital has moved heavily into climate technology in recent years. In 2022, climate tech venture capital investment reached 70 billion dollars worldwide, up 89% on the year before. It covers renewable energy, electric vehicles, sustainable food, and climate data and analytics, among other areas.

According to PwC, climate tech now takes around 14 cents of every venture capital dollar.

Most of this money, however, goes to areas that cover less than 20% of the emissions cuts available. Hard-to-decarbonise sectors such as heavy industry, shipping and farming get much less attention, despite their large share of total emissions.

Cryptocurrencies raise a separate question. Bitcoin alone used an estimated 121 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2023, close to the combined electricity use of Hungary, Ireland and Switzerland. A single bitcoin transaction uses roughly as much energy as an average US household over three weeks. Bitcoin accounts for around 0.16% of global greenhouse gas emissions. Other cryptocurrencies have gone a different way: Ethereum cut its energy use by over 99% after changing its underlying technology.

## What Can You Do With Your Own Money?

Finance is not only for governments and big institutions. Where you save, invest and borrow also has a measurable climate impact.

For most people, the simplest and most effective step is **switching to a greener bank**. As Project Drawdown's report on personal banking explains, banks lend out deposits across the economy. On average, 1,000 dollars kept in a carbon-intensive bank is linked to roughly the same yearly emissions as a one-way flight from New York to Seattle. Moving from a carbon-intensive bank to a climate-responsible one could cut the climate impact of your banking by up to 76%.

If you have investments, it is worth checking the ESG credentials of the funds in your pension or investment accounts. Because ESG ratings are so inconsistent, this means looking past the label at which companies a fund actually holds.

Bank.green, BankTrack and Banking on Climate Chaos give independent assessments of banks and their fossil fuel financing. In the UK, Make My Money Matter campaigns on the issue and offers practical guidance for individuals.

## Where This Leaves Us

The link between finance and climate is complicated. Finance has paid for real progress on clean energy and climate solutions. It also keeps sending huge sums to the fossil fuel industry, often through the same institutions doing both at once.

Closing the climate finance gap needs action at every level. Governments can move public money towards the climate and away from fossil fuel subsidies. Private investors can apply real environmental standards instead of marketing labels. Regulators can enforce accountability. And individuals can make better-informed choices about their own money.

The challenge is large, but so is the amount of capital in the world. The money to tackle climate change exists. What is missing is the will to direct it properly.
