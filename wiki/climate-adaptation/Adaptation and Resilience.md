Climate change is already here. Carbon dioxide in the atmosphere is at record levels, and changing weather patterns can be seen everywhere. Floods are more frequent, droughts last longer, and heatwaves break records season after season.

Most climate discussion focuses on **mitigation: reducing emissions to slow down warming**. There is a second, equally urgent task that tends to get less attention.

> Even if we stopped all emissions today, we would still need to adapt to the changes already locked in. This is what climate adaptation is about.

**Adaptation does not mean giving up on mitigation**. It means accepting what is already happening and acting on it. That includes building flood barriers and fire protection around homes. It also includes installing air conditioning in places that never needed it, setting up early warning systems for extreme weather, and making sure communities have the money and social support to recover when disaster strikes. Adaptation is both a physical and a social challenge.

## 2. Definitions and Context: What Do Adaptation and Resilience Actually Mean?

The two terms used most in this area are adaptation and resilience. They are related, but they are not the same.

> **Resilience** comes originally from psychology, where it means a general ability to cope with difficulties. In climate, it describes how well a system, whether a city, a region or a community, can absorb shocks and keep working.

> **Adaptation** means the specific actions taken in response to climate impacts, or in anticipation of them. The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change defines adaptation as "adjustments in ecological, social, or economic systems in response to actual or expected climatic stimuli and their effects or impacts."

In simple terms: resilience is a capacity, adaptation is action.

These ideas matter because climate change does not affect everyone equally. The IPCC offers a useful starting point: disasters happen when a vulnerable population is exposed to a climate or weather event. Vulnerability often comes from poverty, inequality and being shut out of political decisions. Poorer communities tend to live in more exposed places, such as flood plains or coastlines, and have fewer resources to recover when things go wrong. Understanding how vulnerability, exposure and impact connect is essential for designing responses that work.

## 3. Resilience: For Whom, to What, and Over What Timeframe?

Resilience is not one simple idea that applies the same way everywhere. It depends on who you ask, which system you are looking at, and over what period of time.

### Three ways a system can respond to disruption

A resilient response to a shock can take different forms:

- **Resistance**: the system absorbs the shock and keeps its main features.
- **Recovery**: the system is affected but returns to roughly where it was before.
- **Adaptation**: the system changes in response and settles into a new state that is as stable as before, or more so.

The only outcome that is not resilient is when a system ends up worse off than before.

### Resilience for whom?

Resilience is not neutral. An outcome that helps one group can harm another. A flood wall that protects a city centre might push water towards poorer neighbourhoods on the edge of town. A policy that supports large farming businesses might leave small farmers worse off. Deciding what counts as a good outcome, and whose interests come first, is a political question.

### Resilience to what?

A system can cope very well with one kind of stress and be completely unprepared for another. People living in informal settlements in Mumbai, for example, have developed very effective ways to cope with yearly flooding. Yet the same communities are much more vulnerable to disease outbreaks or economic shocks. Resilience always relates to a particular kind of disruption.

### Resilience over what timeframe?

Building resilience to today's risks can sometimes create new weaknesses later. In parts of Australia, farmers cleared native deep-rooted plants and irrigated their land to deal with water shortages. This raised the water table and eased soil problems in dry periods. But it also left those farms more exposed to heavy rain. Now that rainfall patterns have changed, the strategy that once protected them has become a problem. Short-term resilience can weaken the ability to adapt in the long run.

So a clear framing is always needed. Any serious discussion of resilience should say which system it means, what kind of stress, from whose point of view, and over what timeframe. Without that, the idea becomes too vague to be useful.

## 4. Climate Change and Our Food System: A Serious and Growing Threat

Food is probably where most people will feel climate change most directly. To understand the risk, it helps to see food as a whole system rather than as a product on a shelf.

### A complex and deeply interconnected system

Food systems cover everything from seeds, fertilisers and animal feed, through farming, fishing and livestock, to processing, packaging, transport, shops and waste. They cross borders, link continents and involve hundreds of millions of workers and producers.

Climate change does not hit just one link in this chain. It disrupts all of them at once, and the effects spread in every direction.

### Key numbers

The food system both suffers from climate change and adds to it:

- **21 to 37% of all global greenhouse gas emissions** come from food systems, counting production, land use, transport, processing and retail.
- Farming alone accounts for 9 to 14% of emissions. Deforestation and other changes in land use add a further 5 to 14%.
- Food loss and waste make up 25 to 30% of all food produced in the world, and account for 8 to 10% of all human-caused emissions.
- The world feeds more people than ever, yet **around 673 million people were undernourished in 2024**, about 8.2% of the world's population (UN SOFI 2025 report).

### How climate change is affecting food production

The impacts can already be measured. Between 1980 and 2015, drought cut global wheat and maize output by up to 40%. Projections suggest that yields of staple crops such as maize, soybeans and rice will fall further, and sooner, than earlier models predicted.

South and Southeast Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa face the greatest risks. These regions are highly exposed to climate change and have the least capacity to adapt. In some scenarios, coping with a severe climate shock in South Asia within a few decades could require food reserves three times larger than the region holds today.

In China, extreme heat is already reducing farm output, even taking into account the adaptation measures already in place.

### Progress stalled, and potentially reversing

From the 1970s to around 2015, the world made big progress in reducing hunger, thanks to better farming methods and better food distribution. That progress has stalled and may be going into reverse. The 2021 UN report on food security was frank: the world is not on track to end hunger or malnutrition by 2030. Conflict, economic instability, a more variable climate and the COVID-19 pandemic have all played a part.

### The adaptation challenge in food systems

Responding to these threats means acting at several levels: developing crop varieties that cope with heat and drought, reducing food loss and waste, rethinking land use to balance food production with cutting emissions, and investing in the resilience of local and regional food systems.

There are real trade-offs. Expanding farmland to grow more food increases emissions and drives deforestation. Using land to grow biofuels or plant-based materials might cut emissions but leaves less room to grow food. There are also approaches that help on both fronts, such as agroforestry (growing trees alongside crops or livestock), which can store carbon and make farms more resilient to climate shocks.

One form of adaptation deserves more recognition: migration. When drought or flooding means people can no longer make a living from their land, they move. This is an understandable human response to a harder situation, rather than a failure of policy, and any serious adaptation plan needs to recognise it and plan for it.
