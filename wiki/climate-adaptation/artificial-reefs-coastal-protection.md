# Artificial Reefs for Coastal Protection and Marine Biodiversity

> **Editorial summary:** Original research by Fabien Mossière drawing on Écologie 360 magazine coverage of the Blue Living Reef project in northern France. Coastal erosion already affects ~20% of the French coastline; Cerema projects €1.2 billion in property at risk by 2050. Instead of more concrete dikes, LineUp Ocean is testing 3D-printed reef modules made with recycled oyster-shell mortar. In a two-year Nausicaá aquarium trial, 28 marine species colonised the modules, and modelling suggests 30% wave dampening on install rising to 45% after colonisation. Open-sea trials on the Opale coast (Wimereux) and Mediterranean (Palavas-les-Flots) are next. Promising but not yet peer-reviewed or field-verified at scale.

## 1. The problem: a coastline under pressure

Coastal erosion is a growing problem in France. It already affects close to 20% of the coastline, around 900 kilometres. According to Cerema (the French centre for studies on risk, environment, mobility and planning), the retreat of the coastline could affect around 5,200 homes and 1,400 business premises by 2050, representing a value of about €1.2 billion.

The Pas-de-Calais region, in northern France, shows the scale of the problem. In January 2026, storm Goretti destroyed part of the sea dike at Ambleteuse. At Wissant, the Aval dune is losing an average of 3.7 metres per year, putting infrastructure, homes and coastal ecosystems at risk.

Several factors are driving this erosion: rising sea levels, more frequent storms, and the degradation of marine and coastal ecosystems that would otherwise help protect the shoreline naturally.

## 2. A shift in approach

For a long time, the response to coastal erosion relied on hard engineering: dikes, groynes, breakwaters and rock armouring. A different approach is now emerging. Instead of working against the sea, some projects aim to work with it, using the ecological functions of marine ecosystems to make the coast more resilient.

This is the thinking behind the Blue Living Reef project, led by the start-up LineUp Ocean together with the Blue Living Lab. The project is supported by the endowment fund of Nausicaá, the national sea centre in Boulogne-sur-Mer, with backers including the BPCE banking group. Its aim is to design structures that reduce coastal erosion while also supporting marine biodiversity.

## 3. First results: a test in the aquarium

In early 2024, LineUp Ocean, which is incubated at the Blue Living Lab, submerged two test modules, each weighing 1.5 tonnes, in the large tank at the Nausicaá aquarium. The goal was to observe how marine life would colonise the structures.

The test showed the formation of a biofilm on the surface, an early sign that the structure could support marine biodiversity. Over the following two years, results went beyond expectations. Up to 28 species were recorded on the modules, including diadem sea urchins, jellyfish polyps, Tubastraea coccinea coral, and sergeant major fish eggs. The modules, which were originally meant to be removed at the end of the test, were left in place because of these results.

## 4. Moving to real conditions

After this successful trial in a controlled tank, the next step is to test the modules in the open sea. On the Opale coast, the project is still at an early stage, selecting a suitable test site. This choice depends on several criteria, including the state of local biodiversity and the specific erosion pressures at each location. Wimereux is currently seen as a promising option, though it is still too early to confirm.

A similar project, called Surfreef and also led by LineUp Ocean, is being tested at Palavas-les-Flots in the Hérault department, on the Mediterranean coast. Together, these projects could shape how coastal areas are protected and restored in the future.

## 5. How the reefs work

Robin Alauze, an oceanographer specialising in coastal physics and an ecological engineer, founded LineUp Ocean. He designs bio-inspired artificial reefs intended to support the return of marine biodiversity while reducing the impact of waves on the coast.

According to Alauze, the two test modules in Nausicaá have performed the ecological functions expected of them, acting as habitat or shelter for 28 species. Feeding and reproductive behaviour has been observed, with some species using cavities in the structure as spawning sites.

The modules are made using 3D printing, with a low-carbon mortar made partly from crushed, recycled oyster shells. Replacing part of the sand in the mortar with oyster shell material makes it more resistant to the physical and chemical stresses of the marine environment. It also creates microporosity and increases calcium carbonate content, which supports the growth of organisms at the base of the marine food chain, an important factor for colonisation by other species.

The design of the modules draws on several natural coastal ecosystems: the tangled root systems of mangroves, seagrass meadows, and the rough surface of rocky shore platforms. As the modules become colonised by marine life, they are designed to dissipate more wave energy over time. Based on modelling by LineUp Ocean, the wave-dampening effect is around 30% as soon as the modules are installed, rising to about 45% once colonisation has taken place.

## 6. Credibility and limitations

The results reported here come from a single case study, described in a magazine article rather than a peer-reviewed publication. The tank trial at Nausicaá was conducted in controlled conditions, which are different from an open sea environment. Wave-dampening figures (30% and 45%) are based on modelling by the company developing the technology, and have not yet been independently verified in the field. Open sea testing on the Opale coast has not yet started, and the final test site has not been confirmed. As with many nature-based coastal solutions, real-world performance may differ from laboratory or modelled results, and long-term durability of the low-carbon mortar in open sea conditions is not yet established.

## 7. Bottom line

Artificial reefs made from low-carbon, oyster-shell-based mortar show early promise as a way to combine coastal erosion protection with marine biodiversity restoration. A two-year trial in a controlled aquarium environment recorded encouraging results, with 28 species colonising the test structures. The next and more important test will be in open sea conditions, where the approach has not yet been proven. If successful, this kind of nature-based solution could offer an alternative, or a complement, to traditional hard coastal defences such as dikes and breakwaters.
