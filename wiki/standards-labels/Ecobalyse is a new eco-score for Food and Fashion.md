Clothing sold in France is starting to carry a new kind of environmental label, and Ecobalyse is the tool behind it. (source: See https://ecobalyse.beta.gouv.fr/ )

---

**1. What is Ecobalyse?**

Ecobalyse is a tool built by the French government to help manufacturers work out the environmental impact of products sold in France. It was developed under the direction of ADEME (the French ecological transition agency) and the Ministry of Ecological Transition, and it is hosted on the government's open digital platform, beta.gouv.fr.

It uses ready-made life cycle assessment data to calculate the impact of each stage of a product's life: extracting the raw materials, making the product, shipping it, and finally throwing it away.

> The result is expressed as an impact score, called the "environmental cost." The higher the score, the greater the environmental impact. The score is not capped at a maximum value, so the intention is to compare products against each other rather than against a fixed threshold.

The tool is free, and its source code is fully public on GitHub. That is fairly unusual for environmental regulation, and it means anyone can check how the scores are worked out.

---

**2. What does it measure?**

A product's environmental cost is built from **16 key indicators**. They cover greenhouse gas emissions, water and land use, energy use, the depletion of mineral resources, and toxicity to people and ecosystems. The scoring follows life cycle assessment principles and draws on the European Product Environmental Footprint method.

Textiles get two extra criteria. One penalises sending used clothes for export outside the EU. The other scores materials by how many microplastic fibres they shed in the wash. Together they help separate fast fashion brands, which tend to use synthetic fibres and push high volumes of purchases, from brands that make longer-lasting clothes, even when their basic production figures look alike.

---

**3. Which sectors does it cover?**

> Ecobalyse currently covers clothing, food products, and everyday objects, though the simulator for objects is still under development. The textile sector has been the primary focus, with the food sector already covered by a stable methodology.

For clothing, the tool follows the whole life of a garment: raw materials, processing steps such as spinning, dyeing and assembly, then transport, sale, use and end of life.

---

**4. Why does it matter and what comes next?**

Ecobalyse is the official reference tool for France's environmental labelling scheme for textiles. The scheme was introduced by the AGEC anti-waste law and made formal by the 2021 Climate and Resilience Law.

The timeline is now confirmed. From October 2025, brands can choose to calculate their environmental cost and submit it to a public portal. From **October 2026**, anyone else (retailers, NGOs, comparison sites or journalists) can publish a score for a brand without asking the brand first. If the brand has not done its own calculation, they fill the gaps with cautious default assumptions. So brands that skip the calculation lose control of how they are presented.

It may not stay a French scheme. Under the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation, adopted in 2024, the European Union is preparing a requirement for environmental labelling of textiles in every member state by 2030. Ecobalyse is often cited as a likely model for the European method.

---

**In short**

Ecobalyse is a practical, government-built tool that turns a life cycle assessment into a single number on a product label. For now it applies only in France, but it looks set to spread across Europe. Brands selling clothing in France need to act before October 2026, or someone else may calculate their score for them, on less favourable terms.
