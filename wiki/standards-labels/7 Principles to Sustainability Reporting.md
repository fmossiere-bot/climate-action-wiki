Sustainability reporting is no longer something companies do only if they feel like it. Investors, employees and local communities are all holding companies to account for their impact on the environment and on people. According to the 2022 Edelman Trust Barometer, 58% of consumers buy or support brands based on their beliefs and values, and 60% choose employers the same way. 64% invest based on their values, and **88% of institutional investors** now examine ESG (environmental, social and governance) performance as closely as financial performance.

So it matters that sustainability reports are done well. These 7 principles give a practical framework for doing that.

---

### 1. Completeness

A sustainability report should look beyond the factory gates. A company's impacts are often spread across its whole supply chain, and a complete report reflects that.

This means stating clearly what the report covers, including which parts of the business and which operations. It also means mapping the organisation's value chain and showing where its direct and indirect impacts happen, from suppliers upstream to customers downstream.

PTT Global Chemical is a good example. Its 2022 sustainability report maps the stakeholders affected at every stage of its business, from raw materials through to downstream production. Readers get a clear picture of where impacts occur and how the company is managing them.

---

### 2. Operating Context

A sustainability report that ignores the wider world is of limited use. Operating context means the outside trends and conditions that could affect an organisation's strategy and performance: climate policy, regulation, trends in its sector and broader changes in society.

Reports should name the big trends that matter to the business, discuss how they could affect strategy and performance, and explain how they feed into the materiality process (see principle 3). For companies heavily exposed to climate change, this includes discussing transition plans. It also includes the financial figures linked to physical risks, such as floods and heat, and to transition risks, such as new rules and carbon prices, in line with TCFD recommendations or similar frameworks.

Holcim's 2022 Integrated Annual Report is cited as good practice. It discusses in detail how climate regulation, including the rising cost of carbon and stricter product rules, is shaping the company's move to a low-carbon economy. It sets out concrete actions on materials, waste recovery and innovation.

---

### 3. Materiality

**Materiality** is the process of finding and ranking the ESG issues that matter most, both to the business and to its stakeholders. A materiality assessment weighs how important each issue is to different stakeholders and how much it does or could affect the business. It should do more than shape the report. It should also guide strategy, goals and KPIs (key performance indicators).

The main recommendations are to describe how material topics were identified and checked, using a range of factors such as outside trends, the size of the impact, how likely it is, and how it fits with the company's wider risk management. The results should be published as a matrix or a ranked list, and the report's content should follow them.

The idea of **double materiality** is also gaining ground, especially because of the EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD). Double materiality asks companies to look in both directions: at how sustainability issues affect the business financially, and at how the company's activities affect people and the planet. Royal Philips's 2022 Annual Report is cited as an early example of a company carrying out a first double materiality analysis to prepare for these rules.

---

### 4. Alignment

Once a materiality assessment is done, the report should reflect it. In an aligned report, the disclosures follow the results of the assessment, and quality comes before quantity. A long report that skims many topics is less useful than a focused one that covers the material issues in depth.

In practice, this means avoiding over-disclosure, filling the report with issues that are not material, and under-disclosure, glossing over the ones that are. The organisation's strategy and targets should also be visibly linked to the same material topics. Companies that skip the materiality assessment altogether score lower in quality evaluations.

---

### 5. Stakeholder Engagement

Sustainability reports should not be written in a vacuum. Stakeholder engagement is an ongoing, open conversation with the people and groups the organisation affects, or who affect it, including investors, customers, employees, suppliers and local communities.

Engagement can range from routine tools such as surveys and questionnaires to more formal processes such as stakeholder forums, advisory committees and structured dialogues. What matters is that the report shows how this engagement shapes the company's view of its material issues, and how it influences decisions.

The recommendations are clear. Say who you engage with and how, describe the formal mechanisms in place, and show what each stakeholder group raised and how those concerns were dealt with. A general statement that "we engaged with stakeholders" is not enough. Readers need evidence that the process had substance.

Neste's 2022 Annual Report is highlighted as good practice. It includes a detailed table of engagement methods, the main topics each stakeholder group raised, and how that input fed directly into the materiality assessment and the review of ESG risks.

---

### 6. Balance

A sustainability report that only shares good news is not credible. Balanced reports are open about risks, failures, missed targets and areas to improve, alongside real progress and good performance.

This does not mean dwelling on problems for the sake of it. It means giving a fair account of the reporting period, with concrete examples of difficulties, targets that were not met, and outside voices that add another perspective. Graphics and text should show performance accurately, not selectively.

Balance matters because it builds trust. A report that paints an unrealistically rosy picture is more likely to draw scepticism or accusations of greenwashing than one that admits difficulties alongside progress. IKEA's FY22 Sustainability Report is given as a good example. It uses a clear three-pillar structure and gives each material topic its own space, with open, balanced disclosure that makes the content easy for readers to follow.

---

### 7. External Assurance

The last principle is about credibility. **External assurance** means hiring an independent third party to check the sustainability information in the report. It goes beyond internal controls and audits to give an outside opinion.

The main recommendations are to choose an assurance provider with a broad scope, covering both the reporting process and the material key performance indicators. Companies should aim for a "reasonable" level of assurance, so that sustainability information meets the same standard as financial information. The assurance statement should be easy to find in the report or through direct links. It should state the scope, the boundaries, the standard used and the provider's statement of independence.

As sustainability reporting faces more regulation, especially under the CSRD in Europe, external assurance is moving from optional good practice to a requirement for many organisations.

---

### The Seven Principles Together

Together, these seven principles make a coherent approach to sustainability reporting. Start with a complete picture of where impacts happen. Ground the report in its outside context. Find out what really matters through a careful materiality process, and make the report's content match those findings. Show real engagement with the people affected. Present performance fairly, the bad with the good. Then have the whole thing checked by an independent party.

Reports that apply all seven principles consistently are more useful to readers, more credible to investors, and better able to stand up to the growing scrutiny that sustainability disclosures now face.
