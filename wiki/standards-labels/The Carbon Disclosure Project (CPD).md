CDP (formerly the Carbon Disclosure Project) is one of the most referenced names in sustainability reporting, alongside frameworks like the Gold Standard, GRI and CSRD.

> CDP is not a certification body for individual projects. It is best understood as a global disclosure system: it does not verify climate action itself, but it collects, scores, and publishes the data that companies, cities, and regions report about their own environmental performance.



## 1. What is CDP?

CDP is a UK-registered non-profit founded in 2000 by Paul Dickinson and Tessa Tennant. The original idea was simple: get investors to formally ask companies to disclose their greenhouse gas emissions, so that climate risk could be judged the same way as financial risk. Its scope has since grown well beyond carbon.

Today CDP runs a standard yearly questionnaire covering three main areas:

- **Climate change**: emissions (scope 1, 2 and, more and more, scope 3), climate risks and opportunities, and transition plans.
- **Forests**: exposure to deforestation risk in commodities such as timber, palm oil, soy and cattle products.
- **Water security**: water use, risk and management across operations and supply chains.

Organisations respond either because they are asked (by investors through CDP's "Letter to the Board", by a customer through the Supply Chain programme, or by a city network) or because they choose to. Each response is scored from D- to A, across four levels of maturity: Disclosure, Awareness, Management and Leadership. Companies that reach the top band in all the themes that apply to them make CDP's "A List". Those scoring A in climate, forests and water together earn the rare "Triple A" rating.

CDP's weight comes from its size. In the 2025 cycle, **over 22,100 companies** disclosed. Together they represent more than half of global stock market value, and CDP's data is used by financial institutions that manage a large share of the world's institutional assets. On the local government side, more than 1,000 cities, states and regions, home to over a billion people, disclosed through CDP's dedicated CDP-ICLEI Track platform.

## 2. Latest updates

CDP's questionnaire and structure change most years, and the 2026 cycle brought several shifts:

- **A single integrated questionnaire.** Instead of separate forms for climate, forests and water, companies now fill in one combined questionnaire. Which modules they see depends on their size, sector and risk profile.
- **A new Oceans module.** For the first time, companies can report on ocean-related risks, dependencies and impacts, alongside climate, forests and water.
- **An SME pathway to a Leadership score.** Small and medium-sized companies can now earn an "SME A" score for climate change, separate from the full corporate A score. This gives smaller businesses a clearer route to recognition, in proportion to their size.
- **Closer alignment with mandatory reporting.** CDP has kept increasing the overlap between its questionnaire and frameworks such as the EU's CSRD/ESRS, the ISSB's IFRS S2 and TNFD. The aim is for a CDP response to be a starting point that companies can reuse for several reporting obligations, rather than a separate exercise.
- **Institutional restructuring.** CDP has announced it will split into two linked bodies: a non-profit CDP Foundation and a commercial CDP arm. The idea is to keep the public-interest mission separate from the services and data products CDP sells to businesses and investors.
- **Key 2026 dates**: the questionnaire opened in Q2, the scoring deadline fell in mid-September, and the reporting window closes in late October. A List results are usually published the following January.

## 3. Cities, states and regions

Alongside the corporate questionnaire, CDP runs a parallel system for local and regional governments, together with ICLEI (an international network of local governments working on sustainability), under the name CDP-ICLEI Track. To be scored, a city needs at least a clear greenhouse gas emissions inventory and a published climate action plan. Higher scores require evidence that the plan is being carried out, rather than only written down.

> This part of CDP's work has grown quickly. In the 2025 cycle, 738 cities were scored, with 122 receiving the top A grade, alongside a growing States and Regions A List now in its second year. Cities named on recent A Lists give a sense of the range involved, from major capitals to mid-sized towns: London, Barcelona, Mexico City, Cape Town, Auckland, Johannesburg, Denver, and Quito have all featured in different years, alongside a steady expansion of UK entries such as Birmingham, Brighton & Hove, Cambridge, and Sunderland.

Ireland is worth a direct mention. **Belfast is currently the only city on the island of Ireland with an A rating**, and as of 2026 it has kept it for four years in a row. It sits alongside roughly twenty other UK local authorities. Belfast's submission points to a citywide emissions inventory, a published Net Zero Carbon Roadmap and Climate Action Plan, renewable electricity supply agreements for council buildings, and bodies such as the Belfast Climate Commission. No city in the Republic of Ireland is currently on the A List, though Irish-based companies do appear on it. For example, Accenture and Trane Technologies made the A List in CDP's 2022 climate change report.

On the company side, the CDP Ireland Network, a local affiliate that helps Irish companies through the disclosure process, publishes its own yearly Ireland Climate Change report. Recent editions show steady growth in participation, a rising share of companies with externally verified science-based targets, and more companies getting their emissions data checked by a third party. They also warn, year after year, that the overall pace of decarbonisation is still slower than it needs to be.

## 4. Credibility and controversy

CDP is widely seen as the most established environmental disclosure platform in the world, and it is often described, including by CDP itself, as a kind of "gold standard" for thorough reporting. Financial institutions rely on it, and regulators increasingly refer to it when designing mandatory disclosure rules. Even so, a few points are worth bearing in mind.

**CDP scores disclosure quality, not environmental performance.** A high CDP score means a company has reported thoroughly and openly, has credible targets, and shows evidence of managing environmental risk. It does not mean the company's overall emissions are low or that its total footprint is small. A large, carbon-heavy company with a serious, well-documented transition plan can score higher than a smaller company that reports less carefully but happens to pollute less. This is often lost when CDP scores are used in marketing.

**The system depends on self-reported data.** CDP does not measure a company's emissions itself. It checks that the data is complete and consistent, and increasingly compares it with the company's other public disclosures. But the figures still come from the company, and third-party verification earns extra points rather than being a strict requirement for every data point.

**Participation is still uneven.** Even with more than 22,000 company disclosures a year, large parts of the global economy are not covered, particularly private and smaller companies outside CDP's investor and supply chain networks. The same goes for cities: most participants are in Europe, North America and a limited number of Global South countries. So the A List does not show all climate leadership in the world, only leadership among those who chose, or were asked, to disclose.

**Changes to the scoring method make year-on-year comparison difficult.** Because the questionnaire is revised most years, sometimes heavily, a change in a company's or city's score does not always mean performance really got better or worse. It can also reflect a change in the questions.

None of this makes CDP unreliable. The message is "understand what the score measures" rather than "distrust the system". Used properly, CDP data is one of the better public indicators of how seriously an organisation treats environmental risk. It is also one of the few systems that gives cities the kind of structured scrutiny usually reserved for companies.

## 5. Reading a CDP score

CDP is not a certification like the Gold Standard or FSC, and it does not claim that a project or product is "sustainable". It is a reporting and scoring system that makes environmental disclosure comparable across thousands of organisations, and its size gives it real influence over investment and purchasing decisions.

If you are looking at a company or a city through its CDP score, read the score as a measure of transparency and management maturity. Check whether the emissions data behind it has been verified by a third party. And look at how the score has moved over several years rather than at any single score on its own.
