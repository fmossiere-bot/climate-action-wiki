# SBTi — Science Based Targets initiative

## What is it

The **Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi)** is a UK-based nonprofit that sets the rules for how companies can credibly claim to be pursuing net-zero emissions. It is widely regarded as the gold standard for corporate climate commitments. A company with an SBTi-approved target has had its decarbonisation plan reviewed against climate science — specifically, whether the company's emissions reduction trajectory is consistent with limiting global warming to 1.5°C.

In June 2026, SBTi published a major update to its corporate net-zero standard. The new framework is described as a **"best-efforts" approach**: companies must take every action within their control, but the standard now acknowledges that they cannot control everything.

## Why it matters

Thousands of large companies globally have signed up to SBTi. The rules SBTi sets determine what counts as genuine progress versus greenwashing. The 2026 update is significant because it expands what companies can count toward their targets, which has been welcomed by business as more practical but criticised by some environmentalists as a dilution of standards.

## What changed in the 2026 standard

Until 2026, SBTi required companies to focus their efforts on decarbonising their own supply chains. Buying external credits or offsets did not count toward SBTi-approved targets. The new rules change this in two important ways.

First, companies may now count **purchased environmental credits** in their footprint calculations. This includes instruments such as sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) credits, renewable energy certificates, and similar market-based mitigation tools purchased outside the company's own supply chain. These were previously excluded.

Second, **carbon removal technologies** — including direct air capture, reforestation credits, and similar approaches — will be allowed from 2035 onward, to cover the small share of emissions that are genuinely impossible to eliminate by then.

The changes are framed around the idea that companies face structural barriers they cannot overcome alone, and that penalising them for those barriers does not serve anyone. CEO David Kennedy described the transition as "a continuous improvement journey."

## Power purchase agreements and electricity matching

**Power purchase agreements (PPAs)** — long-term contracts that directly fund new renewable energy — can now be counted toward a company's clean electricity claims. Where structural barriers prevent local PPAs, companies can claim credit for PPAs in other regions.

On hourly electricity matching (calculating emissions at source each hour rather than annually), SBTi says hourly matching is preferable but will not mandate it for target-setting. Companies with significant electricity use, such as data centre operators, must at least report what share of their consumption is matched with low-carbon electricity on an hourly basis.

## Key facts

- SBTi's 2026 update allows **market-based environmental credits** to count toward net-zero targets for the first time.
- **Carbon removals** (reforestation, DAC) allowed from **2035** for hard-to-abate residual emissions.
- **PPAs** now count; local is preferred, but cross-regional PPAs are permitted where barriers exist.
- Hourly electricity matching is described as "generally preferable" but not required.
- Physical decarbonisation within the supply chain remains the primary expectation.
- Companies must be transparent about barriers that limited what was possible.

## July 2026: SBTi publishes the final Corporate Net-Zero Standard V2.0

In late July 2026, SBTi published the **final Corporate Net-Zero Standard V2.0** — the biggest structural redesign of the framework since 2021. It **takes effect on 1 February 2027**, with new-rule target validation opening in Q1 2027. Existing V1.3.1 remains available for new target submissions until **31 January 2028**.

The two-year debate about whether carbon credits belong in an SBTi-aligned strategy is now settled: **yes — through a new structured programme called the Ongoing Emissions Responsibility (OER) framework**, which replaces the previous Beyond Value Chain Mitigation (BVCM) guidance.

### What actually changed

**A two-tier company classification.** Companies are now split into:

- **Category A** — annual turnover **>€50m in high-income countries or >€450m in lower-income countries**. Mandatory transition plans, mandatory scope 3 targets, mandatory third-party assurance, and — from 2035 — mandatory carbon removal purchases.
- **Category B** — mostly SMEs and lower-income-country companies below those thresholds. Lighter obligations, but they will still be asked for emissions data by Category A customers up the value chain.

**Reporting cadence.** Annual reporting is now required (scope 1 and 2 every year; full scope 1-3 at the end of each five-year cycle). Long-term net-zero targets are optional; **near-term five-year targets are not** (scope 1 and 2 for all; scope 3 for Category A).

**Three formal roles for carbon credits.** For the first time, SBTi gives credits a defined place across the whole journey to net zero:

1. **Voluntary OER recognition (2027–2035)** — three public tiers (Engaged / Advanced / Leadership)
2. **Mandatory removal purchases (from 2035 for Category A)** — starting at 1% of ongoing emissions and scaling to 100% by the company's net-zero year
3. **Neutralisation of residual emissions at net-zero year** — offset with removal credits matched to the durability of the emissions being neutralised

**What has not changed:** carbon credits still cannot be used to meet scope 1, 2 or 3 emissions reduction targets. Reductions remain foundational.

### The OER recognition tiers

Measured against cumulative scope 1-3 emissions across the five-year target cycle:

| Tier | Coverage | Contribution budget |
|---|---|---|
| **Engaged** | ≥1% of total scope 1-3 | tonne-for-tonne with retired verified credits, OR **~$20/tonne** contribution budget |
| **Advanced** | 100% of scope 1 & 2 + ≥10% of scope 1-3 | same options |
| **Leadership** | 100% of all ongoing scope 1-3 emissions | **≥$80/tonne** contribution budget + retired verified credits for full covered volume |

The **$80/tonne figure has attracted a lot of press attention** but it is not a market price, compliance requirement or minimum credit price. It is the **defined contribution budget for the Leadership tier only** — what SBTi describes as "the full internalisation of the cost of climate change." A company with 500,000 tCO₂e of ongoing emissions choosing Leadership would face an annual budget of ~$40m; Engaged for the same volume would be ~$100,000.

### The 2035 mandate

From **2035**, the OER programme becomes **mandatory for Category A** companies:

- 2035: cover at least **1%** of ongoing scope 1-3 emissions with **carbon removal credits**
- Requirement scales annually to **100% by the company's net-zero target year**
- **Durable removals phased in**: **10% of the removal requirement in 2035, rising to 100% by net-zero year**

Because SBTi runs on five-year review cycles, many businesses will actually confront the 2035 transition **at their next target validation, not in 2035 itself**. Removal supply is currently limited (~5-10 million tonnes/year against a voluntary market of ~180 million tonnes), so prices are expected to rise as the mandate approaches.

### Nature-based solutions under OER

Under the **voluntary OER programme (2027-2035)**, nature-based reduction, avoidance and removal credits all qualify. From **2035 onward**, the mandatory requirement shifts to **removals only**, but **nature-based removals can still account for up to 90%** in 2035, gradually declining as durable engineered removals scale.

### Quality bar

Credits used under OER must be:

- Measured **after the fact** (ex-post outcomes only)
- **Independently verified** against a recognised standard
- **Permanently retired** when claimed
- **Generated within five years** of the reporting year

### Transparency architecture

Every company must **declare its OER position at target validation** — including any explanation for opting out — and the decision is **published on the SBTi Dashboard**, visible to investors, customers and regulators. That transparency layer is arguably as important as the tier structure itself.
