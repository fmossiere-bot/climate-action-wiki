Carbon credits sold on the voluntary market vary a lot in quality, so the label on a project matters.

> The Gold Standard is one of the two most used certification bodies for carbon credits worldwide, alongside Verra. 

It was set up to fix a specific problem. Many early carbon offset projects were not delivering real cuts in emissions, and some harmed the communities where they operated. Whether the Gold Standard has solved that problem, or only partly, is still debated.

## 1. What is the Gold Standard?

The Gold Standard for the Global Goals (its full name) is a certification scheme for climate and development projects. WWF founded it in 2003, with other environmental groups, because of worries about the quality of credits issued under the UN's Clean Development Mechanism.

> The idea was to set a higher bar: projects should reduce emissions and also bring measurable benefits to local people and ecosystems.

The scheme is run by the **Gold Standard Foundation**, a non-profit body based in Geneva, Switzerland. It is not a government body and it does not create credits itself. It writes the rules, and independent auditors (called Validation and Verification Bodies) check whether a project follows them. A project that passes can issue carbon credits carrying the Gold Standard label.

To be certified, a project generally has to show:

- **Additionality**: the emission cut would not have happened without the project. A solar farm that would have been built anyway, for purely commercial reasons, would fail this test.
- **Real and measurable reductions**: emissions must be tracked with an approved method rather than loosely estimated.
- **No double counting**: a credit can only be claimed once, by one buyer.
- **Contribution to at least three of the UN Sustainable Development Goals**, such as clean water, gender equality or less poverty, on top of the climate benefit.

From 2026 onward, the Gold Standard has tightened its rules again so that all new credits line up with the Paris Agreement. That means stricter checks on additionality, more cautious baselines (the "what would have happened anyway" starting point), and closer attention to whether a project's claimed reductions fit with the host country's own climate policies and targets instead of working against them.

Once a project is certified, its credits and documents are recorded on the Gold Standard's public Impact Registry. Anyone can check a project's status, the credits it has issued, and whether a buyer has already used ("retired") them.

## 2. What kinds of projects are certified?

The Gold Standard covers a wide range of projects. The main categories are:

- **Renewable energy**, such as wind, solar and small-scale hydropower, mostly in regions where grid electricity still relies heavily on fossil fuels.
- **Energy efficiency**, including improved cookstoves that cut the amount of wood or charcoal a household burns.
- **Forestry and land use**, covering new forests, replanting and forest protection.
- **Agriculture**, including practices that build soil carbon or reduce methane from farming.
- **Water access**, through projects that provide clean water so people no longer need to boil it over open fires.
- **Community-based projects** more broadly, where the social or health benefit matters as much as the climate one.

Projects often cited as strong performers include reforestation in Ethiopia (the Humbo Assisted Natural Regeneration project), forest protection around Virunga National Park in the Democratic Republic of Congo, and clean cookstove programmes across East Africa. They tend to score well because they combine believable emissions data with clear, checkable benefits for the community.

Individual projects change all the time as new ones are certified and older ones expire, so the more useful thing to know is that the full, current list is public. Anyone can search it on the Gold Standard Impact Registry and filter by project type, country or status.

## 3. Credibility and controversy

The Gold Standard is generally seen as one of the stricter schemes on the market, and it is often recommended as a safer choice than uncertified offsets. That reputation is largely deserved. Its additionality tests, its SDG requirements and its public registry are all stronger than what many earlier offset schemes offered.

Stricter than the alternatives does not mean free of problems, though. A few criticisms come up again and again.

**Over-crediting in some project types.** Independent academic reviews have found that clean cookstove projects in particular have sometimes overstated their impact. This can happen when more households are assumed to use the new stove than really do, when families keep using their old stove alongside the new one, or when the starting assumptions about fuel use are too generous. The Gold Standard has revised some of its methods in response, but anyone buying cookstove credits should keep an eye on this.

**Additionality is hard to prove for renewable energy.** Older renewable energy projects connected to the grid face the same issue as similar projects certified under other schemes. In countries where renewables already make money on their own or get government support, it is hard to say for sure that a given project needed carbon credit income to go ahead. This is one reason the Gold Standard tightened its additionality rules from 2026 onward, but credits issued under the older rules are still in circulation.

**The wider reputation of the voluntary carbon market.** Much of the criticism aimed at the Gold Standard is really criticism of carbon offsetting as an idea. High-profile scandals at other registries, and cases of outright fraud investigated by regulators in the US and UK, have made buyers more cautious across the board. The Gold Standard has responded publicly. It argues that giving up on credible carbon finance altogether would be the wrong lesson, and that the answer is stronger rules rather than no rules.

**Rules on using credits in marketing.** This is less a criticism of the Gold Standard and more a warning for anyone using its credits. From September 2026, EU consumer protection rules (under the Empowering Consumers Directive) restrict blanket "climate neutral" claims based only on offsetting. German courts have already ruled that any "climate neutral" advert must explain clearly whether the claim rests on real emissions cuts, on offsetting, or on both. So a company can hold Gold Standard credits in good faith and still run into legal trouble if its marketing overstates what those credits mean.

## 4. In summary

The Gold Standard is a credible, well-run certification scheme and a reasonable choice for anyone who wants to support real climate and development projects. It does not guarantee that every credit it issues is flawless. Quality still varies by project type and by when a project was certified. Cookstove projects and older renewable energy projects deserve a closer look than more recent forestry or removal projects.

If you buy or recommend Gold Standard credits, a few habits help. Check the project's entry on the public Impact Registry rather than relying on marketing material. Where possible, favour more recent vintages (the year the emission cut took place) certified under the tighter 2026 rules. And treat credits as an addition to real emissions cuts, never a replacement for them, both for the climate's sake and to stay within EU advertising rules.
