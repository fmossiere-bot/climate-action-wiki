_Companies are paying more attention to the financial risks that come from damage to nature. The TNFD was built to give organisations a way to measure and report those risks, and it is now entering a new phase._

**1. What is the TNFD?**

> The Taskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures (TNFD) is an international framework that helps companies and financial institutions identify, assess, and report on risks related to nature. That includes biodiversity loss, water stress, deforestation, land degradation, and other pressures on ecosystems that businesses both depend on and contribute to.

The TNFD published its final recommendations in September 2023. The framework rests on four pillars that will be familiar to anyone who has worked on climate reporting: governance, strategy, risk and impact management, and metrics and targets. That was deliberate. The TNFD was designed to sit alongside the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) and the ISSB's IFRS sustainability standards, so organisations can cover climate and nature in their reporting in one consistent way.

The framework also lines up with the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF), the international agreement adopted in 2022 that sets targets for halting and reversing biodiversity loss by 2030.

---

**2. Who has adopted it?**

> Adoption has grown quickly since the recommendations were finalised. As of late 2025, over 700 companies with a combined market value of around 9.4 trillion US dollars have committed to TNFD-aligned reporting. This is up from 320 organisations in January 2024 and around 500 by the end of 2024. By October 2026 more than 800 had adopted the recommendations.

Early adopters come from many sectors and countries: 54 countries and 62 of the 77 industry sectors covered by SASB standards. They include KPMG, Qantas, abrdn and Tokyo Electric Power.

The growth is part of a wider shift. According to CDP data, biodiversity disclosures rose by 43% between 2022 and 2024, after the GBF was adopted. Water-related reporting rose by 23%, and forest disclosures by 10%. The gaps are still large, though. Only around 10% of companies currently assess how much they depend on biodiversity, even though nature loss could cost the global economy up to **20 trillion US dollars per year**, according to some estimates.

---

**3. What happens now?**

> In November 2025, the TNFD announced that it will wind down its operations by 2026 and hand responsibility for nature-related reporting standards to the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB).

This is a big step, though it went less far than many expected. The ISSB, which already looks after the IFRS sustainability disclosure standards, decided in April 2026 not to write a new mandatory standard on nature. Instead it proposed a non-mandatory practice statement based on the TNFD framework, which sits alongside its existing standards and which countries can choose to make mandatory. The draft was published for consultation on 22 October 2026, during the COP17 biodiversity talks in Yerevan, and comments close on 19 February 2027. See [the ISSB nature practice statement](issb-nature-practice-statement-2026.md) for the debate this caused.

The TNFD will finish its remaining technical work by the third quarter of 2026. That includes new guidance, published in late 2025, to help companies build nature into their wider transition plans.

Emmanuel Faber, chair of the ISSB, has encouraged organisations already using the TNFD framework to keep going. Reporting in line with the TNFD now is seen as the most practical way to stay consistent with whatever the ISSB eventually publishes.

---

**Where this leaves companies**

The TNFD has put nature on the corporate reporting agenda and built a credible framework where there was none before. Handing over to the ISSB shows that nature reporting has moved from a niche concern into mainstream sustainability disclosure. For companies already reporting on climate, the message is clear: within the next few years, they will be expected to report nature-related risks alongside climate risks in their formal disclosures.
