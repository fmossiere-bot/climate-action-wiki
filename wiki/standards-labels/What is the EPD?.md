An **Environmental Product Declaration, or EPD**, is one of the more technical tools in sustainability. A label such as FSC or the Gold Standard tells you a product meets a bar. An EPD does not say whether a product is "good" or "bad" for the environment. It gives you standardised, checked numbers and leaves the judgement to the reader. That makes EPDs less visible to everyday shoppers, but more and more central to how construction, manufacturing and purchasing decisions are made in Europe.

## 1. What is an EPD?

An EPD is a Type III environmental declaration under the international standard ISO 14025. In practice, it is a document that measures a product's environmental impact across its whole life, from extracting the raw materials through manufacturing and use to disposal. It is based on a Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) carried out under ISO 14040 and ISO 14044.

An EPD differs from an ordinary sustainability claim in three ways:

- **It follows fixed rules.** Every EPD for a given type of product is built using the same Product Category Rules (PCR). This technical document fixes the functional unit (the amount of product being measured), the system boundaries and the impact categories. That is what lets two EPDs for, say, insulation panels or floor tiles be compared like for like, at least when they come from the same PCR and programme.
- **It is independently verified.** A qualified third-party verifier checks the LCA and the final document before it can be published. A self-declared environmental claim (ISO 14021), by contrast, has no independent check.
- **It does not rank or endorse.** An EPD reports figures such as global warming potential, water use or resource depletion. It does not say one product is better for the environment than another, even when a comparison is technically possible. That judgement is left to the reader, usually an architect, specifier or purchasing team.

In construction, the European standard EN 15804 sits alongside ISO 14025 as the common Product Category Rule. That is why EPDs are so common for building materials such as concrete, steel, insulation and timber. Sub-standards, such as EN 16757 for concrete or EN 50693 for electrical products, add detail for particular product families.

An EPD is usually valid for five years, after which it must be renewed or updated. Once the LCA is done, it typically costs several thousand euros to produce, which is one reason smaller manufacturers have been slower to take them up.

## 2. Who issues EPDs, and how the system is organised

There is no single global body issuing EPDs. Instead, a network of "programme operators" runs the rules, verification and publication of EPDs within their own systems. The main ones active in Europe are:

- **EPD International (the International EPD System)**, based in Sweden, one of the largest and most widely used systems in the world.
- **Institut Bauen und Umwelt (IBU)**, based in Germany, prominent in construction.
- **BRE**, in the United Kingdom, which also runs its own Verified EPD scheme linked to BREEAM.
- **AENOR's GlobalEPD programme**, in Spain.
- **EPD Hub**, a newer programme operator working internationally, compliant with ISO 14025, EN 15804 and several related standards.

To make the system less fragmented, the main European programme operators set up **ECO Platform**. It audits member programmes against EN 15804 and its own verification guidelines. An EPD carrying the ECO Platform mark is meant to be recognised across Europe, not only within the programme that issued it, and is available in a digital, machine-readable format through the ECO Portal.

Growth has been fast. The International EPD System alone passed **18,000 valid, registered EPDs** in 2025, and more than 9,000 new EPDs were published worldwide that year across all programme operators. Newer digital formats, such as EN ISO 22057 and openEPD, are being developed so that EPD data can flow straight into building design and Building Information Modelling (BIM) software, instead of being read by hand from a PDF.

## 3. Latest developments

Several regulatory and technical changes are shaping EPDs in 2026 and beyond:

- **A new EU Construction Products Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2024/3110) replaced the older Construction Products Regulation from January 2025 and starts applying to most construction products from January 2026. It links environmental performance data more closely to the mandatory Declaration of Performance. That is expected to turn EPD-style data from a competitive advantage into a basic requirement for construction products sold in the EU.
- **A common EU method for whole-life carbon in buildings.** From 2028, member states must report the whole-life global warming potential of new buildings over 1,000 square metres, and of all new buildings from 2030. This creates direct demand for the product-level carbon data that EPDs supply, because a calculation for a whole building is only as good as the EPDs for its materials.
- **Digital Product Passports.** By 2028, environmental data on construction products, including figures from EPDs, is expected to feed into the EU's Digital Product Passport scheme, so the data lives on well beyond individual sales documents.
- **A revised ISO 14025.** The underlying international standard is being updated for the first time since 2006. The new edition is expected to formalise some practices that grew up informally over the past decade, such as digital and machine-readable formats.
- **EU rules on green claims.** The proposed Green Claims Directive would have required companies making environmental claims about a product to back them with accurate, verified evidence based on the product's life cycle, which is exactly what an EPD is designed to provide. In June 2025 the European Commission announced it intended to withdraw the proposal and negotiations were cancelled. It has not been formally withdrawn, but its future is uncertain. A separate law, the Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive, has applied since 27 September 2026 and already bans vague environmental claims that cannot be proven, which still gives companies a reason to use EPDs to support specific claims.

## 4. Credibility and limitations

EPDs are among the more rigorous environmental disclosure tools, largely because they combine fixed rules (PCRs) with mandatory independent verification. Still, it helps to know their limits before treating an EPD as a simple green or red light.

**An EPD does not mean a product is good for the environment.** This is the most common misunderstanding, so it bears repeating. An EPD is a transparency document, not an endorsement. A product with high embodied carbon can have a perfectly valid EPD. The EPD simply confirms that the high figure has been measured and checked, not that it is low.

**Comparisons have real limits.** Two EPDs can only be safely compared when they share the same PCR, the same functional unit and, ideally, the same programme operator. EPDs from different product categories, or built on different versions of a standard, cannot be compared directly, even though they look alike on the page. Readers unfamiliar with LCA methods can easily draw the wrong conclusion by putting two EPDs side by side.

**Scope choices can flatter the result.** A "cradle-to-gate" EPD stops at the factory door. It usually tells a different, more flattering story than a "cradle-to-grave" EPD, which includes the impacts of use and disposal. Both are allowed under the standards, but a company can pick the narrower scope for a product whose use or disposal impacts look worse, without breaking any rule.

**Verification quality varies between markets.** ECO Platform's audit process is generally well regarded in Europe. EPD programmes outside this network, particularly in North America and Asia, vary more in how strictly they apply PCRs and how carefully they vet verifiers. So "third-party verified" carries slightly different weight depending on the programme behind it.

**Cost is still a barrier for smaller manufacturers.** A full LCA and verification usually costs several thousand euros per product, so EPDs are more common among larger manufacturers with the budget and technical know-how to commission them. That can leave smaller or newer producers at a disadvantage on disclosure, even when their actual environmental performance holds up well.

## 5. How to read an EPD

An EPD is one of the more credible tools for comparing the environmental footprint of individual products, because it is deliberately dull: fixed rules, independent checks, and no marketing spin in the figures. Its value depends on how it is read. Used properly, an EPD supports well-informed choices between products that do the same job. Used carelessly, or quoted out of context in marketing, the same document can give a false impression that a product is greener than others, which the EPD never claimed.

If you are assessing a supplier's EPDs, check which PCR and programme operator were used, whether the scope is cradle-to-gate or cradle-to-grave, and whether the EPD is only being compared with others built on the same rules.
