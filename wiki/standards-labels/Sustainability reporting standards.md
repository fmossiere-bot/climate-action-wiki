Sustainability reporting can be confusing. **More than 600 different standards, frameworks and initiatives** are in circulation worldwide, but only a handful matter to most companies. This page goes through the main ones, groups them by region, and explains which are required by law, which are voluntary but widely used, and how things shifted during 2026, a year of real change on both sides of the Atlantic.

## 1. Why there are so many standards in the first place

Financial accounting has settled on a few globally recognised systems. Sustainability reporting grew up in a more scattered way: NGOs, investor groups, stock exchanges and now governments each built their own frameworks to answer slightly different questions. Some are aimed at investors weighing up financial risk. Others are aimed at regulators, customers or the public. That difference in audience is often the simplest way to tell two standards apart.

> The push toward consolidation is real but still in progress. The International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) has spent the past two years explicitly working to reduce this complexity, partnering with the Transition Plan Taskforce, the GHG Protocol, CDP, the Taskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures (TNFD), and the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) to bring the various systems closer together. As of mid-2026, more than 30 jurisdictions, together accounting for a significant share of global GDP and market capitalisation, have adopted or are moving to adopt ISSB standards in some form. That is real progress, but it has not eliminated the underlying regional split described below.

## 2. The main standards, grouped by what they are for

Before going region by region, it helps to know what each standard is trying to do.

- **GRI (Global Reporting Initiative)**: the oldest and broadest standard, first published in 1997. It is written for everyone with a stake in a company, not only investors. GRI has no central enforcement body, but companies can publish their reports through its database. It is still the most widely used voluntary standard in the world, with roughly 10,000 organisations reporting against it.
- **ISSB / IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards (IFRS S1 and S2)**: created in 2022 by the IFRS Foundation for investors and finance teams, linking sustainability disclosure to the financial statements. IFRS S2 has in effect absorbed and replaced the older TCFD framework, and IFRS S1 has taken on much of SASB's industry-by-industry approach.
- **TCFD (Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures)**: a framework for disclosing climate risk, rather than a full reporting standard. It has now been folded into the ISSB standards, but its four pillars (governance, strategy, risk management, metrics and targets) still shape how climate risk is reported almost everywhere, including in California's state rules.
- **SASB (Sustainability Accounting Standards Board)**: industry-specific metrics aimed at investors. SASB merged into the Value Reporting Foundation in 2021, which the ISSB then absorbed, so today SASB lives on mainly as the industry-specific backbone of IFRS S1.
- **CDP**: a disclosure and scoring system (covered in more detail in a separate article) rather than a reporting standard. Companies, cities and regions answer CDP's questionnaire and get a score, and much of that data is now cross-referenced with ISSB and CSRD requirements.
- **EU CSRD / ESRS**: the EU's own mandatory reporting law and the disclosure standards that go with it. It is covered in detail below, because it now looks quite different from how it was designed in 2022.
- **TNFD (Taskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures)**: a newer framework focused on risks linked to nature and biodiversity. It deliberately copies TCFD's structure, so companies already used to climate risk reporting can apply the same thinking to nature. TNFD and EFRAG (the body behind ESRS) have jointly published a mapping document showing where their ideas, their approach to materiality and their disclosure metrics line up, so that companies covered by CSRD can use TNFD without doing the work twice.
- **B Corp**: a private certification (not strictly a disclosure standard) covering environmental, social and governance performance. It is most used by small and medium-sized companies, often privately owned.

## 3. Europe: the EU CSRD, and why 2026 changed everything

The EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) and its European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS) have been the most comprehensive mandatory sustainability reporting system in the world since they began to apply in 2024. That is still true, but the number of companies covered has shrunk a lot since the directive was first designed.

**What changed.** In February 2025, the European Commission proposed a simplification package known as "Omnibus I", after EU governments and businesses complained about the paperwork. After nearly a year of negotiation, the Omnibus I Directive (EU) 2026/470 was published on 26 February 2026 and came into force on 18 March 2026. It narrows CSRD's scope considerably:

- Mandatory reporting now applies to EU companies with **more than 1,000 employees and over €450 million** in net turnover. That is a big jump from the original thresholds, which would have covered roughly 50,000 companies. Estimates suggest the new thresholds cut that number by a large majority.
- Non-EU companies are only covered if their EU turnover is above €450 million, from financial years starting in 2028 instead of the original 2027 date.
- Sector-specific ESRS standards, which were meant to add detail for industries such as farming or mining, have been dropped altogether.
- Companies no longer have to adopt a formal climate transition plan aligned with the Paris Agreement as part of their disclosure. They only have to describe their approach to one.
- EFRAG (the technical body that drafts the standards) is finalising a simpler version of ESRS, due for adoption in 2026, which aims to cut the number of mandatory data points by roughly 60%.
- Member states have until 19 March 2027 to write these changes into national law. Companies that started reporting under the original 2024 rules (the "Wave 1" companies) may or may not be able to step back out, depending on how their own country handles the change.

**Ireland specifically.** Ireland brought the original CSRD into Irish law on time, through the European Union (Corporate Sustainability Reporting) Regulations 2024. But the Irish version created some oddities in who is covered. The main one is a rule that automatically treats certain listed SMEs as "large companies", which pulls them into full CSRD reporting earlier, and in more depth, than the EU directive intended. According to IAASA, Ireland's audit regulator, only around 20 Irish companies had to report for financial year 2024 in the first wave. Most of them will probably stay in scope after Omnibus I. Whether the smaller Irish companies caught by the anomaly get relief depends on how quickly the Department of Enterprise, Trade and Employment updates the Irish regulations.

**The double materiality principle.** Whatever the final scope, this core idea survives Omnibus I unchanged. Companies in scope must look at materiality from two directions at once: how sustainability issues affect their own finances, and how their business affects people and the environment. This sets CSRD apart from investor-only frameworks like ISSB, which look at financial materiality alone.

**UK.** Outside the EU, the UK has gone its own way. It has formally endorsed UK Sustainability Reporting Standards (UK SRS), based closely on IFRS S1 and S2, which makes it one of the more decisive adopters of the ISSB baseline among major economies, though separate from the EU's CSRD.

## 4. United States: a state-level patchwork, with the federal rule in retreat

The US has moved in the opposite direction from Europe. The SEC's federal climate disclosure rule, finalised in March 2024, was challenged in court almost at once, and in 2026 the SEC itself proposed to scrap it. A final repeal is unlikely before late 2026 or 2027, but in practice the federal rule is not a live requirement that companies need to plan around.

Instead, **California has become the main source** of mandatory climate disclosure for large US companies, through two laws run by the California Air Resources Board (CARB):

- **SB 253 (Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act)**: requires US companies doing business in California with revenue over $1 billion to report their Scope 1 and Scope 2 greenhouse gas emissions, with Scope 3 following from 2027. The first reporting deadline, originally set for mid-2026, has been moved to later in the year while CARB finalised its regulations.
- **SB 261 (Climate-Related Financial Risk Act)**: requires companies with revenue over $500 million to report their climate-related financial risks, broadly along TCFD lines. Enforcement of SB 261 is paused while the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals rules on a legal challenge. SB 253 is not affected by that injunction and remains in force.

Other states, including New York, New Jersey, Colorado and Illinois, are considering similar laws modelled on California's, so state rules rather than federal ones look set to continue. Outside California, most large US companies that report on sustainability do so voluntarily, usually against GRI, SASB-derived metrics within IFRS S1, or CDP.

## 5. Asia-Pacific and other regions: steady, varied ISSB adoption

Outside Europe and the US, the ISSB standards have become the default reference, though the pace and form of adoption vary a lot by country:

- **Australia**: has adopted its own version, AASB S2, closely modelled on IFRS S2 and phased in by company size from 2025.
- **Japan, Hong Kong, Malaysia, Singapore**: have formally aligned with ISSB standards, usually through their stock exchanges or national standard-setters. Hong Kong's exchange requires ISSB-aligned climate disclosure from all listed companies.
- **Brazil**: its securities regulator has required ISSB-aligned climate disclosure from listed companies, in phases, from 2026, which makes Brazil one of the most influential adopters in Latin America.
- **Nigeria, Kenya, Turkey**: among the earlier adopters outside the largest economies, often requiring ISSB standards directly or through close local versions.
- **Canada**: has published its own IFRS-aligned standards (CSDS 1 and CSDS 2). They are voluntary for now, and mandatory use still depends on regulators acting.

Altogether, more than 30 jurisdictions have adopted or are actively moving towards ISSB standards. That is the clearest sign yet of a real, if slow, convergence on a single global baseline for investors, even as the EU keeps running its own separate and broader system alongside it.

## 6. Which standards are legally required, and which are just widely used

This distinction is worth getting right, because sustainability marketing often blurs "widely used" and "legally required".

**Legally required, depending on where you operate:**

- EU CSRD / ESRS, for large EU companies above the revised thresholds, and large non-EU companies with enough EU turnover.
- California SB 253 and SB 261, for large companies doing business in California above the relevant revenue thresholds.
- ISSB-based national standards, where they have been made mandatory (Australia, Hong Kong, Brazil for listed companies, and a growing list of others).
- UK Sustainability Reporting Standards, once they are formally required for UK-listed companies under FCA rules.

**Voluntary, but expected by investors, customers or supply chains:**

- GRI, the most widely used voluntary standard across company sizes and sectors.
- CDP, particularly for companies facing investor requests or supply chain pressure from large customers.
- TCFD-style climate risk disclosure, now mostly absorbed into ISSB but still named directly in several jurisdictions, including as an accepted format under California's SB 261.
- TNFD, for companies that want to extend climate-style risk disclosure to nature and biodiversity. It is increasingly relevant to CSRD companies because of its formal mapping to ESRS.
- B Corp certification, mostly among smaller and privately owned companies that want to make a public commitment to social and environmental standards.

## 7. Choosing a standard

The short answer to "which standard should we use?" is that it depends on where a company operates and who is asking. A large EU company still needs to take CSRD seriously, even with its scope much reduced. A large US company doing business in California needs to take SB 253 and SB 261 seriously, whatever happens to the federal SEC rule. Everyone else is choosing, mostly voluntarily, between GRI for broad reporting to all stakeholders, ISSB-aligned standards if investors are the main audience, and CDP if the request comes from investors or a large customer's supply chain programme.

The work to bring ISSB, GRI, CDP, TNFD and the Transition Plan Taskforce closer together is real progress, and the TNFD-ESRS mapping is a useful concrete example. But a single global standard is still some years away. In 2026 the paths actually moved apart in places, with the EU simplifying its rules just as several other jurisdictions extended theirs.
