# Ireland's Data Centre Dilemma

Ireland has become one of the world's most concentrated hubs for data centres, and the sector now sits at the centre of the country's energy and climate debate. This page brings together the key facts on how much power data centres use, why AI is accelerating that demand, what it is costing Irish households, and how policy is responding.

## Why This Matters

Digital activity, including AI, does not sit comfortably alongside climate goals. Software and digital infrastructure account for close to **4% of global greenhouse gas emissions today**, a figure forecast to climb to **14% by 2040**, driven mainly by AI ([Harvard Business Review, 2020](https://hbr.org/2020/09/how-green-is-your-software)). At the same time, AI has real potential to strengthen resilience to climate change and benefit the bottom line, for example by predicting extreme weather impacts on businesses, optimising supply chains to cut emissions, and identifying new environmentally friendly and profitable solutions ([Economist Impact, 2021](https://impact.economist.com/perspectives/sustainability/green-intelligence-ai-could-boost-efforts-fight-climate-change)). The challenge is choosing how and why the technology gets used, not whether it is used at all.

A few figures frame the debate in Ireland specifically:

- Data centres do not create large numbers of jobs relative to their footprint, which undermines the usual economic case for hosting them.
- In Ireland, data centres already consume close to a fifth of all electricity, a share projected to keep rising.
- Data centres have been responsible for **88% of the total growth in Ireland's electricity demand over the past decade**.

## The Global Picture: Efficiency That Drives More Consumption

Data centres worldwide are expected to increase their power demand by **165% by 2030**, according to Goldman Sachs, with a shorter-term increase of **50% by 2027**. This is not happening despite efficiency improvements, it is happening because of them: a textbook case of the **Jevons paradox**, where making a technology more efficient makes it cheaper to use, so it ends up being used far more. NVIDIA's newest chips may be around 20 times more energy-efficient per unit of computation than older processors, but they also draw roughly **300% more power** individually, because they are being deployed in ever-larger "AI factories" training ever-larger models rather than simply doing the same work for less energy. AI workloads, which currently make up around 14% of data centre power use, are projected to reach **27% by 2027**, with infrastructure growing faster than the clean energy needed to power it.

## Ireland: A Case Study in Unintended Consequences

Ireland shows what happens when this global growth trajectory collides with a small national grid. Data centre electricity consumption **jumped 31% in a single year (2022)**, taking their share of national electricity to around 18-20%; the IEA projected this would reach **32% by 2026**.

The crucial point is that data centres are absorbing Ireland's renewable energy progress rather than adding to it. Since 2015, nearly all growth in Ireland's electricity demand has come from data centres, meaning the wind farms being built are largely keeping up with new AI-driven demand rather than displacing fossil fuels. Between 2020 and 2023, only 16% of the increase in data centre demand was matched by new renewable energy purchases; the rest came from the standard grid mix, keeping Ireland dependent on gas and coal.

The scale involved is striking: proposed new data centres could require up to **5,800 MW of power**, almost equal to Ireland's entire peak demand of around 6,000 MW. In response, new data centres in the Dublin area are now required to build their own power generation. Because renewables are intermittent and nuclear power is years away as an option, this has typically meant gas-powered generators, effectively forcing facilities to build private fossil-fuel power systems because the national grid cannot absorb them.

## Built in All the Wrong Places

Globally, data centre siting has a climate problem of its own: nearly **7,000 of the world's 8,808 data centres** are built in climates too hot for efficient cooling. Countries such as Singapore, Nigeria and the UAE have 100% of their data centres in these "wrong climate" zones. This happens because facilities are sited where data sovereignty laws require data to be stored, not where the climate makes environmental sense, forcing heavy energy use for cooling in places like the Arizona desert or humid tropical regions, rather than in naturally cold climates such as Scandinavia.

Power availability has also become the dominant siting factor: it can now take four years or more to extend transmission lines to a new site in major hubs, so developers increasingly choose locations based on where power lines already exist rather than whether the location makes sense. Ireland's cool climate and European location are genuine advantages here, but they do not remove the underlying grid and siting pressures.

## The Hidden Water Cost

The water debate around AI is often dismissed at a global level, and with some justification: a single AI query uses only around 2 millilitres of water, so it would take roughly 8,000 prompts a day to increase an individual's water footprint by just 1%, and AI water use is tiny compared with agriculture. But water, unlike electricity, cannot simply be transmitted across borders, so the real risk is local. Data centres are increasingly sited in water-stressed areas such as Arizona and the Thames Valley, where a single large facility can strain an entire watershed and compete directly with drinking water and farming.

There are two distinct types of water use: **direct use** for cooling, which evaporates and does not return to local water sources, and **indirect use** at the power plants generating the electricity, which accounts for around 40% of all US freshwater withdrawals. As AI drives up energy demand, it drives up water withdrawals at power plants too, and the shift towards more powerful chips requiring liquid cooling is increasing direct water use as well. In the UK, where water might be assumed to be abundant, there is growing concern about summer water access; without a requirement for data centres to report water usage, as other industries must, regulators are effectively flying blind, and some analysts warn of serious water shortages as soon as 2030. As with energy, there is a case for requiring an assessment of local water supply before new data centres are approved, with limits on consumption and farming needs prioritised.

## Regulatory Response: The New Connection Rules

In December 2025, the Commission for Regulation of Utilities (CRU) lifted the effective five-year moratorium on new data centre grid connections in the Greater Dublin area. New data centres can now connect provided they become **energy neutral after six years** (meaning they must build their own on-site power generation) and source **80% of their electricity from renewables**, the strictest renewable requirement of its kind in Europe. Three environmental groups have since been granted High Court permission to challenge these rules, arguing that the six-year fossil-fuel window before energy neutrality kicks in is too permissive.

## The Hidden Data Centre Tax (May 2026)

A report commissioned by Friends of the Earth Ireland and Beyond Fossil Fuels quantified what many had suspected: Ireland's data centre boom has been directly raising household electricity bills. Data centres used **22% of Ireland's electricity in 2025**, more than all of the country's urban homes combined (Central Statistics Office). The mechanism is that data centres carry high, inflexible demand that increases the number of hours in which gas-fired plants set the electricity market price. This has raised household bills by a cumulative average of **€360 per household between 2015 and 2023**, and is estimated to have drained around **€715 million from the Irish economy**.

Looking ahead, households face a further estimated **€295-€644 cumulative increase between 2025 and 2034**, putting the national total at **€633 million to €1.43 billion**. The industry disputes the findings, pointing to €18 billion in investment and the requirement for new data centres to source 80% of their energy from additional renewables. Beyond Fossil Fuels argues that Ireland's experience is "a warning for Europe: letting big tech expand datacentres unchecked will have massive ripple effects on the economy and European households."

## The Scale of the Sector (June 2026)

The latest Bitpower analysis gives the clearest picture yet of where Ireland's data centre sector stands:

- **107 data centres** now operating across Ireland, run by 32 operators
- A further 9 under construction and 43 with planning permission, a potential total of around 160
- Combined IT load of **1.3 GW**
- Hyperscale operators (AWS, Microsoft, Google, Meta) account for around **75%** of total computing capacity
- Total investment to date: **€18.5 billion**
- EirGrid's median forecast is that data centres will use **31% of all national electricity by 2034**, up from 22% in 2024

Central Bank of Ireland figures also show a sharp spike in AI infrastructure buildout: AI and data centre hardware imports from Taiwan quadrupled in six months, from €0.5 billion in Q3 2025 to €2.2 billion in Q1 2026, driven by a small number of multinationals. Despite the sector's scale, there is still no official census of data centres in Ireland.

## Dublin's Local Concentration

Local reporting from January 2026 sharpens the picture within Dublin specifically. Data centres account for around **7% of Dublin city's emissions**, and data centres plus other commercial facilities together make up **46% of south Dublin county's emissions**, by far the largest emitting slice in that local authority. In September 2025, South Dublin County Council passed a motion calling for a nationwide data centre ban. Councillor Jess Spear (Tallaght Central) framed the vote at the time: "All the indications suggest this government is not going to take climate change seriously, and none more than their approach towards data centres."

Central government sits on the other side of the debate. Taoiseach Micheál Martin has rejected what he called "the demonisation of data centres" and said a moratorium on AI is not on the table. Former Green Party leader Eamon Ryan takes a more optimistic view of the same tension, arguing that if Ireland can develop zero-emissions data centres, the state will have "skills the rest of the world needs."

## Case Study: Amazon's Dublin Campus

Amazon received planning approval for three data centres, with an Environmental Impact Statement lodged for the scheme stating that permitted and future indicative development at the campus would consume **219.7 MW of power** and produce **607,523 tonnes of CO2 per year**. While Amazon has stated that renewable energy purchases will offset the campus's footprint, critics argue that the concept of "offsetting" carbon dioxide in this way is inherently misleading, giving the false impression that the development will not be damaging to the environment.
