# Ireland's Data Centre Dilemma

Ireland has become one of the world's busiest hubs for data centres, and the sector is now at the heart of the country's energy and climate debate. This page brings together the main facts: how much power data centres use, why AI is pushing that demand up, what it is costing Irish households, and how policy is responding.

## Why it matters

Digital activity, including AI, sits uneasily alongside climate goals. Software and digital infrastructure produce close to **4% of global greenhouse gas emissions** today, and that share is forecast to climb to 14% by 2040, driven mainly by AI ([Harvard Business Review, 2020](https://hbr.org/2020/09/how-green-is-your-software)). At the same time, AI could help businesses cope with climate change and save money. It can predict how extreme weather will affect a business, organise supply chains to cut emissions, and help find new solutions that are both greener and profitable ([Economist Impact, 2021](https://impact.economist.com/perspectives/sustainability/green-intelligence-ai-could-boost-efforts-fight-climate-change)). The real question is how and why the technology gets used, not whether it is used at all.

A few figures frame the debate in Ireland:

- Data centres create few jobs for the space and power they take up, which weakens the usual economic case for hosting them.
- In Ireland, data centres already use almost a quarter of all metered electricity (23% in 2025, according to the Central Statistics Office), and that share is expected to keep rising.
- Data centres account for **88% of the total growth** in Ireland's electricity demand over the past decade.

## The global picture: efficiency that drives more consumption

Goldman Sachs expects data centres worldwide to increase their power demand by **165% by 2030**, with a shorter-term rise of 50% by 2027. This is happening because of efficiency improvements, not in spite of them. It is a textbook case of the Jevons paradox: when a technology becomes more efficient, it becomes cheaper to use, so people end up using far more of it. NVIDIA's newest chips may be around 20 times more energy-efficient per unit of computing than older processors. But each one draws roughly 300% more power, because they are going into ever-larger "AI factories" that train ever-larger models, rather than doing the same work for less energy. AI currently makes up around 14% of data centre power use, and that is expected to reach 27% by 2027. The infrastructure is growing faster than the clean energy needed to run it.

## Ireland: a case study in unintended consequences

Ireland shows what happens when this global growth meets a small national grid. Data centre electricity use **jumped 31% in a single year (2022)**, taking their share of national electricity to 18%. The IEA projected in 2024 that this could reach 32% by 2026. That now looks too high: the Central Statistics Office measured 23% in 2025, and EirGrid's central forecast (below) is 31% by 2034.

The key point is that data centres are soaking up Ireland's progress on renewable energy rather than adding to it. Since 2015, around 88% of the growth in Ireland's electricity demand has come from data centres. That means the new wind farms are mostly keeping pace with new AI-driven demand rather than replacing fossil fuels. Between 2020 and 2023, only 16% of the rise in data centre demand was matched by new renewable energy purchases. The rest came from the normal grid mix, which keeps Ireland reliant on gas and coal.

The numbers are large. Proposed new data centres could need up to **5,800 MW** of power, almost as much as Ireland's entire peak demand of around 6,000 MW. In response, new data centres in the Dublin area must now build their own power generation. Because wind and solar do not produce power all the time, and nuclear power is years away as an option, this has usually meant gas-powered generators. In effect, facilities are being pushed to build private fossil-fuel power plants because the national grid cannot take on their demand.

## Built in all the wrong places

Around the world, where data centres are built is a climate problem in its own right. Nearly **7,000 of the world's 8,808 data centres** are in climates too hot for efficient cooling. In countries such as Singapore, Nigeria and the UAE, 100% of data centres are in these "wrong climate" zones. This happens because data sovereignty laws decide where data must be stored, so facilities are built there rather than where the climate makes sense. The result is heavy energy use for cooling in places like the Arizona desert or humid tropical regions, instead of naturally cold places such as Scandinavia.

Access to power has also become the main factor in choosing a site. In major hubs it can now take four years or more to extend power lines to a new site, so developers increasingly pick locations where power lines already exist, whether or not the location makes sense otherwise. Ireland's cool climate and European location are real advantages here, but they do not remove the underlying pressure on the grid and on where to build.

## The hidden water cost

At a global level, worries about AI's water use are often brushed aside, with some reason. A single AI query uses only around 2 millilitres of water, so it would take roughly 8,000 prompts a day to raise one person's water footprint by just 1%, and AI's water use is tiny compared with farming. But unlike electricity, water cannot simply be sent across borders, so the real risk is local. Data centres are increasingly built in areas short of water, such as Arizona and the Thames Valley, where a single large facility can strain a whole river catchment and compete directly with drinking water and farming.

There are two kinds of water use. **Direct use** is water for cooling, which evaporates and does not return to local supplies. Indirect use happens at the power plants that make the electricity, and power plants account for around 40% of all US freshwater withdrawals. As AI pushes up energy demand, it pushes up water use at power plants too, and the move to higher-performance chips that need liquid cooling is raising direct water use as well. Even in the UK, where water might seem plentiful, there is growing concern about water supplies in summer. Data centres do not have to report their water use, unlike other industries, so regulators are working without the full picture, and some analysts warn of serious water shortages as soon as 2030. As with energy, there is a case for checking local water supply before new data centres are approved, with limits on how much they use and farming needs put first.

## Regulatory response: the new connection rules

In December 2025, the Commission for Regulation of Utilities (CRU) lifted what had in effect been a five-year ban on connecting new data centres to the grid in the Greater Dublin area. New data centres can now connect if they become **energy neutral after six years**, meaning they must build their own on-site power generation, and get 80% of their electricity from renewables. That is the strictest renewable requirement of its kind in Europe. Three environmental groups have since been given permission by the High Court to challenge these rules. They argue that the six years allowed before energy neutrality, during which fossil fuels can be used, is too lenient.

## The hidden data centre tax (May 2026)

A report commissioned by Friends of the Earth Ireland and Beyond Fossil Fuels put a figure on what many had suspected: Ireland's data centre boom has been pushing up household electricity bills. Data centres used **22% of Ireland's metered electricity in 2024**, more than all the country's urban homes combined, and 23% in 2025 (Central Statistics Office). Their demand is high and steady, which increases the number of hours in which gas-fired power plants set the electricity price. This has added a cumulative average of **€360 per household between 2015 and 2023** to bills, and is estimated to have drained around €715 million from the Irish economy.

Looking ahead, households face a further estimated **€295-€644** in cumulative increases between 2025 and 2034, bringing the national total to €633 million to €1.43 billion. The industry disputes the findings, pointing to €18 billion in investment and the rule that new data centres must get 80% of their energy from additional renewables. Beyond Fossil Fuels argues that Ireland's experience is "a warning for Europe: letting big tech expand datacentres unchecked will have massive ripple effects on the economy and European households."

## The scale of the sector (June 2026)

The latest Bitpower analysis gives the clearest picture yet of Ireland's data centre sector:

- **107 data centres** now operating across Ireland, run by 32 operators
- A further 9 under construction and 43 with planning permission, a possible total of around 160
- Combined IT load of **1.3 GW**
- Hyperscale operators (AWS, Microsoft, Google, Meta) account for around **75%** of total computing capacity
- Total investment so far: **€18.5 billion**
- EirGrid's central forecast is that data centres will use **31% of all national electricity by 2034**, up from 22% in 2024

Central Bank of Ireland figures also show a sharp rise in building for AI. Imports of AI and data centre hardware from Taiwan **quadrupled in six months**, from €0.5 billion in Q3 2025 to €2.2 billion in Q1 2026, driven by a small number of multinationals. Despite the sector's size, there is still no official count of data centres in Ireland.

## Dublin's local concentration

Local reporting from January 2026 shows the picture within Dublin more closely. Data centres account for around **7% of Dublin city's emissions**. Data centres and other commercial buildings together make up 46% of south Dublin county's emissions, by far the largest share in that local authority. In September 2025, South Dublin County Council passed a motion calling for a nationwide ban on data centres. Councillor Jess Spear (Tallaght Central) said at the time: "All the indications suggest this government is not going to take climate change seriously, and none more than their approach towards data centres."

Central government takes the opposite view. Taoiseach Micheál Martin has rejected what he called "the demonisation of data centres" and said a moratorium on AI is not on the table. Former Green Party leader Eamon Ryan is more hopeful about the same tension. He argues that if Ireland can develop zero-emissions data centres, the state will have "skills the rest of the world needs."

## Case study: Amazon's Dublin campus

Amazon received planning approval for three data centres. The Environmental Impact Statement lodged for the scheme says that permitted and planned future development at the campus would use **219.7 MW of power** and produce 607,523 tonnes of CO2 a year. Amazon says renewable energy purchases will offset the campus's footprint. Critics argue that "offsetting" carbon dioxide in this way is misleading by nature, because it gives the false impression that the development will do no harm to the environment.
