# Ireland Land Use, Soil and Forestry

Ireland's soils, forests and peatlands sit at the centre of its climate strategy. Depending on how they are managed, they can soak up carbon or release it. This page covers the state of Irish soil science, forestry policy and planting targets, peatland restoration, and a few notable projects and controversies, including a 2026 greenwashing case involving the forestry industry and Irish primary schools.

## Sitka Spruce Plantations: A Greenwashing Case Study

In 2026, a children's book called *Sitka Spruce – the Amazing Timber Tree* was sent to Irish primary schools. Told from the point of view of a Sitka spruce, it showed commercial plantations as thriving ecosystems full of wildlife. It carried a foreword by Michael Healy-Rae, then minister of state for forestry.

> The Irish Wildlife Trust called for the book's removal, pointing out that Sitka spruce plantations are widely regarded as **ecological dead zones**: they crowd out native plants, support very little wildlife, acidify soils and waterways, and are routinely clear-felled rather than selectively harvested, contrary to what the book implies. Its claim that Sitka spruce stores carbon effectively is also disputed in parts of Ireland where plantations are grown on bogs that would store far more carbon if left undrained.

The book was paid for by the Society of Irish Foresters, the Irish Timber Council and SEEFA. The Department of Education said it played no part in approving or endorsing material sent straight to schools. That reveals a gap in the system: nothing currently stops an industry from sending promotional material into classrooms as "environmental education." It is a clear Irish example of [Greenwashing](../concepts/key-ideas/Greenwashing.md), with commercial messaging presented to children as fact.

## Soil Management

The Soil Science Society of Ireland (SSSI) is an all-Ireland body for people working and studying in soil science. It promotes research, shares knowledge and encourages sustainable soil management through scientific meetings, field trips and discussion forums.

The Irish Soil Information System project has produced a national soil association map at a scale of 1:250,000. Alongside it sits a digital system with spatial and numerical information on soil types and properties across the country. Together, the [Irish Soil Information System](http://gis.teagasc.ie/soils/index.php) and the [SSSI](https://www.ucd.ie/sssi/) give a good picture of the actual state of Ireland's soils, which underpin both farming and carbon storage.

## Forestry in Ireland

The Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021 commits Ireland to cutting emissions by 51% by 2030 and reaching net zero no later than 2050. Forests have a big part to play in getting there.

Forestry currently covers **11.6% of Ireland's land area** (808,848 hectares, according to the National Forest Inventory 2022, as reported in Forest Statistics - Ireland 2026, published in August 2026). That is up from around 1% at the start of the last century but still far below the **EU average of 39.8%**. Forests, wetlands and bogs remove carbon dioxide from the air and store it, helping to reduce climate change while also providing cleaner air and water and supporting a large diversity of plant and animal species. They also provide timber as a renewable resource, and contribute to air, soil and water quality more broadly.

<!-- tag: eu -->
The EU Forest Strategy for 2030 sets out a vision in which European forests work with nature and the environment while still bringing economic and social benefits to the communities around them.

Ireland's national forest estate is also a large **carbon reservoir**. It held an estimated 312 million tonnes of carbon in 2017 and captures around 3 million tonnes a year on average. Ireland has a busy conifer industry, and wood is increasingly seen as part of the answer to the housing crisis. Conifers grow quickly, and different parts of the tree have different uses. Steel and concrete produce a lot of carbon to make, so wood offers a lower-carbon, recyclable alternative for building.

### Climate Pressures on Forest Management

Forestry is a long game. Irish forests usually run on rotations of 40 to 50 years from planting to final harvest, and climate change is expected to have a big effect within that time. Forest managers need to adopt practices that help forests cope with a changing climate. Teagasc's [Forest Statistics Ireland 2020](https://www.teagasc.ie/media/website/crops/forestry/advice/Forest-Statistics-Ireland-2020.pdf) and the [Climate Ireland forestry sector tool](https://www.climateireland.ie/#!/tools/sectors/forestry) both track how rising CO2 levels and temperatures affect forest growth.

Land use, land-use change and forestry are core parts of the Climate Action Plan, which aims to cut emissions from this sector by **37-58% by 2030**. The measures include:

- Keeping existing afforestation plans, extending rotations and improving forest management, with a planting rate of 8,000 hectares a year by 2030 to store more carbon
- A new Forestry Programme, launched in 2023, focused on climate-smart forestry
- Growing cover crops on at least 50,000 hectares of tillage land by 2030
- Keeping deforestation below 900 hectares a year by 2030
- Rehabilitating 65,000 hectares of peatlands (the National Energy and Climate Plan 2021-2030 updated plan, published in July 2024, sets a CAP 2024 target of rehabilitating 33,000 hectares of exploited peatlands)
- Better carbon management on at least 450,000 hectares of grassland on mineral soils
- Designating 30% of Ireland's marine area as a Marine Protected Area

### Afforestation Is a Top Policy Priority, but Progress Is Slow

Land that stores carbon today will start releasing it as older forests are harvested, so planting new forest is a high priority. A fall in planting rates has been flagged as a major obstacle to Ireland's climate goals. Incentives such as a carbon credit system have been proposed to help close the gap on the target of 8,000 hectares a year.

### Is Ireland's Forest a Carbon Source or Sink?

Ireland's forests are not yet a net source of greenhouse gases. But "managed forest land" is expected to become a small net emitter over the coming years, because a large share of the forest estate is reaching harvesting age (over 30 years old). The Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) sector covers forest land, cropland, grassland, wetlands, settlements, other land and harvested wood products. It was earlier projected to produce net emissions of 9.7 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent by 2030, a 31.7% increase on 2021, mostly because of this expected harvesting. The EPA's Ireland's Greenhouse Gas Emissions Projections 2025-2030, published in May 2026, now projects net emissions of 5.2 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent in 2030 under the With Existing Measures scenario. There is more detail on the [EPA's LULUCF pages](https://www.epa.ie/our-services/monitoring--assessment/climate-change/ghg/lulucf/) and in the [National Climate Action Plan 2025](https://assets.gov.ie/static/documents/c491032e/DECC_Climate_Action_Plan_2025_Main_Report_-_Final_Web.pdf) (the LULUCF plan is on page 127).

### Forestry Grant Scheme (2023)

The government approved a €45 million scheme to help landowners manage forests in ways that are economically, ecologically and socially sustainable. It supports forest growth, protects biodiversity, soil and water quality, helps forests adapt to climate change, and aims to increase how much carbon they capture.

## Peatlands and Bog Restoration

Ireland's peatlands are among its most important carbon stores. The **Enhanced Decommissioning, Rehabilitation and Restoration Scheme (EDRRS)** is funded with up to €108 million from the EU's Recovery and Resilience Facility. It is rehabilitating 33,000 hectares of peatland across 82 former Bord na Móna bogs, which were once stripped of peat to fuel power stations. It is the largest bog rehabilitation programme in the state's history, combining engineering and ecology to help nature recover faster. From 2021, around 350 Bord na Móna workers moved from peat harvesting and haulage jobs to running the EDRRS.

Once rehabilitated, these sites will include peat-forming bogs and a patchwork of wetlands, grasslands and native woodland. They will protect an estimated 100 million tonnes of stored carbon, support more wildlife, and help Ireland towards carbon neutrality by 2050. This comes on top of 41,000 hectares already rehabilitated. The Climate Action Plan aims to rehabilitate **35,900 hectares** through the EDRRS and LIFE People and Peatlands programmes, plus 41,700 hectares of other peatland, for a total of 77,600 hectares by 2030.

Separately, the National Parks and Wildlife Service has sped up restoration of protected raised bogs. The Department of Housing, Local Government and Heritage (gov.ie), in February 2022, reported that since 2018, with more funding and staff, over **5,000 hectares** of protected raised bog had been restored or were being actively restored. An earlier figure was around 4,000 hectares of completed restoration work. More is planned on raised bog Special Areas of Conservation and Natural Heritage Areas. This work supports Ireland's national target of bringing the raised bogs in the network back to a favourable conservation status.

## Innovative Approaches: The Miyawaki Method

A small community forestry trial in Co. Galway has shown that dense forest can grow very quickly in Ireland without digging, as long as the ground is prepared with manure and nutrients a year before planting. A group in Kinvara used the **Miyawaki method**, developed by the Japanese botanist Akira Miyawaki. It grows forests up to ten times faster than the 200 to 300 years a typical forest takes. Many native tree and shrub species are planted very close together (often five saplings per square metre), so the trees race upwards for light instead of spreading out. Miyawaki forests are far denser than normal forest, around 30 times better at absorbing carbon dioxide, and can draw in plenty of wildlife in a short time.

The Kinvara group tested four approaches side by side:
- a full Miyawaki plot, with the soil dug up and enriched with nutrients, mulch, composted horse manure and seaweed, plus extra soil depth
- a plot planted at the same density with nothing added to the soil
- a conventional control plot, with one tree per square metre and nothing added
- a plot where the soil was enriched a year ahead, simply by spreading horse manure, seaweed, cardboard and straw on top and letting it rot down, with no digging

The fully prepared Miyawaki plot did very well. But the last plot, enriched without any digging, gave the best results of all.

Irish attitudes to forestry are broadly positive. A 2022 survey by Behaviour and Attitudes, based on face-to-face interviews with 1,000 adults, found that three in four people want more forests in Ireland.

## Beyond Ireland: The Amazon

The Amazon is still the reference point in most conversations about forests worldwide. A widely read Guardian piece set out seven steps to save it, from holding meat consumption to account to wider policy reform. A more technical development is the use of solar-powered robots to speed up reforestation. In an early-stage project, a single robot could potentially plant around 600 trees in one morning, reaching remote spots that are hard for people to get to.
