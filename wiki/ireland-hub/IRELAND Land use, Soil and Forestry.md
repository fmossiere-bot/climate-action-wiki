# Ireland Land Use, Soil and Forestry

Ireland's soils, forests and peatlands are central to its climate strategy: they can act as carbon sinks or carbon sources depending on how they are managed. This page covers the state of Irish soil science, forestry policy and afforestation targets, peatland restoration schemes, and a few notable projects and controversies, including a 2026 greenwashing case involving a commercial forestry company and Irish primary schools.

## Sitka Spruce Plantations: A Greenwashing Case Study

In 2026, a children's book titled *Sitka Spruce – the Amazing Timber Tree* was distributed to Irish primary schools. Written from the perspective of a Sitka spruce tree, it depicted commercial plantations as thriving ecosystems full of wildlife, and carried a foreword by Michael Healy-Rae, then minister of state for forestry.

> The Irish Wildlife Trust called for the book's removal, pointing out that Sitka spruce plantations are widely regarded as **ecological dead zones**: they crowd out native plants, support very little wildlife, acidify soils and waterways, and are routinely clear-felled rather than selectively harvested, contrary to what the book implies. Its claim that Sitka spruce stores carbon effectively is also disputed in parts of Ireland where plantations are grown on bogs that would store far more carbon if left undrained.

The book was funded by the Society of Irish Foresters, the Irish Timber Council and SEEFA. The Department of Education said it had no role in approving or endorsing material sent directly to schools, exposing a systemic gap: there is currently no safeguard preventing industry from distributing promotional content to classrooms as "environmental education." This is a clear Irish example of [Greenwashing](../concepts/key-ideas/Greenwashing.md), commercially motivated messaging presented to children as fact.

## Soil Management

The Soil Science Society of Ireland (SSSI) is an all-Ireland body for professionals and students working in soil science, promoting research, knowledge transfer and sustainable soil management through scientific meetings, field trips and discussion forums.

The Irish Soil Information System project has developed a national soil association map for Ireland at a scale of 1:250,000, together with a digital soil information system that provides spatial and quantitative information on soil types and properties across the country. Between them, the [Irish Soil Information System](http://gis.teagasc.ie/soils/index.php) and the [SSSI](https://www.ucd.ie/sssi/) give a good sense of the actual state of Ireland's soils, which underpin both agricultural productivity and carbon storage.

## Forestry in Ireland

The Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021 commits Ireland to a 51% reduction in emissions by 2030 and to net-zero emissions no later than 2050, and forests have a key role to play in that transformation.

> Forestry currently covers just **10% of Ireland's land area**, up from around 1% at the start of the last century but still far below the **EU average of 39.8%**. Forests, wetlands and bogs remove carbon dioxide from the air and store it, helping to reduce climate change while also providing cleaner air and water and supporting a large diversity of plant and animal species. They also provide timber as a renewable resource, and contribute to air, soil and water quality more broadly.

The EU Forest Strategy for 2030 sets an ambitious vision for European forests to be fully compatible with, and supportive of, nature and the environment, while also delivering economic and social benefits to communities.

Ireland's national forest estate is also a significant **carbon reservoir**, holding an estimated **312 million tonnes of carbon in 2017**, with an average of around 3 million tonnes captured per year. Ireland has a thriving conifer industry, and wood is increasingly seen as a solution to the housing crisis: conifer grows quickly and different parts of the tree can be used for multiple purposes. Since steel and concrete are highly carbon-intensive to produce, wood is both a lower-carbon and recyclable alternative for construction.

### Climate Pressures on Forest Management

Forestry is a long-term undertaking. Irish forests are generally worked on rotation periods of 40 to 50 years between planting and final harvest, and within that timeframe climate change is projected to have a significant impact. Introducing and adapting management practices that strengthen forests' resilience to climate change is therefore critical. Teagasc's [Forest Statistics Ireland 2020](https://www.teagasc.ie/media/website/crops/forestry/advice/Forest-Statistics-Ireland-2020.pdf) and the [Climate Ireland forestry sector tool](https://www.climateireland.ie/#!/tools/sectors/forestry) both track how rising CO2 concentrations and temperatures affect forest growth.

Land use, land-use change and forestry are key elements of the Climate Action Plan, which targets a **37-58% reduction in emissions from this sector by 2030** through measures including:

- Maintaining existing afforestation plans, extending rotations and improving forestry management, with a planting rate of **8,000 hectares a year by 2030** to increase carbon sequestration
- A new Forestry Programme, launched in 2023, focused on climate-smart forestry
- Increasing the inclusion of cover crops in tillage to at least 50,000 hectares by 2030
- Limiting deforestation to less than 900 hectares a year by 2030
- Rehabilitating 65,000 hectares of peatlands
- Improving carbon sequestration management on at least 450,000 hectares of grassland on mineral soils
- Designating 30% of Ireland's marine area as a Marine Protected Area

### Afforestation Is a Top Policy Priority, but Progress Is Slow

Because land that is currently a carbon sink will soon become a carbon source as older forests are harvested, planting new forest is a high priority. A decline in the rate of forest planting has been flagged as a major obstacle to Ireland's climate goals, and building incentives such as a carbon credit system has been proposed to help close the gap on the 8,000 hectares a year target.

### Is Ireland's Forest a Carbon Source or Sink?

Ireland's forests are not currently a net emitter of greenhouse gases, but "managed forest land" is projected to become a small net emitter over the coming years because a large share of the forest estate is now reaching harvesting age (over 30 years old). The Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) sector, which covers forest land, cropland, grassland, wetlands, settlements, other land and harvested wood products, is projected to have net emissions of 9.7 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent by 2030, an increase of 31.7% compared with 2021, largely because of this expected harvesting. More detail is available from the [EPA's LULUCF pages](https://www.epa.ie/our-services/monitoring--assessment/climate-change/ghg/lulucf/) and the [National Climate Action Plan 2025](https://assets.gov.ie/static/documents/c491032e/DECC_Climate_Action_Plan_2025_Main_Report_-_Final_Web.pdf) (see page 127 for the LULUCF plan).

### Forestry Grant Scheme (2023)

The government approved a €45 million support scheme to help landowners put forest management in place that is economically, ecologically and socially sustainable. The plan promotes forest growth, protects biodiversity, soil and water quality, helps forests adapt to climate change, and aims to increase forests' ability to capture carbon.

## Peatlands and Bog Restoration

Ireland's peatlands are one of its most important carbon stores. The **Enhanced Decommissioning, Rehabilitation and Restoration Scheme (EDRRS)**, funded with up to €108 million through the EU's Recovery and Resilience Facility, is rehabilitating **33,000 hectares of peatlands across 82 former Bord na Móna bogs** that were previously used for peat extraction for electricity generation. It is the largest bog rehabilitation programme in the state's history, combining engineering and ecology work to encourage and accelerate natural regeneration. From 2021, around 350 Bord na Móna employees transitioned from peat harvesting and haulage roles to operating the EDRRS.

Once rehabilitated, these peatlands will include peat-forming bogs and a mosaic of wetlands, grasslands and native woodlands, protecting an estimated 100 million tonnes of stored carbon, enhancing biodiversity, and supporting Ireland's target of carbon neutrality by 2050. This is in addition to 41,000 hectares already rehabilitated. The Climate Action Plan sets a target of rehabilitating **35,900 hectares** through the EDRRS and LIFE People and Peatlands programmes, plus **41,700 hectares** of additional peatlands, for a total of **77,600 hectares rehabilitated by 2030**.

Separately, the National Parks and Wildlife Service has significantly accelerated restoration of protected raised bogs. Since 2018, with increased funding and resources, restoration measures have been completed on around **4,000 hectares** of designated raised bogs, with further work on raised bog Special Areas of Conservation and Natural Heritage Areas planned. These efforts support Ireland's national conservation target of restoring raised bogs in the network to a favourable conservation status.

## Innovative Approaches: The Miyawaki Method

A small community forestry experiment in Co. Galway has shown that dense forest can be grown very rapidly in Ireland without digging, provided the ground is primed with manure and nutrients a year ahead of planting. A group in Kinvara followed the Miyawaki method, pioneered by Japanese botanist Akira Miyawaki, which grows forests up to ten times faster than the 200 to 300 years a typical forest takes. It works by planting various native tree and shrub species very close together (often five saplings per square metre), forcing the trees to grow upwards for sunlight rather than spreading outwards. Miyawaki forests are far denser than normal forest, around 30 times better at absorbing carbon dioxide, and can attract abundant wildlife in a short time.

The Kinvara group trialled four approaches side by side: a full Miyawaki plot (soil dug up and enhanced with nutrients, mulch, composted horse manure and seaweed, plus extra soil depth); a plot planted at the same density with no soil supplementation; a conventional control plot (one tree per square metre, no supplementation); and a plot where the soil was enhanced a year in advance simply by spreading horse manure, seaweed, cardboard and straw on top and letting it rot down naturally, without digging. The first, fully-prepared Miyawaki plot performed remarkably well, but it was the last plot, where the soil was enhanced without digging, that produced the most striking results.

General attitudes to forestry in Ireland are notably positive: a 2022 survey by Behaviour and Attitudes, based on face-to-face interviews with 1,000 adults, found that three in four people want to see more forests in Ireland.

## Beyond Ireland: The Amazon

The Amazon remains the reference point for most discussions of forests globally. A widely-read Guardian piece set out seven steps to save the Amazon, ranging from holding meat consumption accountable to broader policy reform. A related and more technical development is the use of solar-powered robots to accelerate reforestation: an early-stage project has shown that a single robot could potentially plant around 600 trees in one morning, reaching remote locations that are difficult for people to access.
