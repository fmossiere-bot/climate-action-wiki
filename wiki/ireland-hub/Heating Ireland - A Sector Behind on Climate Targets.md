### Summary

Heating is one of Ireland's most overlooked climate problems. It accounts for nearly a quarter of all national greenhouse gas emissions, it still runs mostly on oil and other fossil fuels, and its emissions have been rising since 2014. The SEAI National Heat Study, commissioned by the Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland, is the most thorough analysis so far of what it will take to decarbonise heating by 2050. Its conclusion is that current policy is not enough, and change needs to happen much faster.

_Source: [SEAI National Heat Study](https://www.seai.ie/data-and-insights/national-heat-study/key-insights)_

---

### Key numbers

- Heat-related CO2 emissions make up **38% of total energy-related CO2 emissions** in Ireland, or 24% of total national greenhouse gas emissions
- Emissions from heating have risen by **13% since 2014**
- **Oil** is the main heating fuel in Irish homes, meeting about 41% of household energy demand
- **Electricity** accounts for around 25% of home energy use, natural gas around 19%, and coal and peat a combined 15%
- Renewables supply just **6.8%** of Ireland's heat, compared with an EU average of 22%
- **Home heating** makes up around 50% of total heat demand
- Heat pumps are expected to cover **12 to 20%** of heating demand by 2030 and 33 to 38% by 2050
- District heating could meet up to **50% of building heating demand** in Ireland
- Ireland's legally binding target is to cut emissions by **51% from 2018 levels by 2030**, and to reach net zero by 2050

---

### Why heating is such a problem in Ireland

Ireland heats its homes and buildings differently from most of its European neighbours. Oil boilers are still by far the most common heating system, especially outside towns and cities. Natural gas is mostly found in cities. Peat and coal, both high-carbon fuels, still make up a sizeable share of the mix.

Cutting emissions from heating and cooling is hard, and the study finds that current climate action measures are unlikely to cut heat-related CO2 emissions enough. In electricity, wind and solar have made fast progress in Ireland, but heating has seen no comparable shift. Buildings have become more efficient, but rising demand for heat has cancelled out the gains, and there are still a very large number of oil boilers in use.

The problem is built into the system. Most heating systems last **15 to 20 years**, so what gets installed in homes, offices and factories today will lock in emissions for decades. That is why the study stresses acting now rather than waiting for better or cheaper technology.

---

### What needs to change

The study picks out several areas where action matters most.

**Heat pumps** are the central technology for buildings. In every scenario SEAI modelled, they lead the way in decarbonising homes and commercial buildings. They are more efficient than fossil fuel boilers and run on electricity, which is getting cleaner as Ireland adds more renewable power to the grid. The study finds that heat pumps will be essential for cutting emissions quickly, and that improving a home's energy efficiency before or alongside installation makes a big difference. Insulation, better windows and other improvements to the building fabric help heat pumps work better and lower running costs.

**District heating** has a lot of untapped potential, and this is one of the less discussed findings of the study. In a district heating system, a network of insulated pipes carries heat from a central source to many buildings. It could meet up to 50% of building heating demand in Ireland. It is already common in Scandinavia and parts of central Europe, but in Ireland it has barely been developed. The study calls for policies that remove the planning, regulatory and financing barriers holding it back.

**Bioenergy and biomethane** will play a supporting role. Sustainable bioenergy, including biomethane made from farm and food waste, can help cut emissions in parts of the heat sector that are harder to electrify, especially in industry and in rural areas not connected to the gas grid. However, the study is clear that sustainable biomethane is in limited supply and depends on how land is used. It cannot simply replace natural gas at scale.

The role of the **gas grid** will shrink. In every scenario modelled, use of the existing gas network falls as buildings switch to heat pumps and other low-carbon options. The grid's future depends on whether it can be adapted to carry biomethane or hydrogen, and that change needs to be planned. Connecting new buildings to gas now risks creating assets that become stranded, meaning they lose their value before the pipes reach the end of their working life.

**Fossil fuels** need a phase-out date. The study, published in 2022, said fossil fuels must be phased out faster, starting before 2025, and called for a clear timetable for ending their use in all kinds of heating. Without that signal, investment in low-carbon alternatives will be slower and less certain. One step has since been taken: from 1 January 2025, under EU building rules written into Irish law, public bodies can no longer give grants or other financial incentives for installing stand-alone oil, gas or coal boilers.

---

### The role of the electricity grid

One part of the heat transition that often gets overlooked is how much it depends on electricity. If Ireland is going to heat its buildings with heat pumps, it needs a grid that can supply enough clean electricity. The study is explicit that cleaning up the electricity grid is more than an energy sector goal. Heat cannot be decarbonised without it.

This links different parts of Ireland's climate strategy, which need to be managed together. Expanding offshore wind, improving the grid and adding battery storage all affect whether the switch to heat pumps actually lowers emissions.

---

### What this means for industry

Industry faces a different challenge from buildings. Many industrial processes need very high temperatures that heat pumps cannot reach. Equipment lasts longer, so companies get fewer chances to switch. And the cost of switching can be high.

The study finds that carbon capture, utilisation and storage (**CCUS**), which traps CO2 before it reaches the air, is likely to be needed in some industrial settings to fully decarbonise by 2050. This applies especially to sectors like cement and food processing, where switching fuel alone cannot remove all emissions. Ireland has yet to decide how CCUS will be regulated and supported.

---

### The investment picture

The investment needed is large. The study calls for major spending on energy efficiency, low-carbon heating and infrastructure upgrades. That money needs to flow into homes, businesses and industrial sites at the same time over the coming decades.

For households, the main barriers are the upfront cost, finding reliable contractors, and not knowing which option is best in the long run. SEAI already offers **grants** for heat pumps and home insulation, but far more people need to use them. The study stresses that policy needs to be long-term and stable. If homeowners and businesses are not confident that support schemes and market conditions will last, they will put off their decisions.

---

### Where Ireland stands against its own targets

Ireland has passed legally binding climate targets to reach net zero by 2050 and cut emissions by 51% from 2018 levels by 2030. These match its commitments under the Paris Agreement and the EU's goal of cutting emissions by **55% from 1990 levels by 2030**.

The heating sector is not currently delivering its fair share of those cuts. The study says an unprecedented increase in effort, and extra measures, are needed if heating is to play its part. It was published in 2022, so Ireland has already used up several of the years available before the 2030 deadline.

The way forward is clear. The gap between where the sector is and where it needs to be is large, but it can be closed. That needs faster roll-out of technologies that already exist, a clear end date for fossil fuel heating, and steady investment backed by credible long-term policy.
