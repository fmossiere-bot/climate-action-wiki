# Ireland Wind Energy

## What is it

Wind energy is at the heart of Ireland's plan to meet its 2030 and 2050 emissions goals, with bioenergy playing a supporting role in replacing natural gas (see [Ireland Energy and Biomass](IRELAND%20Energy%20and%20Biomass.md)). By 2030, Ireland aims to cut emissions from the energy industry by **60%** and get 80% of its electricity from renewables. Today the country has 4.5GW of renewable capacity. By 2050 it will need around 25GW. Electricity demand is expected to grow three to four times over, from 29TWh today to between 84 and 122TWh. Over the same period, emissions from Ireland's energy system, currently around 60 million tonnes, have to fall close to zero, even though the system is still dominated by fossil fuels.

## Why it matters

According to Energy in Ireland 2025, published by SEAI in December 2025, Ireland's renewable electricity share (RES-E) was 41.3% in 2024, up from 40.4% in 2023.

In 2019, wind supplied around 10TWh of Ireland's electricity. Renewables made up around 12% of total energy use and 37% of electricity. Closing the gap between today and 2050 will take a large, fast build-out of wind, both onshore and offshore, along with the energy storage that a mostly wind-powered grid needs to stay reliable.

## Path to Wind as Ireland's Main Renewable Source

Ireland's roadmap sees wind output rising from around 10TWh today to more than **77TWh by 2050**, which would make wind the main renewable source in the country's energy mix. Getting there would take between 41GW and 46GW of wind. Ireland currently has only one operating offshore wind farm, Arklow Bank, at about 25MW, and EDF plans to take that to around 3GW by 2030. That target looks small next to the 93GW of new wind capacity added worldwide in 2022 alone. By 2050, Irish wind could meet around 2.5% of total EU electricity demand and produce over 5% of all EU wind energy.

## Ireland's Offshore Wind Efforts

**EDF's partnership with Simply Blue on floating wind.** EDF Renewables and Simply Blue have agreed to develop two offshore wind parks using floating turbines. The Emerald Floating Wind project, south of Cork, would have a capacity of 1.3GW. The Western Star project, 35km off the Clare coast, would have 1.35GW. Together they would support the government's plan to bring an extra 2GW of offshore wind online by 2030.

For more detail on Ireland's wind capacity and plans, see [FACTS about Wind Energy](../solutions/renewables/FACTS%20about%20Wind%20Energy.md).

## The Storage Challenge

Growing wind to the level Ireland's targets need will also mean building enough energy storage. Wind output goes up and down, and the more of the grid runs on wind, the more storage it needs to stay reliable.
