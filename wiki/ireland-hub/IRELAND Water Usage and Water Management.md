# Ireland Water Usage and Water Management

## What is it

Ireland's water system is under two linked pressures. Not enough clean water reaches the taps that need it, and too much pollution reaches the rivers and lakes. Climate change makes both worse. It cuts the water available in summer and autumn, raises the risk of winter flooding, and warms rivers and lakes in ways that help algae grow and make existing pollution worse.

This page brings together Ireland's water challenges: the extra pressure climate change puts on supply and quality, the ecological collapse at Lough Neagh, the 2026 plan to secure Dublin's water from the River Shannon, and the latest national data on falling water quality.

## Why it matters

Water is drinking water. It is what crops and livestock depend on, and it is the rivers and lakes that support wildlife and the places people swim, fish and walk. Ireland's water system is already strained by farm runoff and ageing pipes and treatment plants. Climate change adds more on top: heavier rainfall, drier summers, and warmer water that suits harmful algae.

## Climate Change and Ireland's Water Resources

Climate change is expected to **make existing pressures worse** on water supply, quality and flooding across Ireland. More water will evaporate from soil and plants, and summer and autumn rainfall is projected to fall, so less water will be available. Warmer temperatures are expected to boost the growth of algae and plants, which lowers water quality. Low river flows will become more common and will worsen many existing kinds of pollution. At the same time, demand for water rises in summer, for example for irrigation, which puts already low supplies under more strain. Heavier winter rain raises the risk of flooding, both where it already happens and in places that have not flooded before.

## Environmental Crisis at Lough Neagh

Lough Neagh is the largest freshwater lake by surface area in Ireland and Britain. It supplies 40% of Northern Ireland's drinking water and supports a major eel-fishing industry. It is now in an **unprecedented ecological crisis**, with toxic algal blooms damaging water quality and threatening the wildlife it supports.

Several factors are behind it. Nitrogen and phosphorus from fertiliser running off farmland are thought to be a major cause. The invasive zebra mussel has spread and made the water clearer, so more sunlight gets in and the algae photosynthesise more. Climate change also plays a part: the highest water temperature ever recorded at Lough Neagh was measured in June 2023.

The problem is not new, and it will take decades to fix, even with the Environment Improvement Plan (EIP) now in place. As one official put it: "We all know there are contributing factors: we have the agricultural run-off, we have wastewater infrastructure which needs massive investment, we have septic tanks, we also have the arrival of zebra mussels, we have climate change."

## Dublin's Water Supply on a "Knife Edge" (2026)

Demand for water in Greater Dublin has reached a critical point. In June 2026, Uisce Éireann told the Oireachtas Infrastructure Committee that supply is on a "knife edge" and cannot be met sustainably without a major new source. The problem affects parts of Wicklow, Meath and Kildare as well as Dublin.

**The proposed solution: the Water Supply Project.** The scheme would take 2% of the River Shannon's annual flow from an existing reservoir at the Parteen Basin in Co Tipperary and pipe it east across the country. Uisce Éireann says it will provide a "new, secure and climate-resilient water source, capable of supporting up to 50% of the population." A planning application went to An Coimisiún Pleanála in December 2025.

**Land access:** 80% of landowners along the route have signed voluntary agreements. Compulsory Purchase Order (CPO) powers would be used for the rest if needed, though Uisce Éireann says it hopes it will not come to that.

**The leakage question:** several TDs asked whether existing pipes should be fixed before new ones are built. Leakage in Dublin's network has already come down from 37% to 29%. Uisce Éireann says fixing leaks and building new supply will happen at the same time, not one after the other.

**Environment:** Uisce Éireann says water levels at Lough Derg and the Parteen Basin will not change, and fishing and recreation will not be affected.

## No Improvement to Water Quality (2026)

The Environmental Protection Agency's annual water quality report for 2025 was disappointing. There were no major improvements, and standards are still unsatisfactory in many parts of the country.

**The main figures:**
- Just over half of Ireland's rivers and lakes are rated good or better for biological quality.
- More rivers fell in quality class than improved during 2025.
- 43% of rivers have nitrate levels that are too high, worst in the east and south-east.
- Over 30% of lakes have too much phosphorous, especially in the border region.

**The causes are well understood:** excess nutrients from farm runoff and poorly performing wastewater plants are the biggest drivers. Phosphorous and nitrate levels stay above recommended limits, and the small gains seen in some years do not last.

**One local bright spot:** in the Ballyteigue Bannow catchment in Co Wexford, six of the 16 rivers surveyed improved in biological quality in 2025. The EPA says learning what worked there could guide action elsewhere.

Dr Jenny Deakin of the EPA has called for actions designed for individual rivers and lakes, alongside national targets. She notes that the measures now under way are not yet on the same scale as the problem.

## Fresh Water: A Finite Resource

Fresh water is far more limited than most people realise, and Ireland's pressures are part of a global pattern of overuse. Worldwide, people are taking **17% more fresh water** out of the ground and rivers than nature can replace. China takes around 22% more than its natural replenishment rate, and India around 52% more. Much of this water, especially from deep groundwater, cannot be replaced on any human timescale, and demand is expected to keep growing, by around 55% by 2050. As a result, a quarter of humanity already faces water shortages every year.

Farming uses most of it. 87% of the water people consume goes to irrigated agriculture, which uses 22 times more water than the households of all the world's 7.8 billion people put together (the population when this estimate was made; it passed 8.2 billion in 2025). To show how scarce fresh water is, the US Geological Survey pictured all the fresh water in the Earth's lakes and rivers gathered into a single ball. It would be a bubble only 35 miles across. That is all the surface fresh water the planet has.
