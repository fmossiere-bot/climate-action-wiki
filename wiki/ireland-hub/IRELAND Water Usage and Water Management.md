# Ireland Water Usage and Water Management

## What is it

Ireland's water system faces two connected pressures: not enough clean water reaching the taps that need it, and too much pollution reaching the rivers and lakes that should be thriving. Climate change makes both problems worse. It reduces the water available during summer and autumn while increasing the risk of winter flooding, and it warms rivers and lakes in ways that encourage algae growth and worsen existing pollution.

This page draws together Ireland's water challenges: the specific pressures climate change adds to water supply and quality, a case study of ecological collapse at Lough Neagh, the 2026 plan to secure Dublin's water supply from the River Shannon, and the latest national data on Ireland's declining water quality.

## Why it matters

Water is not an abstract resource. It is drinking water, the water crops and livestock depend on, and the rivers and lakes that support biodiversity and recreation. Ireland's water system is already under strain from agricultural runoff and ageing infrastructure, and climate change is layering additional pressure on top: more intense rainfall, drier summers, and warmer water that is more hospitable to harmful algae.

## Climate Change and Ireland's Water Resources

Climate change is expected to **exacerbate existing pressures** on water supply, quality and flooding across Ireland. Rising evapotranspiration combined with projected decreases in summer and autumn rainfall will reduce the amount of water available. Warmer temperatures are expected to drive **increased algal and plant growth**, degrading water quality, while lower river flows will become more frequent and will worsen many existing forms of pollution and contamination. At the same time, rising summer demand for water, for example for irrigation, will put already-depleted supplies under further pressure, while heavier winter rainfall raises flood risk both in areas already prone to flooding and in areas that have not previously been at risk.

## Environmental Crisis at Lough Neagh

Lough Neagh, the largest freshwater lake by surface area in Ireland and Britain, supplies 40% of Northern Ireland's drinking water and sustains a major eel-fishing industry. It is now facing an unprecedented ecological crisis driven by toxic algal blooms that have damaged water quality and put its biodiversity at risk.

Several factors are driving the crisis. Nitrogen and phosphorus running off agricultural land from fertiliser use are believed to be a major contributor. The spread of the invasive zebra mussel has made the water clearer, allowing more sunlight to penetrate and stimulating further algal photosynthesis. Climate change is a further factor: the highest water temperature ever recorded at Lough Neagh was measured in June 2023.

This is not a new problem, and it will take decades to fix, even with the Environment Improvement Plan (EIP) now in place. As one official summarised it: "We all know there are contributing factors: we have the agricultural run-off, we have wastewater infrastructure which needs massive investment, we have septic tanks, we also have the arrival of zebra mussels, we have climate change."

## Dublin's Water Supply on a "Knife Edge" (2026)

Greater Dublin's water demand has reached a critical point. In June 2026, Uisce Éireann told the Oireachtas Infrastructure Committee that supply is on a "knife edge" and cannot be met sustainably without a major new source, a problem affecting not just Dublin but parts of Wicklow, Meath and Kildare.

**The proposed solution: the Water Supply Project.** This scheme would divert 2% of the River Shannon's annual flow from an existing reservoir at the Parteen Basin in Co Tipperary, piping it east across the country. Uisce Éireann says it will provide a "new, secure and climate-resilient water source, capable of supporting up to 50% of the population." A planning application was submitted to An Coimisiún Pleanála in December 2025.

**Land access:** 80% of landowners along the route have signed voluntary agreements. Compulsory Purchase Order (CPO) powers would be used for the remainder if needed, though Uisce Éireann says it hopes this will not be necessary.

**The leakage question:** several TDs raised whether existing pipes should be fixed before building new infrastructure. Dublin's network leakage has already been reduced from 37% to 29%. Uisce Éireann says leak reduction and new supply work will proceed in parallel rather than in sequence.

**Environment:** Uisce Éireann says water levels at Lough Derg and the Parteen Basin will not change, and there will be no impact on fishing or recreation.

## No Improvement to Water Quality (2026)

The Environmental Protection Agency's annual water quality indicators report for 2025 made sobering reading: no major improvements were achieved, and standards remain unsatisfactory in many parts of the country.

**The headline numbers:**
- Just over half of Ireland's rivers and lakes are rated in good or better biological quality.
- More rivers declined in quality class than improved during 2025.
- 43% of rivers have nitrate concentrations that are too high, worst in the east and south-east.
- Over 30% of lakes have excess phosphorous, particularly in the border region.

**The root causes are well understood:** excess nutrients from agricultural runoff and underperforming wastewater infrastructure are the biggest drivers. Phosphorous and nitrate levels remain higher than recommended limits, and any partial year-on-year improvements are not being sustained.

**One local bright spot:** in the Ballyteigue Bannow catchment in Co Wexford, six of the 16 rivers surveyed improved in biological quality in 2025. The EPA says understanding what worked there could help inform action elsewhere.

The EPA's Dr Jenny Deakin has called for actions tailored to individual water bodies rather than national-level targets alone, noting that the scale of measures currently under way does not yet match the scale of the problem.

## Fresh Water: A Finite Resource

Freshwater is a far more limited resource than most people realise, and Ireland's pressures sit within a global picture of overuse. Globally, humanity is pulling 17% more fresh water out of the ground and rivers than nature can regenerate. China extracts around 22% more than its natural replenishment rate, and India around 52% more. This water cannot be restored, and demand is projected to keep growing, by around 55% by 2050. As a result, a quarter of humanity already faces water shortages every year.

Agriculture dominates global water consumption: 87% of the water people consume goes to irrigated agriculture, which uses 22 times more water than the households of the world's 7.8 billion people combined. To put the scarcity of fresh water in perspective, the US Geological Survey has visualised what it would look like if all the Earth's surface fresh water in lakes and rivers were collected into a single sphere: a bubble only 35 miles in diameter. That is the entire fresh water supply available to the planet.
