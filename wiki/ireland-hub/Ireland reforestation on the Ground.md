What reforestation looks like in practice, through three cases?  
- A volunteer native woodland project in County Wicklow
- A working farm moving from commercial conifer to permanent forest
- A large private rewilding estate

Figures and quotes should be read as illustrative of on-the-ground experience rather than official statistics.

## 1. Planting Native Woodland in County Wicklow

A volunteer association in County Wicklow plants mainly oak and cherry trees, sourcing seedlings from mother trees within around 5 kilometres. 

> Keeping the source this local is meant to preserve the genetic makeup of ancient local woodland rather than introducing trees bred elsewhere. 

Young saplings are fitted with plastic tubes to stop deer from browsing them, and after about five years, once the trees have established, the volunteers remove the tubes. Organisers describe the work as a way of building public awareness of native biodiversity, since many volunteers grew up with little exposure to Irish trees themselves.

## 2. From Sheep to Sitka: Tibraden Farm

Tibraden Farm, in the Dublin mountains, was originally a sheep farm. In the 1980s, the owner diversified into forestry, planting Sitka spruce and receiving state subsidies for 20 years, a common path for Irish farmers at the time. Sitka spruce is fast-growing and not native to Ireland, but it was chosen because it produces timber and captures carbon quickly, even on poorer soils.

> Current regulations require new forestry plots to leave a third of the land for natural regeneration and to include 20% non-commercial trees. 

The farm's owner argues that this makes commercial planting far less viable financially, since the non-commercial share produces no income. His stated long-term goal is a "permanent forest": a mixed, continuous canopy where individual trees are selected for felling and replaced gradually, rather than a plantation that is clear-felled all at once. This sits closer to continuous-cover forestry, an alternative management approach also referenced in the main forestry page.

## 3. EU Pressure and a Different Way to Spend Public Money

The EU has threatened Ireland with financial penalties if forest cover does not increase quickly enough, echoing the targets described in the Ireland Land Use, Soil and Forestry page. One view raised in the report is that instead of eventually paying fines for missing targets, that money could be redirected now to pay farmers directly for creating habitats and forest ecosystems on their land. This is presented as an argument, not a confirmed policy, and would need checking against actual EU procedure before being treated as fact.

## 4. Private Rewilding at Scale: The Dunany Estate

The Dunany Estate, around 45 minutes from Dublin, covers roughly 650 hectares and has a history going back to a castle built in 1180. Since 2014, about a third of the estate has been set aside for renaturation: trees and hedgerows have been planted, grass has been left uncut, and wild animals now graze it instead of machinery. According to the report, local farmers were initially sceptical, seeing it as good agricultural land going to waste, but attitudes shifted as older residents began noticing species returning that they had not seen since childhood.

The estate uses natural grazing as a form of disturbance, on the logic that without some pressure from grazing animals, a single dominant species can crowd everything else out. A team of scientists is documenting how the ecosystem develops over time. The report describes Dunany as the largest private renaturation project in Ireland, though a proposed railway line is now reported to threaten splitting the estate in two.

## 5. Two Structural Barriers: Land Status and Land Price

Two barriers recur across all three cases. First, Irish law currently treats a change from agricultural to forest land classification as permanent: once land is classified as forest, it cannot revert to farmland. This is described in the report as a strong deterrent for farmers who want to keep future options open for themselves or their heirs. Second, the report cites land prices reaching around €40,000 per hectare, a cost it describes as a serious obstacle for both farmers and volunteer or private conservation groups looking to expand reforestation.


## 6. Bottom Line

Behind Ireland's national forestry targets are individual decisions shaped by land law, subsidy history and land prices. Volunteers plant native woodland by hand, a working farm is trying to shift from pure commercial conifer to a more mixed permanent forest model, and a private estate has rewilded a third of its land despite early scepticism from neighbours. All three point to the same underlying constraints: forest status is legally hard to reverse, and land itself is expensive, which slows down reforestation regardless of how motivated individual landowners or volunteers are.
