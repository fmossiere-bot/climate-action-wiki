This page looks at what reforestation means in practice in Ireland, through three cases:
- A volunteer native woodland project in County Wicklow
- A working farm moving from commercial conifer to permanent forest
- A large private rewilding estate

The figures and quotes reflect people's experience on the ground. They are not official statistics.

## 1. Planting Native Woodland in County Wicklow

A volunteer group in County Wicklow plants mostly oak and cherry, growing its seedlings from mother trees within around 5 kilometres.

> Keeping the source this local is meant to preserve the genetic makeup of ancient local woodland rather than introducing trees bred elsewhere. 

Young saplings get plastic tubes to stop deer eating them. After about five years, once the trees are established, the volunteers take the tubes off. The organisers see the work as a way to help people learn about native wildlife, since many volunteers grew up knowing little about Irish trees themselves.

## 2. From Sheep to Sitka: Tibraden Farm

Tibraden Farm, in the Dublin mountains, started out as a sheep farm. In the 1980s the owner branched out into forestry. He planted Sitka spruce and received state subsidies for 20 years, a common path for Irish farmers at the time. Sitka spruce is not native to Ireland, but it was chosen because it grows fast, produces timber and captures carbon quickly, even on poorer soils.

> Current regulations require new forestry plots to leave a third of the land for natural regeneration and to include 20% non-commercial trees. 

The owner says this makes commercial planting much less worthwhile financially, because the non-commercial share earns nothing. His long-term aim is a **"permanent forest"**: a mixed, unbroken canopy where single trees are chosen for felling and replaced bit by bit, instead of a plantation cut down all at once. This is close to continuous-cover forestry, an alternative approach also mentioned on the main forestry page.

## 3. EU Pressure and a Different Way to Spend Public Money

The EU has threatened Ireland with financial penalties if forest cover does not grow quickly enough, in line with the targets on the Ireland Land Use, Soil and Forestry page. One idea raised in the report is to spend that money now instead of paying fines later, by paying farmers directly to create habitats and forest on their land. This is an argument, not a confirmed policy, and it would need checking against actual EU procedure before being treated as fact.

## 4. Private Rewilding at Scale: The Dunany Estate

The Dunany Estate, around 45 minutes from Dublin, covers roughly 650 hectares. Its history goes back to a castle built in 1180. Since 2014, about a third of the estate has been set aside to return to nature. Trees and hedgerows have been planted, grass is left uncut, and wild animals graze where machines once worked. According to the report, local farmers were sceptical at first and saw it as good farmland going to waste. Attitudes changed as older residents began to notice species coming back that they had not seen since childhood.

The estate uses grazing animals to disturb the land, because without that pressure a single species can take over and crowd out everything else. A team of scientists is recording how the ecosystem develops over time. The report calls Dunany the largest private rewilding project in Ireland, though a proposed railway line is now reported to threaten splitting the estate in two.

## 5. Two Structural Barriers: Land Status and Land Price

Two barriers come up in all three cases. The first is the law. In Ireland, changing land from agricultural to forest status is currently permanent: once land is classed as forest, it cannot go back to being farmland. The report says this puts off farmers who want to keep options open for themselves or their children. The second is cost. The report cites land prices of around **€40,000 per hectare**, a serious obstacle for farmers and for volunteer or private conservation groups that want to plant more.


## 6. What the Three Cases Show

Behind Ireland's national forestry targets are individual decisions shaped by land law, the history of subsidies and the price of land. Volunteers plant native woodland by hand. A working farm is trying to move from pure commercial conifer to a more mixed, permanent forest. A private estate has rewilded a third of its land despite early doubts from its neighbours. All three run into the same limits: forest status is legally hard to reverse, and land is expensive. That slows reforestation down, however motivated the landowners and volunteers are.
