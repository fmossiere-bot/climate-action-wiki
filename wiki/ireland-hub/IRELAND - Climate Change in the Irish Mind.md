# Ireland - Climate Change in the Irish Mind

## What is it

The **Climate Change in the Irish Mind Wave 2 Report**, published by the EPA, surveyed 1,355 respondents in Ireland on their attitudes to climate change. The results are broadly encouraging: 95% of respondents accept that climate change is happening, and only 5% remain outright deniers. But the report also finds a significant gap between attitudes and a sense of personal agency, which helps explain why widespread concern does not always translate into widespread action.

**Key findings:**
- 95% accept climate change as reality; only 5% are outright deniers.
- 53% see climate change as mostly caused by human activity, while 39% attribute it partly to human behaviour and partly to natural changes.
- 79% support government action on climate as a priority, though support is stronger for measures that don't directly affect respondents personally.
- 33% discuss climate change often, and a further 45% discuss it at least occasionally, with friends and family.
- 28% believe other citizens need to do more, while only 16% believe they themselves should be doing more.

## Why it matters

Understanding how people actually think about climate change, not just what they say they believe, is essential for designing communication and policy that works. This survey uses the theory of planned behaviour, one of the most widely used and validated models of human behaviour, which holds that people act when they judge a behaviour as positive, socially normative, and within their own control. Breaking the results down through this lens shows where progress is being made, and where the gaps still lie.

## Attitudes, Norms and the Agency Gap

On a high level, Irish respondents are both worried about climate change and supportive of action to prevent further deterioration. A majority believe climate change is already harming people in Ireland (54%), that it will harm future generations a great deal (83%), and that it is very or extremely important to them personally (62%). It follows that most respondents also want climate change to be a government priority (79%).

However, when specific interventions are examined, support skews toward measures with limited direct impact on the respondent, such as greater investment in environmentally friendly transport or funding to support EV sales. Support is markedly more sceptical for measures like banning oil or peat for home heating, or higher taxes on diesel. News about climate change has become part of daily life for most respondents, with 74% hearing about it on the news at least weekly, and a third discussing it often with friends and family.

Taken together, Irish respondents display two of the three components the theory of planned behaviour identifies as necessary for action: a positive attitude toward acting, and a sense that acting is socially normative. What appears to be missing is the third: a sense of personal agency. When asked who should do more, 28% say other citizens should do much more, while only 16% believe they themselves should be doing much more. This is an example of pluralistic ignorance, where people underestimate how much others already care and act, and in turn hold back from acting themselves. In practice, this shows up starkly: only 12% of respondents regularly boycott companies that damage the environment, and 17% specifically buy from environmentally friendly companies.

The report identifies scientists as being in a strong position to close this gap, since a wide margin of respondents, 61%, say they trust scientists more than any other source on climate change.
