# Ireland Biodiversity Plan

## What is it

**Ireland's 4th National Biodiversity Action Plan (2023-2030)** is the country's main roadmap for halting and reversing the loss of nature by 2030. The Department of Housing, Local Government and Heritage published it in January 2024. It contains 194 actions to tackle biodiversity loss. It is also the first such plan with a legal footing: under the Wildlife Amendment Act 2023, public bodies must now build biodiversity into their own plans and policies. Earlier plans had no such force behind them.

There are two official sources for the plan. One is gov.ie, the government's publication page. The other is npws.ie, the site of the National Parks and Wildlife Service, which runs the plan and offers the full document for download in English and Irish.

## Why it matters

The plan responds to a serious situation. Almost a third of EU-protected species and 85% of EU-protected habitats in Ireland are in poor condition. More than half of native Irish plant species have declined over the past 20 years, and a fifth of breeding bird species are falling too. Biodiversity loss is closely tied to climate change. Healthy ecosystems give us clean water, food, flood protection and carbon storage, all of which are climate priorities as well.

## What the Plan Includes

The plan has five main goals:
- a "whole of government, whole of society" approach
- meeting urgent conservation and restoration needs
- protecting what nature gives people, such as clean water and food
- building better evidence through data and monitoring
- strengthening Ireland's part in international biodiversity efforts

Five of its headline actions show the range of the 194 measures:

- Restore and rewet peatlands. Restoring and rehabilitating Ireland's peatlands, including rewetting raised bogs damaged over decades, is one of the plan's flagship actions.
- Create Marine Protected Areas. The government will draft laws on Marine Protected Areas (MPAs) and move quickly to put them in place, aiming to protect **30% of the marine environment by 2030**.
- Tackle invasive species harder. A dedicated invasive alien species unit will speed up enforcement, with work on the ground to clear species such as Japanese knotweed and rhododendron from native woodland and peatland.
- Make farming more nature-friendly. Ireland's farm schemes will bring in more nature-friendly measures, with a target to cut pesticide use by 50% by 2030.
- Expand and protect National Parks. This includes enlarging National Parks, doing more against wildlife crime, and working with local communities to protect habitats.

These five are the broad headline actions. The full plan of 194 actions covers everything from green spaces in towns to ocean protection.

## Pollinators on the Ground: Bees and Ponds

Two 2026 articles show how the plan's aims are playing out on individual farms and business parks.

**Industrial estates as bee habitat.** Over 30 of Ireland's 100 native bee species are now threatened with extinction, so action is urgent. Industrial estates cover a large share of Ireland's built-up land but have mostly been left out of national conversations about biodiversity. Yet they could form a network of pollinator corridors across the country. Researchers at Maynooth University have written a practical guide for industrial estates with three levels of action: quick wins such as bee hotels and patches left unmown, a middle "building up" stage, and more ambitious rewilding. It is designed for real-world conditions rather than ideal ones.

**Farm ponds and pollinators.** A new four-year research project is looking at an overlooked feature of Irish farmland, the humble pond, and how it might help struggling pollinators. Pollinator decline is serious enough that County Wicklow has set up Ireland's first dedicated Bee Sanctuary. The Ponds for Pollinators Project will look at how a pond's size, age and surrounding plants affect the number and variety of pollinators on working farms. It will also explore why some farmers choose to dig ponds and others do not.
