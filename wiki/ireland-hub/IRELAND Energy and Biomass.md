# Ireland Energy and Biomass

## What is it

**Biomass**, meaning wood, crops and organic waste burned or processed for energy, is a central part of Ireland's climate plan for energy, despite the risks and disputes around it. This page covers Ireland's move away from peat-fired power, the growing role of bioenergy in the country's renewable supply, and the debate over biomethane (gas made from farm and food waste) as a partial replacement for natural gas.

## Why it matters

Ireland used to rely heavily on burning peat for heating and power, a policy that goes back to decisions on national energy security made during the Second World War. That reliance damaged Ireland's peatlands, which the country is now working to restore as part of its climate action plan. At the same time, Ireland is trying to produce more sustainable bioenergy and biomethane at home rather than relying on imports. Not all biomass is equally sustainable, and getting that balance right decides whether biomass helps or hinders Ireland's climate goals.

## The end of the peat-fired era

Ireland has plenty of peatland, so for a long time it burned peat to heat homes and make electricity. Peat-fired power became national policy during the Second World War. That reliance badly damaged Ireland's peatlands, which is why restoring them is now a major commitment in Ireland's climate action plan. The country's last peat-fired power station has since closed, with the **Edenderry power plant** switching fully to burning biomass.

## The rise of bioenergy

Bioenergy already supplies around **40%** of Ireland's renewable energy. Even so, Ireland has a lot of untapped potential to *produce* biomass: much of what it uses today is imported rather than grown or made locally. Building up home-grown biomass, and making better use of what is already produced locally, is therefore an important goal. Along the way, several former peat-fired plants have switched to biomass. This shift is controversial, because some imported biomass and biofuels can add to climate problems rather than solve them, depending on how sustainably the raw material is sourced.

Ireland's future energy system is expected to rely heavily on wind energy and bioenergy, alongside other renewable sources.

## Biomethane's potential

Biomethane, which is renewable and described as carbon-neutral, could in theory replace around a quarter of Ireland's natural gas use. This is an estimate of potential from a 2023 Gas Networks Ireland report, not a government target. According to RTÉ, this could cut Ireland's total emissions by almost 4 million tonnes a year, or **6.5%** of the country's total emissions. At present, 176 projects across Ireland could, if they go ahead, produce 14.8 terawatt hours of biomethane a year.

One industry figure noted that the first Irish policy support for biomethane is still being developed, and said it must give producers long-term certainty on prices, with planning and permits suited to the sector's needs. Friends of the Earth Ireland is sceptical of Gas Networks Ireland's projections. It warns that using more biomethane must not come at the cost of sustainability or of Ireland's climate commitments to cut fossil fuels quickly. Stable prices for producers are also seen as important for the sector to grow.

## Gas Networks Ireland's biomethane plan

Gas Networks Ireland, a state company, is working with the Commission for the Regulation of Utilities (CRU) on a formal process to buy Irish-produced biomethane, following the publication of the government's biomethane strategy. The strategy's target is lower than the potential described above: it aims to replace **10%** of the natural gas used in the Republic by 2030 (up to 5.7 terawatt hours a year) with biomethane made by processing farm and food waste.

Ireland has large amounts of farm waste suitable for making biomethane, yet it still produces less than EU countries such as Denmark. The government hopes its strategy will support the building of up to 200 biomethane plants, known as anaerobic digesters, in rural communities.

## Ireland's draft biomethane strategy

Ireland's biomethane action plan sets out a joined-up strategy for developing bioenergy across agriculture, business, transport, the environment and energy.
