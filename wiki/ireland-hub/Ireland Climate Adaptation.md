# Climate Adaptation in Ireland

## 1. Definition

Climate adaptation means making a country less vulnerable to the harmful effects of climate change, and making use of any benefits that come along. It is different from climate mitigation, which means cutting greenhouse gas emissions to limit further warming. The two work on different timescales and both are needed, because past emissions have already locked in some warming and its effects.

## 2. What This Means for Ireland

Ireland's National Adaptation Framework sets out two broad kinds of impact the country needs to prepare for.

**Acute, extreme weather events.** These include heavier rainfall, more frequent flooding, stronger storms and more intense heatwaves. The government points to recent examples such as the flooding of Midleton in October 2023, the 2023 fodder crisis that hit farmers, and storms Babet (2023), Eunice (2022) and Kathleen (2024).

**Slow-onset changes.** These build up over decades rather than arriving all at once. They include sea level rise, gradual loss of wildlife, and shifting rainfall and temperature patterns. They mostly affect sectors such as forestry, farming, water and tourism.

Because Ireland sits on the Atlantic, it avoids the temperature extremes of many countries at similar latitudes. That can give the impression its climate impacts will be fairly mild. The evidence so far only partly backs this up. Ireland had its warmest year on record in 2023, along with its wettest July, and extreme weather is already costing a great deal.

## 3. How Ireland Organises Its Adaptation Response

Since 2018, with a major update in June 2024, Ireland's approach has four parts.

1. A National Adaptation Framework (NAF), which sets the overall national strategy and the legal requirements under the Climate Action and Low Carbon Development Act.
2. Sectoral Adaptation Plans (SAPs), now covering 13 priority sectors, each led by a government department. The sectors fall under four themes: Natural Environment, Built Environment and Infrastructure, Human, and Economy.
3. A shared way of assessing risk, the EPA's National Climate Change Risk Assessment (NCCRA). It gives every sector the same, comparable basis for rating climate risk, replacing the mix of different methods used before.
4. Local Authority Climate Action Plans, which bring adaptation down to decisions about zoning and development. They replace the earlier local authority adaptation strategies.

## 4. Two Guiding Principles

**Just Resilience.** The costs and benefits of adaptation do not fall evenly across society. This principle asks plans to think about fairness, meaning who pays and who benefits, instead of judging measures only on whether they work technically.

**Avoiding maladaptation.** A measure that fixes one problem while causing or worsening another counts as a failure, even if it looks like progress on its own. A common example is a flood defence that protects one area but pushes the flooding onto the next.

## 5. Credibility and Limitations

Ireland's adaptation framework is a policy and governance document, not a technical or scientific one. For the underlying climate science and detailed risk data, the EPA's National Climate Change Risk Assessment and Ireland's Climate Change Assessment are better references.

There is also a clear gap between policy and delivery. Independent reviews by the Climate Change Advisory Council found that progress on the first round of sectoral plans (2018 to 2024) was uneven. Delivery was especially weak where money for physical protection, such as flood defences or a sturdier grid, had not been secured.

## 6. Where Things Stand

In Ireland, climate adaptation means a defined, legally required government strategy to reduce vulnerability to both sudden extreme weather and slower, building climate change. For now, it is clearer on paper than in finished infrastructure and funded projects. The direction of policy is settled. How fast and how evenly it is carried out across sectors and local authorities is still an open question.

## Dublin Bay: Sea Level Rise at Twice the Global Rate

Water levels in **Dublin Bay are rising at 7 to 8 mm per year**, roughly double the global rate of 3 to 4 mm/year, according to reporting by the Irish Independent in January 2026. Coastal Dublin is home to about 1.5 million people across four local authorities (DCC, DLRCC, FCC, SDCC), and it is already living with the consequences: flooding from Storm Bram, erosion at Portrane Beach in north Co Dublin, and growing risk to key infrastructure such as the DART line.

### Planned retreat starts to enter the conversation

Sabrina Dekker, regional climate action coordinator for Dublin City Council, told the Irish Independent that authorities will soon have to deal with buying up coastal property, known formally as **planned retreat**, as an alternative to building coastal defences forever. Instead of putting up higher and higher walls, the state buys at-risk homes so the owners can move.

The existing scheme is small. The Voluntary Homeowners Relocation Scheme was set up in 2017 to help households hit by serious flooding. Out of **174 potentially eligible homes**, only 31 were offered financial help to relocate, and only 20 had accepted by September 2024. When Social Democrats leader Holly Cairns pressed the issue after the 2023 floods, then junior housing minister Kieran O'Donnell confirmed there were no plans to extend the scheme.

Dekker was frank about how hard this is for people: *"The challenge is people grew up on that street; they had all these memories on these streets. Their family's been here for generations and this is their identity. It's not easy."*

### Protecting the DART line

In several places, the DART and the wider east-coast railway run within metres of the Irish Sea. Richard Fitzpatrick, climate action officer at DLRCC, warns that high tides combined with wind and storms can send the sea over the DART line. DLRCC and Irish Rail are now working together on the **€230 million East Coast Railway Infrastructure Protection Projects**. He sums up the awkward trade-off:

> *"The rail network system is one of the solutions to reducing our carbon emissions, so it's a double whammy if that's not protected."*

Losing the rail line would push more people back into cars, and transport already makes up 36% of Dublin's emissions.

### Inland flooding and green infrastructure

South Dublin is doing more than defending the coast. Fionnghuala Ryan, SDCC's climate action coordinator, says "increased rainfall and severe rainstorms" are pushing the council to use **parks, rivers and canals as buffer zones** and natural drainage. Flood relief works on the River Poddle, River Camac and Whitechurch Stream, begun after the 2011 floods, sit alongside projects to cut emissions in the county's decarbonising zones: Clondalkin (SDCC), Balbriggan (FCC), Ballymun and Ringsend (DCC), and a 7 km coastal stretch between Blackrock and Dún Laoghaire (DLRCC).
