# Climate Adaptation in Ireland

## 1. Definition

Climate adaptation means reducing a country's vulnerability to the negative effects of climate change, while also taking advantage of any positive impacts that may arise. It is distinct from climate mitigation, which refers to efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and limit further warming. Adaptation and mitigation work on different timescales and are both necessary, since a certain amount of warming and its effects are already locked in because of past emissions.

## 2. What This Means for Ireland

Ireland's National Adaptation Framework identifies two broad categories of impact that the country needs to prepare for.

**Acute, extreme weather events.** These include heavier rainfall, more frequent flooding, stronger storms and more intense heatwaves. Recent examples cited by the government include the flooding of Midleton in October 2023, the 2023 fodder crisis affecting farmers, and storms Babet (2023), Eunice (2022) and Kathleen (2024).

**Slow-onset changes.** These build up gradually over decades rather than arriving as a single event. They include sea level rise, gradual biodiversity loss, and shifting rainfall and temperature patterns, and they mainly affect sectors such as forestry, agriculture, water and tourism.

Ireland's Atlantic location means it does not experience the temperature extremes of many countries at similar latitudes, which can create an impression that its climate impacts will be relatively mild. The evidence available so far only partly supports this. Ireland recorded its warmest year on record in 2023, along with its wettest July, and the costs of extreme weather already being experienced are substantial rather than hypothetical.

## 3. How Ireland Organises Its Adaptation Response

Since 2018, and updated significantly in June 2024, Ireland's approach is structured around four elements.

1. **A National Adaptation Framework (NAF)**, setting the overall national strategy and legal requirements under the Climate Action and Low Carbon Development Act.
2. **Sectoral Adaptation Plans (SAPs)**, now covering 13 priority sectors, each led by a government department. The sectors are grouped under four themes: Natural Environment, Built Environment and Infrastructure, Human, and Economy.
3. **A shared risk assessment method**, the EPA's National Climate Change Risk Assessment (NCCRA), which gives all sectors a common, comparable basis for rating climate risk, replacing a previous patchwork of different methods.
4. **Local Authority Climate Action Plans**, which bring adaptation down to the level of zoning and development decisions, replacing the earlier local authority adaptation strategies.

## 4. Two Guiding Principles

**Just Resilience.** Adaptation measures have costs and benefits that are not distributed evenly across society. This principle asks that plans consider fairness in who bears the costs and who receives the benefits, rather than judging measures on technical effectiveness alone.

**Avoiding maladaptation.** A measure that solves one problem while creating or worsening another is considered a failure, even if it appears to be progress when viewed in isolation. A commonly cited example is a flood defence that protects one area but shifts the flooding onto a neighbouring one.

## 5. Credibility and Limitations

Ireland's adaptation framework is a policy and governance document rather than a technical or scientific one. For the underlying climate science and detailed risk data, the EPA's National Climate Change Risk Assessment and Ireland's Climate Change Assessment are the more appropriate references.

It is also worth being direct about the gap between policy and delivery. Independent reviews by the Climate Change Advisory Council found progress on the first round of sectoral plans (2018 to 2024) to be uneven, with implementation particularly weak where funding for physical resilience measures, such as flood defences or grid resilience, had not been secured.

## 6. Bottom Line

Climate adaptation in Ireland refers to a defined, legally mandated government strategy for reducing vulnerability to both sudden extreme weather and slower, cumulative climate change. In practice, it currently exists more clearly on paper than in delivered infrastructure and funded projects. The direction of policy is well established; the pace and consistency of implementation across sectors and local authorities remains the open question.

## Dublin Bay: Sea Level Rise at Twice the Global Rate

Water levels in **Dublin Bay are rising at 7 to 8 mm per year, roughly double the global rate of 3 to 4 mm/year** according to reporting by the Irish Independent in January 2026. Coastal Dublin — home to about **1.5 million people** across four local authorities (DCC, DLRCC, FCC, SDCC) — is already living with the consequences: Storm Bram flooding, erosion at Portrane Beach in north Co Dublin, and rising exposure of critical infrastructure like the DART line.

### Planned retreat starts to enter the conversation

**Sabrina Dekker**, regional climate action coordinator for Dublin City Council, told the Irish Independent that authorities will soon have to grapple with **coastal property acquisition** — the formal term is **planned retreat** — as an alternative to endless coastal defences. The idea is that instead of building higher and higher walls, the state buys out at-risk homes so owners can relocate.

The existing scheme is small. The **Voluntary Homeowners Relocation Scheme** was set up in 2017 to help households affected by serious flooding. Out of **174 potentially eligible homes**, only **31 were given offers of financial help to relocate**, and only **20 had accepted by September 2024**. When Social Democrats leader Holly Cairns pushed after the 2023 floods, then junior housing minister Kieran O'Donnell confirmed there were no proposals to extend the scheme.

Dekker was blunt about the emotional side of this: *"The challenge is people grew up on that street; they had all these memories on these streets. Their family's been here for generations and this is their identity. It's not easy."*

### Protecting the DART line

The DART and the wider east-coast rail corridor sit within metres of the Irish Sea in several places. **Richard Fitzpatrick**, DLRCC climate action officer, warns that high tides combined with wind and storms can lead to the sea inundating the DART line. DLRCC and Irish Rail are now working together on the **€230 million East Coast Railway Infrastructure Protection Projects**. His framing captures the awkwardness of the trade-off:

> *"The rail network system is one of the solutions to reducing our carbon emissions, so it's a double whammy if that's not protected."*

Losing the rail line would push more people back into cars in a country where transport already accounts for 36% of Dublin's emissions.

### Inland flooding and green infrastructure

South Dublin has moved beyond only defending the coast. **Fionnghuala Ryan**, SDCC's climate action coordinator, describes "increased rainfall and severe rainstorms" pushing the council to use **parks, rivers and canals as buffer zones and natural drainage systems**. Flood alleviation works on the River Poddle, River Camac and Whitechurch Stream — begun after the 2011 floods — sit alongside proactive projects to cut emissions in the county's **decarbonising zones**: Clondalkin (SDCC), Balbriggan (FCC), Ballymun and Ringsend (DCC), and a 7 km coastal stretch between Blackrock and Dún Laoghaire (DLRCC).
