# Ireland and invasive species: a bureaucratic paralysis

## What is it

Invasive alien species are one of the main drivers of biodiversity loss worldwide, and tackling them is a specific Kunming-Montreal target for 2030. Ireland's response has in effect stalled. The government **announced an Invasive Alien Species Bureau in May 2026**. By late October 2026 the Bureau had no staff and no plan to hire any. Communities trying to clear invasive species themselves run into one obstacle after another: unclear licensing rules, no defined "qualified professional" status for removal by hand, no funding scheme, and a transport licence that requires an approved waste facility, from a list that neither the NPWS nor the EPA will provide.

Meanwhile, well-known invasives keep spreading while community groups wait for policy to catch up: rhododendron in Killarney's ancient woodlands, gunnera on Achill, Japanese knotweed everywhere, Asian hornets starting to appear, and box tree moth across Europe.

## Why it matters

Under the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (COP15, Montreal 2022), Ireland committed to tackling invasive species. Minister Christopher O'Sullivan has said plainly that Ireland is "not on track" to meet its 2030 nature targets. Invasive species show why most clearly. Ireland has a legal framework (the EU third-schedule species regulation) and a stated commitment (the Bureau), but no working system to turn them into action on the ground.

For anyone who cares about wildlife, invasive species sit alongside habitat loss and pesticide use as the main drivers of nature loss in Ireland. If communities cannot act and the state is not acting, the problem grows every year.

## The current state

### What exists on paper

- **EU Regulation on invasive species**: breeding, transporting, releasing or spreading a third-schedule species without a licence is an offence. Each member state issues and regulates its own licences
- **Third-schedule list for Ireland**: includes rhododendron ponticum, Japanese knotweed, Spanish bluebells, giant hogweed, and more
- **National Parks and Wildlife Service (NPWS)**: issues licences to transport invasive species waste
- **National Biodiversity Data Centre (NBDC)**: receives and organises sightings through its invasive species officer, Collette O'Flynn
- **Local Environmental Action Fund (LEAF)**: can in theory provide up to €9,000 towards a community removal project
- **Invasive Alien Species Bureau**: announced by Minister O'Sullivan in May 2026

### What is missing in practice

- **Staff and a clear purpose for the Bureau**: Parliamentary Question 383 (14 July 2026, Social Democrats TD Jennifer Whitmore) confirmed that the Bureau has no staff and no plan to hire. It appears to be a new name for existing NPWS/NBDC collaboration
- **A clear definition of "qualified"**: LEAF funding requires a "qualified professional" to invoice the work, but LEAF cannot say what that means. For weedkiller (glyphosate) use, the professional pesticide licence sets a clear bar. For removal by hand, there is no equivalent licence and no legal basis for one
- **A list of approved waste facilities**: the NPWS transport licence requires an "approved" disposal site. NPWS told journalist Orla ní Dhúill in writing to ask the EPA. The EPA told her it has no such list either, and that applicants should contact disposal businesses directly and ask them to vouch for themselves

## What invasive plants are in Ireland now

| Species | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Rhododendron ponticum** | Degrading ancient woodlands in Killarney National Park and elsewhere |
| **Gunnera** (giant rhubarb) | Clogging watercourses on Achill and in the west |
| **Japanese knotweed** | Huge concern: damages buildings, roads and infrastructure, and resists many weedkillers |
| **Giant hogweed** (Heracleum mantegazzianum) | The only plant on this list that is dangerous to human health: its sap causes burns when skin is exposed to sunlight (phytophotodermatitis) |
| **Spanish bluebells** | Still legally sold in garden centres across Ireland despite being on the third-schedule list |
| **Box tree moth** | Wrecking box hedges across Europe since 2007 |
| **Asian hornets** | Growing concern for native bees and pollinators |

## Why the "do nothing" default is a problem

ní Dhúill makes a precise argument. Where invasive species are already everywhere, waiting for a "qualified professional" who does not clearly exist amounts to deciding to let the invasion carry on. The unspoken policy, that doing nothing is safer than risking spreading a species, ignores the fact that the species is already spreading faster than the state can respond.

Clearing by hand by informed community groups is one answer among several. It is a valid partner to professional clearance with weedkiller, and it suits the values of wildlife groups, many of which will not use glyphosate because of its documented harm to ecosystems.

## What a working system would look like

ní Dhúill's reporting points to three practical fixes:

- A public list of approved waste facilities, from either NPWS or the EPA, official and kept up to date, so people applying for a transport licence know where they can send waste
- A defined "qualified for manual removal" status. This could be a short training requirement like the professional pesticide licence, or a clear statement that people can remove plants on their own land without any further qualification
- LEAF funding that can go to community volunteers, by dropping the rule that "qualified professionals" must invoice the work before community groups can use the fund

## Context from the broader Irish biodiversity picture

Ireland's record on biodiversity is poor. An assessment published in 2024-2025 found **90% of Ireland's protected habitats** in bad health. Ireland was the first EU member state to submit its National Nature Restoration Plan to Brussels, pushed for the EU Nature Restoration Law in 2024, and holds the EU Council Presidency until the end of 2026. But the gap between the legal framework and what happens on the ground is the same at national and EU level. Observations by Fintan Kelly and Jane Stout from COP17 in Yerevan (see [Halfway to 2030: COP17](../legislation-policy/halfway-to-2030-biodiversity-cop17-overview.md)) make the same point about the EU Nature Restoration Law lacking funds.
