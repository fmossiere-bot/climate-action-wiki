# Ireland and invasive species: a bureaucratic paralysis

## What is it

Ireland's response to invasive alien species — one of the main drivers of global biodiversity loss and a specific Kunming-Montreal 2030 target — is in effective paralysis. The government **announced an Invasive Alien Species Bureau in May 2026**. By late October 2026 the Bureau had no staff and no hiring plan. Communities trying to remove invasive species themselves hit a wall of unclear licensing requirements, no defined "qualified professional" status for manual removal, no funding scheme, and a transport licence that requires an approved waste facility — a list that neither the NPWS nor the EPA will provide.

The result: well-known invasives — rhododendron in Killarney's ancient woodlands, gunnera in Achill, Japanese knotweed everywhere, Asian hornets emerging, box tree moth across Europe — spread while community groups wait for policy to catch up.

## Why it matters

Ireland committed under the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (COP15, Montreal 2022) to tackle invasive species. Minister Christopher O'Sullivan has said plainly that Ireland is "not on track" to meet its 2030 nature targets. The invasive species story is the most visible example of why: Ireland has a legal framework (the EU third-schedule species regulation) and a stated commitment (the Bureau), but no working system to translate those into action on the ground.

For a reader concerned about biodiversity, invasive species rank alongside habitat loss and pesticide use as the main drivers of nature loss in Ireland. If communities cannot act and the state is not acting, the problem compounds year on year.

## The current state

### What exists on paper

- **EU Regulation on invasive species** — breeding, transporting, releasing or dispersing a third-schedule species without a licence is an offence. Each member state applies and regulates licences
- **Third-schedule list for Ireland** — includes rhododendron ponticum, Japanese knotweed, Spanish bluebells, giant hogweed, and more
- **National Parks and Wildlife Service (NPWS)** — issues licences for transport of invasive species waste
- **National Biodiversity Data Centre (NBDC)** — receives and organises sightings via its invasive species officer, Collette O'Flynn
- **Local Environmental Action Fund (LEAF)** — can theoretically provide up to €9,000 toward a community removal project
- **Invasive Alien Species Bureau** — announced by Minister O'Sullivan in May 2026

### What is missing in practice

- **Staff and clear purpose for the Bureau** — confirmed by Parliamentary Question 383 (14 July 2026, Social Democrats TD Jennifer Whitmore). The Bureau has no staff and no hiring plan; it appears to be a rebrand of existing NPWS/NBDC collaboration
- **Clear definition of "qualified"** — LEAF funding requires a "qualified professional" to invoice the work, but LEAF cannot define the term. For herbicide (glyphosate) use, the professional pesticide licence sets a clear bar. For manual removal, there is no equivalent licence or legal basis for one
- **A list of approved waste facilities** — the NPWS transport licence requires an "approved" disposal site. NPWS told journalist Orla ní Dhúill in writing to ask the EPA. The EPA told her they also have no such list and that applicants should contact disposal businesses directly and ask them to vouch for themselves

## What invasive plants are in Ireland now

| Species | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Rhododendron ponticum** | Degrading ancient woodlands in Killarney National Park and elsewhere |
| **Gunnera** (giant rhubarb) | Congesting watercourses in Achill and the west |
| **Japanese knotweed** | Huge concern — damages buildings, roads, infrastructure, resistant to many herbicides |
| **Giant hogweed** (Heracleum mantegazzianum) | Only one dangerous to human health (phytophotodermatitis burns) |
| **Spanish bluebells** | Still legally sold in garden centres across Ireland despite being on the third-schedule list |
| **Box tree moth** | Wrecking box hedges across Europe since 2007 |
| **Asian hornets** | Growing concern for native bees and pollinators |

## Why the "do nothing" default is a problem

ní Dhúill makes a precise argument: in areas so badly affected that invasive species are already everywhere, waiting for a "qualified professional" who does not clearly exist is functionally the same as deciding to let the invasion continue. The implicit policy — that it is safer to do nothing than to risk spreading a species — ignores that the species is already spreading faster than the state can respond.

Manual clearance by informed community groups is not the only answer, but it is a legitimate complement to herbicide-based professional clearance, and it is the method most compatible with the ethics of biodiversity-focused groups (many of whom will not use glyphosate because of its documented ecological harm).

## What a working system would look like

Three concrete things, drawn from ní Dhúill's reporting:

1. **A public list of approved waste facilities** — either from NPWS or EPA, authoritative, updated, so transport-licence applicants know where they can send waste
2. **A defined "qualified for manual removal" status** — either a short training requirement similar to the professional pesticide licence, or an explicit recognition that private citizens can remove plants on their own land without further qualification
3. **LEAF funding that can go to community volunteers** — removing the requirement that "qualified professionals" must invoice the work before community groups can access the fund

## Context from the broader Irish biodiversity picture

Ireland's record on biodiversity is poor: an assessment published in 2024-2025 found **90% of Ireland's protected habitats** in bad health. Ireland was the first EU member state to submit its National Nature Restoration Plan to Brussels, pushed for the EU Nature Restoration Law in 2024, and holds the EU Council Presidency through the end of 2026. But the gap between legal framework and operational implementation is the same at national and EU level — the Fintan Kelly / Jane Stout observations from COP17 Yerevan (see [Halfway to 2030: COP17](../legislation-policy/halfway-to-2030-biodiversity-cop17-overview.md)) make the same point about the EU Nature Restoration Law lacking funds.
