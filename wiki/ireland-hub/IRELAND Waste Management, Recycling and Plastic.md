# Ireland's Waste Management, Recycling and Plastic Problem

Ireland's waste system has changed substantially over the past two decades, but recycling and composting rates still lag behind EU targets, and a large share of what goes into general waste bins should not be there. This page covers where household waste actually ends up, how the brown and recycling bins are processed, practical recycling rules, and the latest performance data against EU targets.

## Where Household Waste Actually Goes

Over the past 10 to 15 years, the journey taken by the contents of Irish domestic bins has become far more varied, and arguably more transparent, despite a small number of rogue operators still illegally dumping waste on roadsides or at unlicensed sites. Twenty years ago the picture was much simpler and considerably worse: there was little separation between bin types, and most waste was either burned in landfills or exported, largely to China.

It took China's refusal, from 2017, to keep accepting the huge volumes of contaminated mixed waste sent by European countries, the United States and Australia to force waste management companies to change their practices. The number of Irish landfill sites fell from around 125 in 2000 to just three in 2021, as part of the EU Landfill Directive.

**Repak**, funded by fees paid by retailers and manufacturers and covering around 70% of packaging waste, subsidises household recycling bins, bottle banks and local authority recycling centres, and monitors what private waste operators send to their depots and where it ends up. The Irish Waste Management Association, which accounts for around 80% of waste processed in the country, recommends checking that any household waste collector holds a valid permit via the National Waste Collection Permits Office. The EPA issues licences to waste treatment plants and has prosecuted 19 companies for non-compliance since 2016.

## The Brown Bin: Food and Organic Waste

Correctly used, the brown bin should contain only cooked and raw food, light garden waste and compostable packaging. Its contents go for anaerobic digestion or industrial composting, paid for by waste management companies. The Composting and Anaerobic Digestion Association of Ireland (Cré) began certifying compostable packaging in June 2021, and has called for a subsidy scheme for brown bins similar to the one that already exists for recycling bins, citing Italy's model where producer fees fund the collection and recycling of food waste and compostable plastics specifically.

Around **800,000 of the 1.3 million households** using kerbside waste collection in Ireland have a compostable waste (brown) bin. Roughly **10%** of brown bin contents are sent to anaerobic digestors in Ireland and the UK, where methane gas is extracted and converted into electricity fed into the grid; the residual material, known as digestate, is used by farmers as an organic alternative to nitrogen fertiliser, reducing the greenhouse gases associated with fertiliser use.

## The Recycling Bin: Sorting and Value

Recycling bin contents are processed at material recovery facilities, where trucks unload onto conveyor belt systems that use different tools to separate metals from plastics and paper. Material that does not fit these categories is turned into solid recovered fuel, used in cement kilns in place of coal.

Recycled plastic is now worth more than virgin plastic, a shift driven by legislation requiring manufacturers to include recycled content in packaging, which has made the recycling bin an increasingly valuable stream. Aluminium is the highest-value material collected, sold on to smelters in the US and Europe. Soft plastics (such as bottle labels, punnet film, cling film and carrier bags) were reintroduced to recycling bins in September 2021, having been removed in 2017 due to high contamination and limited recycling capacity; facilities have since improved and contamination from items such as leftover food has fallen, though it remains an issue.

## Case Study: Bord na Móna's Materials Recovery Facility

Bord na Móna Recycling processes 450,000 tonnes of material a year across its materials recovery facilities (MRFs) as part of the company's broader shift from a "brown" to a green strategy, moving away from fossil fuels and peat-burning power stations towards wind farms, peatland rehabilitation, recycling and waste-to-power generation.

Its MRF in Tullamore, Co. Offaly, accepts more than 40,000 tonnes of domestic and commercial recyclable waste a year from 135,000 customers. Materials are sorted into commodities including paper, cardboard, PET (plastic drinking bottles), HDPE (shampoo bottles) and PP (yoghurt pots, food tubs and trays): paper and board are extracted and recycled into new paper products, plastics are turned into flake for reprocessing into new containers, and metals such as aluminium and tin cans are melted down for reuse.

Landfill is treated as a last resort: only a small percentage of the 170,000 tonnes of general refuse Bord na Móna collects each year is landfilled, typically only during unplanned outages at waste-to-energy facilities. Methane captured from the Drehid landfill is converted into enough electricity to power around **8,500 homes a year**.

At present, around **70%** of material collected by Bord na Móna Recycling is turned back into commodities, a figure that could be higher were it not for contamination in recycling bins, most often from textiles, wood, coffee cups, glass, batteries and, above all, food waste. According to EPA statistics, if contamination were eliminated, household recycling rates would rise by around a third and commercial recycling rates by around a half.

## Ten Rules for Better Recycling

- All recyclable items should be **clean, dry and loose**. Don't nest one recyclable inside another (for example, a cereal box filled with other recyclables), as this stops sorting machines from scanning and separating items correctly.
- Shredded or torn paper is technically recyclable but too small for sorting machines to detect; put paper into the recycling bin whole wherever possible.
- Soft plastic, such as bottle labels, film on fruit punnets, cling film and plastic carrier bags, **cannot be recycled** in Ireland and should be kept out of the recycling bin.
- Tinfoil **can be recycled** if it is clean and rolled into a ball, but crisp and popcorn packets with foil linings cannot.
- Aluminium drinks cans are recyclable, but should be rinsed of sticky residue first.
- PET plastic (marked with the recycling symbol and the number 1), used for bottles, milk cartons, tubs and shampoo bottles, is genuinely recyclable if cleaned, dried and placed loosely in the bin.
- Black plastic is technically recyclable, but its colour is hard for sorting machines to detect against the conveyor belts, especially when dyed with carbon; it should still be cleaned, dried and placed in the recycling bin.
- Paper and cardboard are only recyclable if clean: no greasy pizza boxes, food-stained napkins or food-stained lunch boxes.
- Nappies must never go in the recycling bin: a person, not a machine, has to remove them, and they contaminate everything around them.
- Disposable coffee cups are generally not recyclable, unless specifically labelled otherwise.

## Are We Separating Waste Well Enough?

The EPA's National Municipal Waste Characterisation Project, conducted every four years, found that **over two-thirds of the material placed in general (black) waste bins should have been placed in recycling or organic waste bins instead**. The EU Waste Directive requires all households to have a separate organic waste collection, and Irish regulations have required all commercial premises to have one since July of the reporting year. Ireland is still falling well short of proper separation:

- **64%** of food waste is still placed in black bins
- Only **69%** of households have an organic (brown) bin
- **65%** of household plastic is placed in black bins instead of recycling bins
- **75%** of commercial plastic waste is placed in general bins rather than recycling streams

Other EPA data from the same period: **1.2 million tonnes of packaging waste** were generated in 2021; **84%** of glass and **73%** of cardboard were recycled, but only **28%** of plastic was recycled, with the rest incinerated, releasing around **2.7 kg of CO2 per kilogram** of plastic burned, against a target of 55% plastic recycling. Much plastic never reaches recycling at all, because it is mixed in with other waste before it gets the chance.

## Recycling Performance vs EU Targets (2022 Data)

Ireland's waste and recycling performance has stalled against EU Green Deal targets, which mandate that **55% of municipal waste** and **65% of packaging waste** be recycled by 2025. According to the EPA, Ireland currently recycles **41% of municipal waste** and **60% of packaging waste**, well short on the municipal side. Ireland is not alone: the European Environment Agency estimates that 18 of the 27 EU member states are likely to breach one or both targets.

Ireland generated **15.7 million tonnes of waste in 2022**, equivalent to around 8kg per person per day. The overall figure was down 1.9 million tonnes on the previous year, though this decline is mostly attributed to a fall in construction and demolition waste, likely driven by cyclical economic factors rather than a genuine improvement in waste generation.
