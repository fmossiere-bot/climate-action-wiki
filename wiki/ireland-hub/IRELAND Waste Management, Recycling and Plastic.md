# Ireland's Waste Management, Recycling and Plastic Problem

Ireland's waste system has changed a great deal over the past two decades. Even so, recycling and composting rates still fall short of EU targets, and much of what goes into general waste bins should not be there. This page covers where household waste actually ends up, how the brown and recycling bins are processed, practical recycling rules, and the latest figures against EU targets.

## Where Household Waste Actually Goes

Over the past 10 to 15 years, the journey of what goes into Irish household bins has become far more varied, and probably more transparent. A small number of rogue operators still dump waste illegally on roadsides or at unlicensed sites. Twenty years ago the picture was simpler and much worse. There was little separation between bin types, and most waste was either buried in landfill or exported, largely to China.

Change came when China refused, from 2017, to keep taking the huge volumes of contaminated mixed waste sent by European countries, the United States and Australia. That forced waste companies to change how they worked. Under the EU Landfill Directive, the number of Irish landfill sites fell from around 125 in 2000 to just three in 2021.

**Repak** is funded by fees from retailers and manufacturers and covers around 70% of packaging waste. It subsidises household recycling bins, bottle banks and local authority recycling centres, and it monitors what private waste operators send to their depots and where it ends up. The Irish Waste Management Association, which handles around 80% of the waste processed in the country, recommends checking that any household waste collector holds a valid permit through the National Waste Collection Permits Office. The EPA licenses waste treatment plants and has prosecuted 19 companies for breaking the rules since 2016.

## The Brown Bin: Food and Organic Waste

Used properly, the brown bin should hold only cooked and raw food, light garden waste and compostable packaging. Its contents go to anaerobic digestion or industrial composting, paid for by waste companies. The Composting and Anaerobic Digestion Association of Ireland (Cré) began certifying compostable packaging in June 2021. It has called for a subsidy scheme for brown bins like the one that already exists for recycling bins. It points to Italy, where producer fees pay specifically for collecting and recycling food waste and compostable plastics.

Around **800,000 of the 1.3 million households** with kerbside collection in Ireland had a brown bin for compostable waste in an earlier Irish Times count, roughly two-thirds. Roughly 10% of brown bin contents go to anaerobic digesters in Ireland and the UK. There, methane gas is extracted and turned into electricity for the grid. What is left, called digestate, is used by farmers as an organic alternative to nitrogen fertiliser, which cuts the greenhouse gases linked to fertiliser use.

## The Recycling Bin: Sorting and Value

Recycling bin contents are processed at material recovery facilities. Lorries tip their loads onto conveyor belts, where different tools separate metals from plastics and paper. Anything that does not fit these categories becomes solid recovered fuel, burned in cement kilns instead of coal.

Recycled plastic is now worth more than new plastic. That shift comes from laws requiring manufacturers to put recycled content into packaging, and it has made the recycling bin an increasingly valuable stream. Aluminium is the most valuable material collected, and it is sold on to smelters in the US and Europe. Soft plastics (such as bottle labels, punnet film, cling film and carrier bags) went back into recycling bins in September 2021. They had been removed in 2017 because of high contamination and limited recycling capacity. Facilities have improved since, and contamination from things like leftover food has fallen, though it is still a problem.

## Case Study: Bord na Móna's Materials Recovery Facility

Bord na Móna Recycling processes 450,000 tonnes of material a year across its materials recovery facilities (MRFs). This is part of the company's wider move from a "brown" to a green strategy, away from fossil fuels and peat-burning power stations and towards wind farms, peatland rehabilitation, recycling and generating power from waste.

Its MRF in Tullamore, Co. Offaly, takes in more than 40,000 tonnes of household and commercial recycling a year from 135,000 customers. Materials are sorted into commodities including paper, cardboard, PET (plastic drinking bottles), HDPE (shampoo bottles) and PP (yoghurt pots, food tubs and trays). Paper and board are recycled into new paper products. Plastics are turned into flake and reprocessed into new containers. Metals such as aluminium and tin cans are melted down and reused.

Landfill is a last resort. Only a small percentage of the 170,000 tonnes of general rubbish Bord na Móna collects each year goes to landfill, usually only when a waste-to-energy plant has an unplanned outage. Methane captured from the Drehid landfill is turned into enough electricity to power around **8,500 homes a year**.

At present, around **70%** of the material Bord na Móna Recycling collects is turned back into commodities. That figure could be higher without contamination in recycling bins, most often from textiles, wood, coffee cups, glass, batteries and, above all, food waste. According to EPA statistics, if contamination were removed, household recycling rates would rise by around a third and commercial rates by around a half.

## Ten Rules for Better Recycling

- All recyclable items should be **clean, dry and loose**. Don't put one recyclable inside another (for example, a cereal box filled with other recyclables), as this stops sorting machines from scanning and separating them properly.
- Shredded or torn paper can in theory be recycled, but the pieces are too small for sorting machines to pick up. Put paper in the recycling bin whole wherever you can.
- Soft plastic, such as bottle labels, film on fruit punnets, cling film and plastic carrier bags, can go in the recycling bin in Ireland (since September 2021), as long as it is clean, dry and loose. If it is dirty, put it in the general waste bin.
- Tinfoil can be recycled if it is clean and rolled into a ball, but crisp and popcorn packets with foil linings cannot.
- Aluminium drinks cans are recyclable, but rinse off any sticky residue first.
- PET plastic (marked with the recycling symbol and the number 1), used for bottles, milk cartons, tubs and shampoo bottles, is recyclable if cleaned, dried and put loose in the bin.
- Black plastic can in theory be recycled, but sorting machines struggle to see it against the conveyor belts, especially when it is dyed with carbon. It should still be cleaned, dried and put in the recycling bin.
- Paper and cardboard can only be recycled if clean: no greasy pizza boxes, food-stained napkins or food-stained lunch boxes.
- Nappies must never go in the recycling bin. A person, not a machine, has to pull them out, and they contaminate everything around them.
- Disposable coffee cups are generally not recyclable, unless they are clearly labelled otherwise.

## Are We Separating Waste Well Enough?

The EPA's National Municipal Waste Characterisation Project, carried out every four years, found that **over two-thirds** of what goes into general (black) bins should have gone into recycling or organic waste bins instead. The EU Waste Directive requires every household to have a separate organic waste collection, and Irish rules have required all commercial premises to have one since July of the reporting year. Ireland is still falling well short on separation:

- 64% of food waste still goes into black bins
- Only around two-thirds of households (69%) have an organic (brown) bin
- 65% of household plastic goes into black bins instead of recycling bins
- 75% of commercial plastic waste goes into general bins rather than recycling

Other EPA data from the same period shows that **1.2 million tonnes** of packaging waste were produced in 2021. Of that, 84% of glass and 73% of cardboard were recycled, but only 28% of plastic, against a target of 55% plastic recycling. The rest of the plastic was burned, releasing around 2.7 kg of CO2 for every kilogram. A lot of plastic never reaches recycling at all, because it is mixed with other waste before it gets the chance.

## Recycling Performance vs EU Targets (2022 Data)

Ireland's recycling progress has stalled against the EU Green Deal targets, which require **55% of municipal waste** and 65% of packaging waste to be recycled by 2025. According to the EPA, Ireland currently recycles 41% of municipal waste and 60% of packaging waste, leaving it well short on the municipal side. Ireland is not alone: the European Environment Agency estimates that 18 of the 27 EU member states are likely to miss one or both targets.

Ireland produced **15.7 million tonnes of waste in 2022**, around 8kg per person per day. That was 1.9 million tonnes less than the year before. Most of the fall came from less construction and demolition waste, which probably reflects the ups and downs of the economy rather than a real cut in the waste we produce.
