# Ireland's Emissions and Climate Action Plans

Ireland has made real progress cutting greenhouse gas emissions in recent years, but independent assessments consistently find it falling well short of its legally binding 2030 target of a 51% reduction. This page brings together the national emissions data, the sector-by-sector picture (agriculture, transport, energy, land use), the country's key climate plans, and how climate change is already being felt in Ireland.

Further detail by sector is available from [Climate Ireland](https://www.climateireland.ie/) (agriculture, forestry, fisheries, water and more) and from [CSO.ie's environmental indicators](https://www.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-eii/environmentalindicatorsireland2022/energy/).

## National Emissions Overview

Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions for 1990-2022 (provisional figures based on SEAI's June 2023 energy balance) show total emissions of **60.76 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent (Mt CO2eq) in 2022**, a fall of 1.9% (1.19 Mt CO2eq) from 61.95 Mt CO2eq in 2021, which had itself risen 5.1% on the year before. 2022 emissions were 4.6% below pre-Covid 2019 levels.

Within 2022, emissions in the stationary ETS sector fell 4.3% and emissions under the Effort Sharing Regulation (ESR) fell 1.1%; including land use, land-use change and forestry (LULUCF), total national emissions fell 1.8%. Most large sectors saw emissions fall, with the exception of transport, waste and commercial services, which rose 6.0%, 4.9% and 0.2% respectively.

Emissions per capita fell from 12.4 tonnes CO2eq/person in 2021 to **11.9 tonnes CO2eq/person in 2022** (the ten-year average is 12.7 tonnes), a rate that still leaves Ireland **third-highest per capita in the EU**. With population projected to grow from 5.12 million (2022 census) to 5.5 million by 2030, 5.9 million by 2040 and 6.2 million by 2050, per capita emissions need to fall sharply just to keep total emissions flat: at current per capita levels, each additional 500,000 people would add around 6 million tonnes of CO2eq a year. Detailed breakdowns by sector and by gas are available from the [EPA's latest emissions data](https://www.epa.ie/our-services/monitoring--assessment/climate-change/ghg/latest-emissions-data/) and its [summary by gas](https://www.epa.ie/our-services/monitoring--assessment/climate-change/ghg/summary-by-gas/).

## Ireland as an EU Outlier

Ireland's emissions rose by **12% in late 2022**, making it an outlier among the 27 EU member states. The increase came alongside GDP growth, but other EU members managed to reduce emissions despite similar economic growth, underlining that decoupling economic growth from emissions has been one of the biggest challenges. Across the EU as a whole, 938 million tonnes of CO2 and other greenhouse gases were emitted that quarter, a 4% decrease on the same period in 2021. By the end of 2022, Ireland's average per-capita emissions were roughly twice the EU average.

## Closing the Emissions Gap (2024)

The government was forced to adopt a series of additional measures in 2024 to address an emissions gap totalling **26 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent**, needed to keep its legally binding 2026-2030 climate targets within reach, at an estimated cost of **€3-8 billion**. The additional measures included increased rail freight linked to ports, scaled-up heat pump deployment in buildings, developing green hydrogen capacity, increased use of low-carbon feeds in agriculture, investment in additional electricity interconnectors, and more "long duration battery storage" to pair with wind and solar generation.

Carbon capture and storage in cement manufacturing and incineration was also due to be adopted, with the potential to cut emissions by 500,000 tonnes of CO2; then-Minister for Climate Eamon Ryan called these measures "challenging but do-able." Further measures not yet factored into verified reductions included a national biomethane strategy and legislation enabling district heating schemes. Combined, these measures were projected to deliver 42% of the emissions reductions needed by 2030, with government-appointed taskforces on transport, heat and the built environment, and agriculture tasked with identifying further measures to close the remaining gap to the 51% target.

## 2023: Lowest Emissions in Three Decades

Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions fell **6.8% in 2023**, with reductions in almost all sectors, taking overall emissions to their lowest level in three decades, according to the EPA. This represented a fall of 4 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent compared with 2022, and the agency concluded the data "indicates a move towards reducing carbon emissions at the scale and pace required to meet [Ireland's] climate ambition of a 51 per cent reduction by 2030."

EPA director general Laura Burke described the yearly fall as the largest reduction in emissions outside of a recession, attributing it to more renewables and interconnection powering electricity, less fossil fuel use in home heating, reduced nitrogen fertiliser use in agriculture, and more biofuel in transport. She cautioned, however, that "while these are positive results for the year 2023, we are still well off track in terms of meeting EU and national 2030 targets. We need to maintain and further build momentum."

Key figures from 2023:

- Emissions per capita fell from 11.4 to **10.4 tonnes CO2eq per person**; total emissions were **55 million tonnes CO2eq**, excluding LULUCF.
- Despite the progress, current trends mean legally-binding carbon budgets are unlikely to be met, with compliance costs for Irish taxpayers potentially exceeding **€5 billion by 2030**.
- Energy industry emissions fell **21.6%** to **7.8 million tonnes**, driven by a twelvefold increase in imported electricity (9.5% of electricity supply), a rise in renewable energy to 40.7%, and reduced use of coal, oil and peat.
- Agriculture emissions fell **4.6%** to **20.8 million tonnes**, due to an 18% reduction in fertiliser nitrogen use, reduced lime application and fewer livestock overall; dairy cow numbers rose 0.6% even as total milk production fell 4.7%.
- Residential emissions fell **7%** to **5.3 million tonnes**, the second large annual reduction in a row, driven by high fuel prices, a milder winter, and new solid fuel regulations. More than 30,000 heat pumps were installed in 2023, bringing the total installed to 120,000.
- Transport emissions rose marginally, **0.3%** to **11.8 million tonnes**, though they remained 4.3% below pre-Covid 2019 levels; growth in EVs and biofuel use partly offset a 3% increase in the national vehicle fleet.

Including land use, emissions were 7.8% lower than in 2018, still well off the 51% reduction ambition for 2030. The EPA concluded that Ireland needed "an extremely challenging annual reduction of 8.3 per cent for each of the years 2024 and 2025" to stay within its first carbon budget (2021-2025).

## On Course to Miss the 2030 Target

By mid-2023, the EPA's outlook had turned more pessimistic: Ireland was "on course to miss its 2030 climate targets by a large margin" unless all sectors, including agriculture, electricity and transport, rapidly delivered and sustained further emissions reductions. Against a goal of a **51% reduction**, the EPA's projection at the time put Ireland on course for only around **29%**, with strong economic growth continuing to work against the targets.

Sector-level projections at that point were:

- **Agriculture**: emissions projected to fall 4-21%, through a mix of reduced fertiliser use and lower bovine numbers.
- **Transport**: targeted to fall 35%.
- **Energy**: targeted to cut energy industry emissions by 60% and reach 80% renewable electricity, though progress had slowed due to continued reliance on coal and the geopolitical situation.
- **Land use**: the LULUCF sector was projected to see emissions increase from 2021 to 2030 as Ireland's forests reach harvesting age and shift from a carbon sink to a carbon source; planned measures such as increased afforestation, water table management on agricultural organic soils, and peatland rehabilitation are projected to limit the scale of that increase.

A subsequent Irish Times report (January 2026) found the picture had worsened further: instead of a 51% reduction, Ireland was tracking closer to a **20-25% reduction**, driven largely by delays in ramping up wind power, which accounts for most of the energy sector's planned emissions cuts. At that point, around 10% of Ireland's electricity came from solar, not counting rooftop installations, and two large offshore wind projects in the pipeline could add 5 GW of power, more than current demand. However, electricity demand itself is expected to double by 2050, driven by both population growth and electrification of heating and transport.

## Ireland Climate Action Plan 2023 (CAP23)

The government's Climate Action Plan 2023 sets out the policies and measures across every sector needed to meet Ireland's climate targets. The full plan and summary are available from [gov.ie](https://www.gov.ie/en/publication/7bd8c-climate-action-plan-2023/).

## July 2026: Only 10 of 74 Climate Commitments Delivered

A progress report from the Department of Climate and the Environment found the government has completed just **10 of the 74 measures** committed to across the 2023, 2024 and 2025 Climate Action Plans. The 2026 plan itself was still unpublished at the time, delayed to Q3 2026.

Completed items: publication of the second Circular Economy Strategy, 200km of cycling infrastructure, a new national demand management strategy for transport. The remaining **64 measures** are attributed to capacity constraints, consultation requirements, funding/procurement issues, and alignment with other national or EU policy — delays the report itself warns "threaten Ireland's ability to meet" its binding emissions targets.

Notably, **neither of the two agriculture-specific commitments** in the 2023-2025 plans has been delivered: a framework to reduce feed costs for organic livestock, and a draft carbon farming framework, now over two years late. This matters because agriculture was Ireland's highest-emitting sector in 2025 at **36% of national emissions**, with methane (mostly cattle, sheep and manure management) making up **70%** of that sector's total.

Other undelivered measures include shared mobility hubs in four cities/towns, ~225 EV charging points at sports clubs, a roadmap to phase out fossil fuel heating in buildings, and an OPW retrofit programme for historic buildings.

Taoiseach Micheál Martin called the findings "a clear reminder that we must move faster"; Climate Minister Darragh O'Brien said there is "no room for delay."

## Land Restoration in the Dublin Mountains

Under a new initiative, **2,000 hectares** of land in the Dublin mountains are to be restored. The project aims to boost biodiversity, reduce soil erosion, improve water quality and keep carbon locked in the ground, through planting new trees, fencing to keep out sheep, and removing invasive species. There is much more still to be done on land use across Ireland; see [Ireland Land Use, Soil and Forestry](../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Land%20use%2C%20Soil%20and%20Forestry.md).

## Waste Separation Falling Short

According to an EPA report, around two-thirds of the material placed in general (black) waste bins should have been placed in recycling or organic waste bins instead. The EPA's National Municipal Waste Characterisation Project, which runs every four years, found:

- **64%** of food waste still placed in black bins
- Only **69%** of households have an organic (brown) bin
- **65%** of household plastic placed in black bins instead of recycling bins
- **75%** of commercial plastic waste placed in general bins

Other EPA data from the same period: **1.2 million tonnes** of packaging waste were generated in 2021; **84%** of glass and **73%** of cardboard were recycled, but only **28%** of plastic was recycled, with the rest incinerated (releasing around 2.7kg of CO2 per kilogram burned), against a target of 55% plastic recycling. For a fuller picture of Ireland's waste system, see [Ireland's Waste Management, Recycling and Plastic Problem](../ireland-hub/IRELAND%20Waste%20Management%2C%20Recycling%20and%20Plastic.md).

## Climate Impacts Already Being Felt in Ireland

**The "cold blob."** Global warming has produced a striking anomaly in the North Atlantic that is already shaping Ireland's weather: a cooling patch that is protecting Ireland from the worst summer heat seen on the continent. This cold patch is caused by a significant slowdown in the Gulf Stream, the flow of warm water that travels north-eastward across the Atlantic from the coast of Florida and acts as a kind of central heating for the seas west of Ireland, keeping the climate temperate. Every other part of the world has been getting warmer; only this patch of the Atlantic has not. Scientists expect the Gulf Stream to weaken by around 30%, driven partly by falling water salinity from Greenland ice melt and increased rainfall. The cold blob is expected to mean warmer summers across Europe generally, but because of where Ireland sits, it should escape the worst of that heat.

**Weather extremes.** Recent storms in Ireland have grown significantly stronger as a result of climate change, according to climatologist Paul Moore. Ireland's average temperature is now **1.59°C above the 1961-1990 baseline**, and almost 1°C above the more recent 1991-2020 baseline. Storm Éowyn, in January 2025, recorded the strongest wind gust ever measured in Ireland, at **184 km/h**. A marine heatwave in April 2025 was also linked to climate change.

## Agriculture: Ireland's Highest-Emitting Sector

Agriculture was responsible for almost **40% of Ireland's emissions in 2020**, according to CSO data, far above the roughly 25% global average for agriculture (itself the third-largest emitting sector after industry and transport, of which 80% comes from livestock; see [Carbon Emission FACTS](../concepts/key-ideas/Carbon%20Emission%20FACTS.md)). Agriculture's emissions rose **11%** over the preceding decade, but the gross value of agricultural production rose far more, by **71%**, showing some decoupling between emissions and economic activity.

On a more positive note, household emissions fell **21%** over the same decade, to **15 million tonnes**. Of household emissions, 46% come from heating, 32% from private driving and 20% from electricity. The main driver of the reduction was a shift away from oil heating (common in the early 2000s) towards electric heating, which accounted for 49% of new dwellings between 2015 and 2020.

**Incentives for farmers to protect nature.** A new scheme, the **Wild Atlantic Nature Scheme**, incentivises farmers to protect nature based on land quality, the biodiversity it supports, and its value for carbon capture and water filtration, with bogs and peatland especially targeted. Farmers are paid more for land that is in better condition and worth protecting, an approach some farmers see as the future of land management. Total direct payments to farmers under the scheme in 2022 exceeded **€2.4 million**, with individual payments ranging from €800 to more than €10,000 and averaging €3,100. The scheme focuses on blanket bogs because, as one farmer put it, "they're the highest quality habitats that we have, from a European perspective, they're like our rainforests here in Ireland. We have about 85% of European blanket bogs."

## Public Awareness and the Path Ahead

In January 2023, the EPA launched **Ireland's Climate Change Assessment (ICCA)**, the first comprehensive assessment of Ireland's understanding of, and response to, climate change, alongside a second report, **Climate in the Irish Mind**, a national survey of climate attitudes. Dr Eimear Cotter, Director of the EPA's Office of Evidence and Assessment, has discussed both reports' key findings and the scale of the challenge ahead, while stressing there is still an opportunity to reduce climate impacts and build resilience.

Key findings from the ICCA include that a recent flooding event was found to be twice as likely with 1°C of additional warming, and that rainfall intensity has increased by 7% over the past 30 years. Other documented impacts include the spread of invasive species, and pressure on coastal communities, coastal erosion, agriculture and infrastructure.

Nearly all sectors are reducing emissions except agriculture, which remains Ireland's largest emitting sector, an unusual position within the EU given the scale of support it receives. Proposed solutions include expanding forestry (from around 8,000 hectares planted a year towards a target of 35,000 hectares), pursuing a climate-neutral agricultural system by 2050, diversifying beyond livestock, and scaling up research and innovation, including new feed additives and fertilisers that still need to move from trials into the mainstream.

On energy, roughly 50% of Ireland's electricity comes from renewables, but transport, heat and data centres remain major unresolved challenges, and around 86% of Ireland's overall energy system still depends on fossil fuels despite the progress made. To reach the 51% reduction target by 2030, Ireland needs an average annual emissions reduction of around 12%; actual progress has been closer to 2%. Progress is being eroded by continued economic reliance on fossil fuels, a link that needs to be broken in favour of clean, renewable energy. There is also a persistent public confusion about which individual actions matter most (flying has an outsized impact; recycling's impact is often overestimated relative to measures like dietary change), pointing to a need for clearer public education on where individual impact is greatest.
