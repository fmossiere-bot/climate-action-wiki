# Average Carbon Footprint in Ireland

The average carbon footprint in Ireland depends on how you measure it. You can count what the country produces (territorial emissions) or what its residents consume (the lifestyle footprint). The latest data from the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), along with economic analysis, shows that the two measures give quite different pictures.

## The numbers at a glance

| Metric | Estimate (per person) | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Production-based | 10.4 tonnes CO2e | Emissions released within Irish borders (e.g. farming, power plants, driving) |
| Consumption-based | ~11.5-12.7 tonnes CO2e | Emissions caused by Irish lifestyles, including imported goods (cars, electronics) minus exports |

## Production-based footprint (the official number)

The current figure is **10.4 tonnes CO2 equivalent (CO2e)** per person, based on 2023 provisional data from the EPA (July 2024). This is the standard figure reported to the EU and UN. It takes all the greenhouse gases emitted within Ireland and divides them by the population. Ireland has the highest greenhouse gas emissions per person in the EU, often around 50% above the EU average.

The main drivers are:

- **Agriculture (~38%)**: unlike in most wealthy countries, a large share of Ireland's production footprint is methane from cattle and nitrous oxide from fertiliser
- **Transport (~21%)**: heavy reliance on private cars and a spread-out population
- **Energy (~14%)**: renewables are growing, but Ireland still relies on fossil fuels for electricity and heating

The figure fell from 11.4 tonnes in 2022, mostly because Ireland imported more electricity and used less lime and fertiliser.

### Comparison with EU neighbours

Ireland stands out in Europe. The EU average is around 7-8 tonnes per person, compared with Ireland's 10.4+ tonnes. In most EU countries, agriculture is a small slice of emissions. In Ireland, it is the largest slice. Ireland's housing pattern also plays a part: many people live in one-off rural houses rather than apartments, which raises transport and heating emissions compared with continental Europe.

## Consumption-based footprint (the "real" lifestyle impact)

The consumption-based estimate is roughly **10% to 22% higher** than the production figure (11.5 to 12.7 tonnes, against 10.4), according to analysis from the Economic and Social Research Institute (ESRI) and the Global Carbon Project. This measure accounts for trade. It subtracts the emissions of goods Ireland exports (such as beef and dairy) and adds the emissions of goods Ireland imports (such as cars, clothes, phones and machinery).

You might expect Ireland's consumption footprint to be lower, since the country exports so much food and the methane that comes with it. In practice, comfortable Irish lifestyles rely on a lot of imported manufactured goods. The emissions built into those imports, often from China, the UK or elsewhere in the EU, outweigh the emissions "sent away" with beef exports. The average Irish resident is a net importer of emissions, so a personal lifestyle footprint is likely closer to 12 tonnes than the official 10.4 tonnes suggests.

### Where your ~12.5 tonnes comes from

Switching from production to consumption changes the shape of the footprint. The large agriculture slice shrinks, because most beef is exported, while transport and housing grow. Based on data from SEAI, the EPA and the ESRI, the average Irish person's consumption footprint of roughly 12.5 tonnes breaks down like this:

| Category | Share of footprint | Estimated impact | Why is it so high? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Transport | ~30% | 3.8 tonnes | Car dependency: diesel and petrol burned, plus the embodied carbon of manufacturing the car (an imported emission) |
| 2. Housing & energy | ~25% | 3.1 tonnes | Heating oil and poor insulation: Ireland relies unusually heavily on oil boilers (36% of new homes in the early 2000s) and spread-out housing |
| 3. Food | ~25% | 3.1 tonnes | Red meat and dairy: a high-meat diet, plus imported foods such as fruit and vegetables out of season |
| 4. "Stuff" & services | ~20% | 2.5 tonnes | Imported goods: the emissions from manufacturing phones, clothes and furniture, often in China or elsewhere in Asia |

### Transport: the biggest personal contributor

In production statistics, transport is around 21% of emissions. In a person's lifestyle footprint, it is closer to **30%**. The Central Statistics Office (CSO) says that 36% of direct household emissions come purely from burning fuel in cars. This category also includes flying, which is not fully counted in national production totals but makes up a large part of a person's consumption footprint, along with the emissions from making the vehicle itself. Because Ireland's population is so spread out and rural public transport is comparatively poor, people drive longer distances than the EU average.

### Housing and energy: the "leaky sieve" problem

According to the CSO, **41%** of direct household emissions come from fossil fuels used for heating, and another 21% from electricity use. The consumption view also adds the embodied carbon of the cement and steel used to build a house. Ireland has an older housing stock that loses heat and relies heavily on kerosene (home heating oil) and, in older homes, peat or coal. Much of continental Europe relies more on district heating or gas.

### Food: from 38% (agriculture) to 25%

Agriculture makes up around 38% of production-based emissions. Food makes up around 25% of the consumption footprint. The gap is wider than it looks, because the 38% agriculture figure includes emissions from beef that feeds around 40 million people abroad. Even after removing exports, the average Irish diet is still high in emissions-heavy foods such as dairy, beef and lamb compared with a plant-based diet. The EPA estimates the average Irish household throws away **€700-€1,000** worth of food a year. The carbon emitted to grow, ship and package that wasted food is part of this figure.

### "Stuff": the invisible emissions

Imported goods are the one category missing entirely from national production totals. If you buy a fast-fashion item made in Bangladesh or a smartphone made in China, the emissions were released there, but the responsibility belongs to you as the buyer. ESRI analysis confirms that Ireland is a net importer of emissions in this category: Irish consumers buy more carbon-heavy manufactured goods than the country sells.

## Separating out waste

A revised view treats waste as a category of its own. It removes the carbon from thrown-away food out of the food category, and the carbon from discarded textiles and packaging out of the consumption category. This shows exactly how much carbon comes from pure waste.

### Revised Irish consumption footprint: ~12.7 tonnes CO2e

| Category | Est. tonnes CO2e | % of total | What is included |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Transport | 3.8 t | 30% | The commute and travel: fuel burned in cars and public transport, vehicle manufacturing, and international flights |
| 2. Housing | 3.1 t | 24% | Heat and light: kerosene and gas for heating, electricity use, and home maintenance and materials |
| 3. Consumption | 2.3 t | 18% | Stuff you keep: clothing, electronics, furniture and services (insurance, banking) that are actually used, not discarded |
| 4. Food | 2.4 t | 19% | Calories eaten: the carbon cost of meat, dairy and plant food actually eaten by the household |
| 5. Waste | 1.1 t | 9% | Waste: wasted food, fast fashion thrown out quickly, packaging, and the energy used to collect and treat household bins |
| **Total** | **~12.7 t** | **100%** | Average Irish resident |

The **1.1 tonnes** of waste breaks down further. Roughly 65% is food waste (growing, shipping and chilling food that ends up in the bin), 20% is material waste (textiles, plastics and tech bought and thrown out quickly), and 15% is processing (the emissions from bin collection and incineration or landfill). It is perhaps the most frustrating category, because it is carbon emitted for no benefit to anyone's diet or life.
