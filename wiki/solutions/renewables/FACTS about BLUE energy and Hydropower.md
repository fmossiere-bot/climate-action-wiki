# Facts about Blue Energy and Hydropower

**Blue energy** refers to renewable power generated using new technologies in the ocean, harnessing the power of waves, currents, or temperature differences between water layers. These technologies show real potential but are still at an early stage, unlikely to contribute massively to global energy supply within the next five years. Even so, their theoretical potential is enormous: estimates suggest the available production power is roughly **four times current global energy needs** (see [CNBC's coverage of wave power](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/09/07/why-wave-power-could-complement-solar-and-wind.html)).

## Hydropower: bigger is not always better

From an ecological standpoint, bigger hydropower projects are not necessarily better. Large dams have delivered very cheap electricity and other valuable development services, such as irrigation water, to millions of people worldwide. But they also cause significant deforestation, biodiversity loss, and forced human displacement. Research suggests [the costs of mega dams can outweigh their benefits](http://news.mongabay.com/2018/11/mega-dam-costs-outweigh-benefits-global-building-spree-should-end-experts/).

Large dams are also not automatically climate-friendly. In tropical regions, large dams can [contribute more emissions than fossil fuel power plants](https://www.nature.com/articles/nclimate1540) for decades after construction, as forests submerged by the reservoir decompose underwater and release methane, a potent greenhouse gas. Despite these downsides, [more than 500 new dams](https://www.worldwildlife.org/magazine/issues/spring-2021/articles/wwf-study-finds-509-new-dams-planned-or-under-construction-in-protected-areas) are planned or under construction in protected areas around the world, with a large share of those projects located in China.

**Mini- and micro-hydro projects are a different story entirely.** These smaller-scale systems can power local mini-grids and provide additional services, such as water reservoirs for the dry season, without the same ecological toll. They tend to make the most sense in hilly regions with perennial streams and waterfalls. For more detail, see the [World Small Hydropower Development Report](https://www.unido.org/our-focus-safeguarding-environment-clean-energy-access-productive-use-renewable-energy-focus-areas-small-hydro-power/world-small-hydropower-development-report) from UNIDO. The director of the [Hydro Empowerment Network](https://www.hpnet.org/) is a mentor with Terra.do for anyone wanting to learn more about small-scale hydro.
