# First Osmotic Power Plants

Japan has switched on Asia's first osmotic power plant, in Fukuoka. It runs 24 hours a day using only fresh water and seawater. It is the second plant of its kind in the world, after one that opened in Denmark in 2023. Japan's plant is larger than the Danish one, a real step forward for a renewable energy source that is still little used but shows promise.

## What is osmotic power?

**Osmosis** is the natural process that helps plants draw water up from the soil and keeps our own cells hydrated. Water moves through a special membrane from a less salty side, such as fresh water, towards a saltier side, such as seawater. Osmotic power plants put this movement to work.

Fresh water, or treated wastewater, sits on one side of a membrane. On the other side is seawater, made even saltier with leftover brine from a desalination plant. The difference in saltiness pulls the fresh water across the membrane and builds up pressure on the saltwater side. That pressure drives a turbine, which generates electricity.

## What the numbers look like

Fukuoka's plant is expected to generate about **880,000 kilowatt-hours of electricity a year**. That is enough to help run a nearby desalination plant and supply around 220 homes. It is roughly what two football pitches of solar panels would produce, with one difference: osmotic power keeps going day and night, in any weather.

The process is fully renewable and produces no carbon dioxide. Because there is effectively no shortage of seawater, osmotic power is seen as a stable source of electricity that can run around the clock, every day of the year.

Today, osmotic power makes only a tiny fraction of the world's electricity. Researchers believe that if the remaining technical problems can be solved, it could meet up to **15% of global energy demand by 2050**.
