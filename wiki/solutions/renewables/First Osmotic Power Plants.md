# First Osmotic Power Plants

Japan has switched on Asia's first osmotic power plant, in Fukuoka, a facility that runs 24 hours a day using nothing but fresh water and seawater. It is only the second plant of its kind in the world, following one that opened in Denmark in 2023. Japan's version is larger than its Danish predecessor and marks a real step forward for this little-used but promising renewable energy source.

## What is osmotic power?

Osmosis is the same natural process that helps plants draw water up from soil and keeps our own cells hydrated: the movement of water from an area of low salt concentration, such as fresh water, towards an area of high salt concentration, such as seawater, through a special membrane. Osmotic power plants put this passive movement to work.

Fresh water, or treated wastewater, sits on one side of a membrane, while seawater, made even saltier by concentrating leftover brine from a desalination process, sits on the other. The difference in salinity pulls the fresh water across the membrane, building up pressure on the saltwater side. That pressure is then used to drive a turbine, generating electricity.

## What the numbers look like

Fukuoka's plant is expected to generate about **880,000 kilowatt-hours of electricity per year**, enough to help run a nearby desalination facility and supply around **220 homes**. That is roughly equivalent to the output of two football pitches' worth of solar panels, with one key difference: osmotic power keeps generating day and night, in any weather.

The process is entirely renewable and produces no carbon dioxide. Because oceans and seas are effectively boundless for this purpose, osmotic power is considered a genuinely stable source of electricity that can operate around the clock, every day of the year.

Today, osmotic power still contributes only a microscopic fraction of global electricity generation. But researchers believe that if the remaining technical challenges can be solved, it could eventually meet up to **15% of global energy demand by 2050**.
