# Facts about Solar Energy

## Solar farms need a lot of space

A solar farm requires roughly **40 to 50 times** the land area of a coal power plant producing the same output. That is one reason floating solar farms, built on water, are becoming more common. They save land, and the water underneath reflects extra light onto the panels, so they produce more.

## Ways to boost solar output and save space

**SolarisFloat**, an installation by the company Proteus, turns to follow the sun so it catches as much light as possible. That raises production by **40%**. It uses 180 panels on a round structure 34 metres across, is running in the Netherlands, and has a peak capacity of 73kWp (73 kilowatts of power in full sun) (see [SolarisFloat.com](https://www.solarisfloat.com)).

**Agrivoltaics**, where crops and solar panels share the same land, can also help. Research suggests the panels stay cooler in these combined systems. The reasons are taller panel mounts, more reflective ground cover, and the crops beneath releasing more water vapour (evapotranspiration), which cools the air. Taller installations seem to benefit both the crops and the panels (see [Agriland's coverage of the research](https://www.agriland.ie/farming-news/research-co-locating-crops-and-solar-can-increase-performance/)).

## The basics of solar photovoltaic technology

According to the [US National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL)](https://www.nrel.gov/research/re-photovoltaics.html), the word photovoltaics (PV) comes from turning light (photons) into electricity (voltage), a process known as the **photovoltaic effect**. Three main types of solar cell are in use today:

- **Silicon solar cells**: the large majority of today's solar cells are [made from silicon](https://www.nrel.gov/pv/silicon-materials-devices-rd.html). They offer a good balance of reasonable cost and solid efficiency (the share of sunlight a cell turns into electricity).
- **Thin-film solar cells**: [made from very thin layers](https://www.nrel.gov/pv/polycrystalline-thin-film-photovoltaics.html) of semiconductor material such as cadmium telluride or copper indium gallium diselenide, only a few micrometres thick (millionths of a metre). Because they are flexible and light, they suit portable uses, such as a soldier's backpack, or products like windows that generate electricity from sunlight.
- **III-V solar cells**: named for the elements that make them up, mainly from Group III (such as gallium and indium) and Group V (such as arsenic and antimony) of the periodic table. These [cells](https://www.nrel.gov/pv/high-efficiency-iii-v-solar-cells.html) cost much more to make than the alternatives, but they turn far more of the sunlight into electricity. That makes them the first choice for satellites, drones and other uses where power for the weight matters most.

Researchers at NREL and elsewhere are also working on newer kinds of solar cell, including cells made from [organic materials](https://www.nrel.gov/pv/organic-photovoltaic-solar-cells.html), [quantum dots](https://www.nrel.gov/news/press/2017/scientists-elevate-quantum-dot-solar-cell-world-record.html), and hybrid organic-inorganic materials known as [perovskites](https://www.nrel.gov/pv/perovskite-solar-cells.html). They may eventually be cheaper or easier to make, but more research is needed to see whether that holds up.
