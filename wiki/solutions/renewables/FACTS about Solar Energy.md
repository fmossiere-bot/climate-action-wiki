# Facts about Solar Energy

## Solar farms need a lot of space

A solar farm requires roughly **40 to 50 times** the land area of a coal power plant producing the same output. This is one reason floating solar farms, built directly on water, are becoming more common: alongside saving land, the water beneath the panels reflects additional light onto them, making them more productive.

## Clever ways to boost solar output and manage space

**SolarisFloat**, an installation by the company Proteus, tracks the sun to maximise the light it captures, increasing production by **40%**. The installation uses 180 panels across a 34-metre diameter structure and is operating in the Netherlands, with a potential output of 73kWh (see [SolarisFloat.com](https://www.solarisfloat.com)).

**Agrivoltaics**, where crops and solar panels share the same land, can also improve performance. Research suggests these combined systems offer increased active cooling, thanks to taller panel heights, more reflective ground cover, and a higher rate of evapotranspiration from the crops beneath. Taller panel installations appear to bring a particular dual benefit here (see [Agriland's coverage of the research](https://www.agriland.ie/farming-news/research-co-locating-crops-and-solar-can-increase-performance/)).

## The basics of solar photovoltaic technology

According to the [US National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL)](https://www.nrel.gov/research/re-photovoltaics.html), the term photovoltaics (PV) comes from the process of converting light (photons) into electricity (voltage), known as the **photovoltaic effect**. Three main types of solar cell technology are in use today:

- **Silicon solar cells**: the large majority of today's solar cells are [made from silicon](https://www.nrel.gov/pv/silicon-materials-devices-rd.html), offering a good balance of reasonable cost and solid efficiency, the rate at which a cell converts sunlight into electricity.
- **Thin-film solar cells**: [made from very thin layers](https://www.nrel.gov/pv/polycrystalline-thin-film-photovoltaics.html) of semiconductor material such as cadmium telluride or copper indium gallium diselenide, only a few micrometres thick (millionths of a metre). Their flexibility and light weight make them well suited to portable applications, such as a soldier's backpack, or to products like windows that generate electricity from sunlight.
- **III-V solar cells**: named for the elements that make them up, mainly from Group III (such as gallium and indium) and Group V (such as arsenic and antimony) of the periodic table. These [cells](https://www.nrel.gov/pv/high-efficiency-iii-v-solar-cells.html) are considerably more expensive to manufacture than the alternatives, but convert sunlight into electricity at much higher efficiencies. That makes them the preferred choice for satellites, unmanned aerial vehicles, and other applications where the ratio of power to weight matters most.

Researchers at NREL and elsewhere are also pursuing newer photovoltaic technologies, including cells made from [organic materials](https://www.nrel.gov/pv/organic-photovoltaic-solar-cells.html), [quantum dots](https://www.nrel.gov/news/press/2017/scientists-elevate-quantum-dot-solar-cell-world-record.html), and hybrid organic-inorganic materials known as [perovskites](https://www.nrel.gov/pv/perovskite-solar-cells.html). These next-generation technologies may eventually offer lower costs or easier manufacturing, though further research is needed to confirm whether these promises hold up.
