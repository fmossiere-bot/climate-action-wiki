## How Much Wind Power Is Out There

According to the [Global Wind Energy Council](https://gwec.net/global-wind-report-2022/), total global wind power capacity reached 837 GW in its 2022 report. That capacity saves over **1.2 billion tonnes of CO2** a year, roughly the annual carbon emissions of South America. Over the last decade, wind has been the world's main renewable technology after hydropower, and capacity was projected to double by 2027.

The [13MW GE Haliade-X turbine](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/01/01/business/GE-wind-turbine.html), featured in the New York Times in January 2021, has a blade 107 metres long and can power about 12,000 homes on its own.

Wind turbines can be **onshore** (on land) or **offshore** (out at sea), and offshore turbines can now even [float](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2021/aug/29/floating-wind-turbines-ocean-renewable-power) rather than being fixed to the seabed. As the sections below on Taiwan and Scotland show, offshore turbine foundations can also become a home for marine life.

### How much energy do wind turbines actually produce?

Every wind turbine has a range of wind speeds, typically around 30 to 55 mph, in which it produces its rated (maximum) output. At lower wind speeds, output falls off sharply: if the wind speed halves, power drops by a factor of eight. So on average, turbines rarely run close to their rated capacity. Industry estimates put annual output at 30-40% of capacity, but real-world experience shows 15-30% is more typical. This figure is called the **capacity factor**.

For example, a 2MW turbine with a 25% capacity factor would produce:

2 MW × 365 days × 24 hours × 25% = 4,380 MWh (4,380,000 kWh) in a year.

### 2021 in review, from the Global Wind Energy Council

- The global wind industry had its second-best year in 2021, adding almost 94 GW of capacity, only 1.8% behind 2020's record.
- Europe, Latin America, and Africa and the Middle East had record years for new onshore wind. Even so, total onshore installations in 2021 were 18% lower than the year before, because the two largest onshore markets, China and the US, slowed down.
- 21.1 GW of offshore wind was commissioned in 2021, three times more than in 2020. It was the best year in offshore wind history, and offshore's share of global new installations rose to 22.5%.
- China accounted for 80% of the offshore wind capacity added worldwide in 2021, bringing its total to 27.7 GW. It took Europe three decades to reach a similar total.
- Wind auctions bounced back in 2021, with more than 88 GW of capacity awarded globally, 153% more than in 2020.
- With more countries committing to net zero and energy security back on the agenda, the outlook was positive. Under the policies in place at the time, 557 GW of new capacity was expected over the following five years, more than 110 GW a year through 2026.
- Even so, that growth needed to roughly quadruple by the end of the decade for the world to stay on a 1.5°C pathway and reach net zero by 2050.

## The Hidden Cost of Wind Power: Balsa Wood and Deforestation

Wind power's growth has had costs of its own. Reporting from the [Rainforest Journalism Fund](https://rainforestjournalismfund.org/stories/how-wind-power-boom-driving-deforestation-amazon) has linked the wind farm boom to deforestation and harm to local communities in the Amazon. The cause is demand for **balsa wood**, which is used in turbine blades in Europe, and increasingly in China.

Turbines already installed, with blades up to 80 metres long, can sweep an area of about 21,000 square metres, roughly three football pitches. Newer designs with blades up to 100 metres long can use around 150 cubic metres of balsa wood each, which is several tonnes. Demand rose by 75%, mostly supplied from the Ecuadorian Amazon. That drove illegal logging and unsustainable deforestation, and as prices rose, communities were offered bribes to let loggers in.

The clearest fix is to use less balsa and switch to other materials, which several manufacturers are already doing. Alternatives were used in blade production from at least 2014 and moved into full production after supply problems in 2020. One is Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET), a [low-density foam made from recycled plastic bottles](https://www.lmwindpower.com/en/stories-and-press/stories/sustainability/from-a-plastic-bottle-to-a-wind-turbine-blade). The consultancy Wood Mackenzie forecast that PET's share of blade material would rise from 20% in 2018 to more than 55% by 2023, while demand for balsa would stay broadly stable.

## Recycling Wind Turbine Blades

The industry has started to deal with what happens to blades at the end of their life. RWE's Sofia offshore wind farm, then under construction as the UK's largest, agreed with Siemens Gamesa to use **recyclable blades** on 44 of its 100 turbines. The aim was for 50% of Sofia's blades to be made in the UK. It was the largest order of recyclable blades in the world, and followed their successful installation at RWE's Kaskasi wind farm off the German island of Heligoland.

Siemens Gamesa's recyclable blades use a new kind of resin that can be cleanly separated from the other materials at the end of the blade's life. The materials can then be reused in new cast products, such as car parts, or consumer goods like suitcases and flatscreen casings. Other technologies are being developed to recycle the conventional blades already in service, which were not designed to be recycled.

Blades can also be reused whole. In a new high-tech neighbourhood in Lund, southern Sweden, blades from a decommissioned wind farm now form a visible part of the façade of an eco-friendly multi-storey car park. Turbine blades are built to generate electricity and survive rough weather for decades. They are made of composite materials, such as glass fibre, carbon fibre, epoxy resin, balsa wood, metals and various fillers, pressed tightly together in layers. That makes them extremely durable, but hard to recycle by normal means.

## Wind Energy Costs: China's Breakthrough

The cost of installing a wind turbine in China hit a record low, falling 45% in a year to roughly one-fifth of the equivalent cost in the United States, according to the [South China Morning Post](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/science/article/3257036/historic-breakthrough-chinas-installed-wind-turbine-cost-drops-one-fifth-us-green-energy-race). The price fell to just over 2 yuan (US$0.28) per watt, down from the previous year's lowest domestic price of 3.9 yuan per watt, and well below the roughly $1.50 per watt typical in the US. One large project in the Gobi desert is thought to be partly behind the drop. China installed less onshore wind than the year before, but its total wind capacity grew from 4 GW in 2007 to **329 GW in 2021**, a 31% annual growth rate, according to researchers from Xi'an Jiaotong University and Harvard's China Project on Energy, Economy and Environment.

## 2026: Offshore Wind as an Accidental Reef, with 86 New Fish Species at Formosa (Taiwan)

Peer-reviewed research published in Frontiers in Marine Science in 2026 shows an unusually clear benefit for nature from offshore wind. Researchers surveyed the Formosa Wind Farm off Miaoli County in Taiwan, which has 69 turbines across roughly 90 km² of what used to be flat, sandy seabed. SCUBA teams dived there in 2017, 2018 and 2025. The 2025 survey found **86 species of reef fish** living only within 50 metres of the turbine foundations. None of those 86 species had appeared in any earlier fishery record for that patch of sea.

### How the reef assembled

- **Within months of construction**: barnacles, sponges and corals settled on the steel
- **Then**: shrimp, crabs, bristle worms, gastropods, mussels, whelks and starfish moved in
- **Later**: groupers, snappers and lobsters arrived to feed and spawn
- **By 2025**: the number and mix of species, and the food chain around the foundations, could not be told apart statistically from established, purpose-built artificial reefs in the region

The foundations work like a **"cliff face"**: a vertical steel column from seabed to surface, with rough welds, bolted flanges and a ring of protective boulders at the base. That shape draws in species that live near the surface, in midwater and on the bottom, all at once. Purpose-built reefs rarely manage that across the whole depth of the water.

### Attraction or production?

Scientists still debate whether structures like these **produce new fish** or simply draw existing fish away from the surrounding water. At Formosa, the 86 species had never been recorded locally before construction, which points to more than fish just moving around. A wider review in *Fish and Fisheries* reaches a similar conclusion. Hard vertical structures increase the number and variety of reef fish nearby, while the community living on the soft seabed further away stays largely the same.

### What this means for the wider debate

- Where turbines stand on flat, featureless seabed, **offshore wind can cut emissions and boost wildlife at the same time**, without a separate marine reserve programme
- The result does not automatically apply to floating offshore wind, turbines close to shore, or seabeds that already hold valuable habitat
- There is a parallel on land: [solar farms on rewetted peatland](../../biodiversity-land/solar-on-peatland.md) attracted a wider range of bird species than nearby hay fields, but only when the peat was rewetted first

## 2026: Floating Wind Shows the Same Effect at Scotland's Hywind Pilot Park

A second, separate case extends the reef effect to **floating** offshore wind, the technology that lets turbines move into deeper water where fixed foundations cannot reach. A remote-controlled underwater robot inspected 41 underwater components across the five floating turbines of Equinor's Hywind Scotland Pilot Park, 12 miles off the Scottish coast. These turbines are held in place by mooring cables and suction anchors rather than fixed to the seabed.

Researchers recorded **121 distinct forms of sea life** on the mooring lines, steel frames and anchors. Near the surface there were dense clusters of orange starfish, sea urchins and plumose sea anemones. Deeper down, squat lobsters, brown crabs and large fish were sheltering. Most notable were soft corals and possible deep-sea stony corals, slow-growing and sensitive species, on the frame itself. Together the structures form an artificial reef reaching roughly 400 feet down (about 120 metres). As at Formosa, bottom-trawling is banned on the site, so the reef is left undisturbed.

This is a colder, deep-water North Atlantic site, unlike Formosa's shallow sandy seabed, and the turbines float rather than stand on the bottom. Together, the two findings suggest the reef effect is not limited to one kind of turbine. Each still rests on a single site, though, and needs several more years of follow-up to confirm.
