## How Much Wind Power Is Out There

According to the [Global Wind Energy Council](https://gwec.net/global-wind-report-2022/), total global wind power capacity reached 837 GW as of its 2022 report, helping the world avoid over 1.2 billion tonnes of CO2 annually, roughly equivalent to South America's annual carbon emissions. Wind has been the primary non-hydro renewable technology globally over the last decade, and capacity was projected to double by 2027.

The [13MW GE Haliade-X turbine](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/01/01/business/GE-wind-turbine.html), featured in the New York Times in January 2021, has a blade 107 metres long and is capable of powering approximately 12,000 homes on its own.

Wind turbines can be **onshore** (on land) or **offshore** (out at sea), and offshore turbines can now even be [floating](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2021/aug/29/floating-wind-turbines-ocean-renewable-power) rather than fixed to the seabed. As the sections below on Taiwan and Scotland show, offshore turbine foundations can also double up as habitat for marine life.

### How much energy do wind turbines actually produce?

Every wind turbine has a range of wind speeds, typically around 30 to 55 mph, in which it produces at its rated (maximum) capacity. At slower wind speeds, production falls off dramatically: if wind speed halves, power production drops by a factor of eight. As a result, wind turbines rarely generate near their rated capacity on average. Industry estimates project an annual output of 30-40% of capacity, but real-world experience shows annual outputs of 15-30% are more typical.

For example, a 2MW turbine with a 25% capacity factor would produce:

2 MW × 365 days × 24 hours × 25% = 4,380 MWh (4,380,000 kWh) in a year.

### 2021 in review, from the Global Wind Energy Council

- The global wind industry had its second-best year in 2021, with almost 94 GW of capacity added globally, trailing 2020's record growth by only 1.8%.
- Europe, Latin America, and Africa and the Middle East had record years for new onshore installations, but total onshore wind installations in 2021 were still 18% lower than the previous year, driven by a slowdown in the world's two largest onshore markets, China and the US.
- 21.1 GW of offshore wind capacity was commissioned in 2021, three times more than in 2020, making it the best year in offshore wind history and bringing offshore's share of global new installations to 22.5%.
- China accounted for 80% of offshore wind capacity added worldwide in 2021, bringing its cumulative offshore installations to 27.7 GW. It took Europe three decades to reach a similar cumulative total.
- Wind auction activity bounced back in 2021, with more than 88 GW of capacity awarded globally, 153% higher than in 2020.
- Against a backdrop of growing net-zero commitments and renewed urgency around energy security, the outlook for the industry looked positive: 557 GW of new capacity was expected over the following five years under current policies at the time, more than 110 GW annually through 2026.
- Even so, this growth needed to roughly quadruple by the end of the decade for the world to stay on course for a 1.5°C pathway and net zero by 2050.

## The Cost Wind Power Doesn't Always Account For: Balsa Wood and Deforestation

Wind power's growth has not been entirely without cost. Reporting from the [Rainforest Journalism Fund](https://rainforestjournalismfund.org/stories/how-wind-power-boom-driving-deforestation-amazon) has linked the boom in wind farm construction to deforestation and impacts on local communities in the Amazon, driven by demand for **balsa wood**, used in Europe, and increasingly in China, as a component in wind turbine blades.

Already-installed turbines with blades stretching to 80 metres can cover an area of approximately 21,000 square metres, roughly three football pitches, and more recent turbine designs with blades up to 100 metres long can consume around 150 cubic metres of balsa wood each, equivalent to several tonnes. A 75% increase in demand, most of it sourced from the Ecuadorian Amazon, has driven illegal logging, unsustainable deforestation, and pressure on local communities, with bribes offered to persuade communities to allow loggers in as prices rise.

The clearest solution is to lower demand for balsa and shift to alternative materials, something a number of manufacturers are already doing. Alternative materials were incorporated into blade production from at least 2014, and moved into full production after supply problems in 2020, including Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET), a [low-density foam made from recycled plastic bottles](https://www.lmwindpower.com/en/stories-and-press/stories/sustainability/from-a-plastic-bottle-to-a-wind-turbine-blade). Consultancy Wood Mackenzie forecast that the share of PET used in blades would rise from 20% in 2018 to more than 55% by 2023, while demand for balsa was expected to remain broadly stable.

## Recycling Wind Turbine Blades

The industry has started tackling what happens to turbine blades at the end of their life. RWE's Sofia offshore wind farm, then under construction as the UK's largest, agreed with Siemens Gamesa to deploy **recyclable blades** on 44 of its 100 turbines, aiming for 50% of Sofia's blades to be produced in the UK. It marked the largest order of recyclable blades in the world, and followed the successful installation of recyclable blades at RWE's Kaskasi wind farm off the German island of Heligoland.

Siemens Gamesa's recyclable blades use a new resin type with a chemical structure that can be efficiently separated from other materials at end of life, allowing them to be reused in new casting applications, such as automotive components or consumer goods like suitcases and flatscreen casings. Other technologies are also being developed to recycle conventional (non-recyclable-by-design) blades already in service.

One striking example of reuse: in a new high-tech neighbourhood in Lund, southern Sweden, turbine blades from a decommissioned wind farm have become a visible part of the façade of an eco-friendly multi-storey car park. Wind turbine blades are built to generate electricity and withstand tough weather for decades, using composite materials such as glass fibre, carbon fibre, epoxy resin, balsa wood, metals and various fillers pressed tightly together in layers, which makes them extremely durable but difficult to recycle by conventional means.

## Wind Energy Costs: China's Breakthrough

China's installed wind turbine cost hit a record low, dropping 45% year-on-year and reaching roughly one-fifth of the equivalent cost in the United States, according to reporting by the [South China Morning Post](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/science/article/3257036/historic-breakthrough-chinas-installed-wind-turbine-cost-drops-one-fifth-us-green-energy-race). The installation price fell to slightly more than 2 yuan (US$0.28) per watt, down from the previous year's lowest domestic price of 3.9 yuan per watt, and well below the roughly $1.50 per watt typical in the US. One large project in the Gobi desert is thought to be partly responsible for the drop. China did less onshore installation than the year before, but its overall wind capacity grew from 4 GW in 2007 to 329 GW in 2021, a 31% annual growth rate, according to researchers from Xi'an Jiaotong University and Harvard's China Project on Energy, Economy and Environment.

## 2026: Offshore Wind as an Accidental Reef — 86 New Fish Species at Formosa (Taiwan)

Peer-reviewed work published in **Frontiers in Marine Science in 2026** documents an unusually clear biodiversity co-benefit from offshore wind. Researchers surveyed the **Formosa Wind Farm** off Miaoli County in Taiwan — **69 turbines across roughly 90 km² of previously flat, sandy seabed** — with SCUBA teams in **2017, 2018 and 2025**. Their 2025 survey documented **86 species of reef-associated fish exclusively within 50 metres of the turbine foundations**. Not one of those 86 species had been recorded in any prior fishery record for that patch of ocean.

### How the reef assembled

- **Within months of construction**: barnacles, sponges and corals colonised the steel
- **Then**: shrimp, crabs, bristle worms, gastropods, mussels, whelks and starfish moved in
- **Later**: **groupers, snappers and lobsters** arrived to feed and spawn
- **By 2025**: species richness, diversity and trophic structure at the wind-farm foundations were **statistically indistinguishable from established, purpose-built artificial reefs** in the region

The foundations effectively provide **"cliff face" habitat**: a vertical steel column from seabed to surface, with roughened welds, bolted flanges and a ring of protective boulders at the base. That geometry attracts **surface, midwater and bottom-dwelling** species simultaneously — something purpose-built reefs rarely achieve across the whole water column.

### The attraction-vs-production caveat

Scientists still debate whether structures like these **produce new fish** or simply **concentrate existing fish** away from surrounding water. At Formosa the fact that the 86 species had never been recorded locally before construction points to something more than pure redistribution. A wider *Fish and Fisheries* review reaches a similar conclusion: hard vertical structures **locally increase abundance and diversity** of reef-associated species, while the soft-bottom community beyond the foundations stays largely unchanged.

### Why this matters for the wider debate

- Sited on featureless soft-bottom seabed, **offshore wind can deliver climate mitigation and biodiversity uplift at the same time** — with no separate marine reserve programme required
- The result **does not automatically generalise** to floating offshore wind, near-shore installations, or seabeds that already host valuable habitat
- Parallel finding on land: [solar farms on rewetted peatland](../../biodiversity-land/solar-on-peatland.md) attracted more diverse bird species than nearby hay fields, but only when peat was rewetted first

## 2026: Floating Wind Gets the Same Result — Scotland's Hywind Pilot Park

A second, separate case now extends the reef effect to **floating** offshore wind, the technology that lets turbines move into deeper water where fixed pylons cannot reach. A remote-controlled submersible robot inspected 41 underwater components across the five floating turbines of **Equinor's Hywind Scotland Pilot Park**, 12 miles off the Scottish coast, anchored by mooring cables and suction anchors rather than seabed pylons.

Biological researchers logged **121 distinct forms of sea life** on the mooring lines, steel frames and anchors: dense clusters of orange starfish, sea urchins and plumose sea anemones near the surface; squat lobsters, brown crabs and large fish sheltering deeper down; and, most notably, **soft corals and possible deep-sea stony corals** — slow-growing, sensitive species — on the frame itself. Together the structures form an artificial reef reaching roughly **400 feet down**, and, as with Formosa, the site's exclusion of bottom-trawling leaves the reef undisturbed.

This is a colder, North Atlantic, deep-water case rather than Formosa's shallow sandy seabed, and it is floating rather than fixed-bottom — so the two findings together suggest the reef effect is not limited to one turbine technology, though each still rests on a single site and needs multi-year follow-up to confirm.
