## What Is Geothermal Energy

Geothermal energy is heat stored beneath the surface of the Earth. It comes from deep inside the planet, and temperatures increase with depth.

The Earth is made up of three main layers: the crust, the mantle and the core. Heat constantly flows from the core towards the surface. Temperatures are hottest at the Earth's centre, around 5,000°C, and decrease towards the surface, in what's called the geothermal gradient. Most of this heat comes from the natural decay of radioactive elements in different rock types, with some left over as "primordial heat" from when the planet formed about 4.5 billion years ago.

Close to the surface, in the top few tens to hundreds of metres, most of the heat stored in the ground actually comes from the sun rather than from the Earth's core.

### Where is it stored?

A geothermal reservoir typically consists of a heat source providing energy to a water-bearing rock layer, the reservoir, which can be tapped to access the geothermal resource. An insulating layer above traps the heat within the reservoir.

### What can geothermal energy be used for?

Geothermal energy can be used across a wide range of applications, including space heating and cooling, industry, agriculture, and electricity generation. Temperatures as low as 10°C are usable for heating, while temperatures of 150°C or more are typically needed for electricity generation.

In volcanic areas such as Iceland, geothermal energy can be a major energy source, but usable geothermal resources also exist far from tectonic plate boundaries, in areas with no volcanic activity. Geothermal energy is already used for heating and cooling in countries including France, Denmark, Belgium, the Netherlands and Ireland.

Geothermal resources are generally divided into two categories, shallow and deep, with different extraction methods and uses for each.

**Why choose geothermal energy?** It is a secure, environmentally sustainable and cost-effective source of renewable energy. Roughly a third of energy demand goes on heating, the vast majority of it still powered by fossil fuels. Because geothermal is a renewable, low-to-zero carbon emission resource, tapping into national geothermal potential could replace a meaningful share of that fossil-fuelled heat with something secure and sustainable.

### Shallow geothermal energy

Heat is harnessed from the shallow subsurface, usually from depths of 0-200 metres and occasionally up to 500 metres. There are two main types of collector system, or loop, used to extract heat from the ground, or release heat into it for cooling:

- **Closed loop** systems use a sealed pipe buried in the ground, filled with a thermal transfer fluid. As the fluid circulates, it absorbs heat, which is then passed through a heat exchanger.
- **Open loop** collectors obtain energy directly from groundwater, pumping it through a heat exchanger.

### Deep geothermal energy

Deep geothermal energy involves drilling much further below the surface than shallow systems, to harness higher temperatures found deeper underground. The temperatures reached at these depths can be used to generate electricity, or, more commonly, to supply district heating networks and buildings.

For deeper systems, technologies such as EGS and AGS are used:

- **EGS (Enhanced Geothermal Systems)** improve the permeability of rock to create an artificial reservoir in settings where subsurface temperatures are high but natural permeability is low.
- **AGS (Advanced Geothermal Systems)** are deep, large closed-loop circuits, with one or more wells drilled into hot rock and fluid circulated through the loop to bring heat to the surface.

With higher temperatures, there is also potential for geothermal electricity production. The most common approach uses steam power plants: fluids at very high temperatures are pumped up from deep underground to produce steam, which drives a turbine that generates electricity.

## Geothermal Power's Small but Growing Slice of the Energy Mix

Geothermal power's share of the clean energy mix is still small. The United States leads the world in geothermal electricity generation, but even so it makes up only about 0.4% of total US utility-scale electricity generation, according to the US Energy Information Administration. Iceland, straddling two diverging tectonic plates, is a geological outlier and produces about a quarter of its electricity this way.

By contrast, solar power is on track to overtake coal globally in installed capacity by 2027, according to the International Energy Agency, with global wind capacity also expected to almost double over the same period. Even so, geothermal has its champions. The US Department of Energy has said that cutting-edge geothermal technology "holds the potential to power more than 65 million American homes and businesses, and is the next frontier for renewable energy deployment."

The technology the DOE is most excited about is **Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS)**, which differ from naturally occurring geothermal systems that rely on a happy confluence of underground water, permeable rock and heat from the Earth's core. As the DOE puts it: "In many areas, however, the underground rock is hot but there is not enough natural permeability or fluids present. In those cases, an enhanced geothermal system can be used to create a human-made reservoir to tap that heat for energy."

The idea borrows drilling techniques pioneered by the oil and gas industry to help displace it: by carefully fracturing rock a few miles down and injecting water, engineers can create what journalist Gregory Barber, writing in *Wired*, called "an artificial hot spring that can drive steam turbines." This is appealing because geothermal energy is **dispatchable**, meaning it's available whenever needed, unlike wind and solar, which are variable and require storage to keep the lights on when the wind isn't blowing or the sun isn't shining.

Proving EGS can work at scale is a genuine challenge, given the cost and difficulty of operating machinery miles underground at high temperatures. The US Department of Energy has spent hundreds of millions of dollars in recent years on FORGE, the Frontier Observatory for Research in Geothermal Energy, a Utah-based effort to de-risk EGS, which reported early success proving a key part of the process. In Nevada, the geothermal startup Fervo Energy touted its own win with a successful full-scale well test. "There's a virtually unlimited resource down there if we can get at it," Fervo CEO Tim Latimer told the New York Times. "Everyone who looks into it gets obsessed with it."

## Geothermal Energy in Ireland

Ireland has excellent shallow geothermal energy reserves across the country. Shallow groundwater provides a stable resource of thermal energy that can supply heating at very high efficiencies, and Ground Source Heat Pumps (GSHPs) are becoming more popular; with sufficient insulation, they can be a very efficient way to heat homes and businesses (see the Geological Survey Ireland [homeowners manual](https://www.gsi.ie/en-ie/publications/Pages/Ground-Source-Heat-and-Shallow-Geothermal-Energy-Homeowner-Manual.aspx), which also includes a [video overview](https://youtu.be/mCRDf7QxjDk)).

Ireland also has recognised potential for deep, low-to-medium temperature geothermal resources, which may be suitable for large-scale or district heating and cooling in municipal, residential and industrial areas. The Geological Survey Ireland (GSI) published [An Assessment of Geothermal Energy for District Heating in Ireland](https://www.gsi.ie/en-ie/publications/Pages/Geothermal-Energy-for-District-Heating-in-Ireland.aspx), which maps this potential.

In 2020, the Department of the Environment, Climate and Communications published "A roadmap for a policy and regulatory framework," a key step in developing geothermal energy in Ireland. In 2023, the then Minister for the Environment, Climate and Communications, Eamon Ryan TD, published the [Policy Statement on Geothermal Energy for a Circular Economy](https://www.gsi.ie/en-ie/events-and-news/news/Pages/GeothermalPolicy.aspx).

### Trinity College Dublin's geothermal refurbishment

Trinity College Dublin's Rubrics building refurbishment included the installation of a 188 kW Ground Source Heat Pump system to provide heating and domestic hot water, supplied by a network of 21 sealed, closed-loop geothermal probes installed in New Square.

The works, undertaken by GeoServ, included drilling the boreholes, installing the geothermal collector, and connecting it via pre-insulated pipe to a central chamber and an underground plant room, alongside supplying the heat pumps and buffers. The geothermal collector provides energy to the heat pumps in the underground plant room, offering a low-visual-impact renewable solution that helps preserve and upgrade the historic building. The work was carried out for main contractor Clancy, in collaboration with Walls Well Drilling, Glenergy and Geolorn.

## July 2026: Three New Routes to Geothermal, Anywhere on Earth

Geothermal has always been "spectacular but geographically lucky," concentrated in places like Iceland, New Zealand, Kenya and Indonesia. A decade of drilling advances is now on track to untether it from geology entirely. The **International Energy Agency** estimates the technical potential of geothermal, if heat down to about 8km could be exploited, at **around 150 times current global electricity demand**. Traditional plants are limited to less than 2km deep, which leaves most of that potential out of reach.

New Scientist's July 2026 explainer by Katharine Sanderson sets out three new techniques engineers are pursuing to close that gap, in ascending order of ambition and difficulty.

### Route 1: Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS)

EGS targets **hot but impermeable rock at 2-10km depth**. Engineers pump water at high pressure into deep wells to open or widen fractures, creating an artificial reservoir. It is the **most mature of the new techniques**, but it comes with a real risk.

**Pohang, South Korea, November 2017:** a nearby EGS project **triggered a magnitude-5.5 earthquake**, injuring over **80 people** and causing around **300 billion Korean won (£148m) in damage**, the most destructive earthquake in South Korean history. A South Korean government investigation concluded EGS caused it. The mechanism: pumping water into deep rock raises pore pressure, propping fault sides apart and reducing the friction holding them together. If a fault is already close to slipping, that can be enough to trigger it.

**Iceland's mitigation approach:** rather than blasting reservoirs open all at once, engineers at HS Orka pump cold water slowly and at low pressure over months, so the cooling rock contracts and cracks naturally. Iceland also uses its geothermal wells themselves as seismic sensors: when magma begins to move, it compresses the surrounding rock, creating a telltale signal in the well.

### Route 2: Advanced Geothermal Systems (AGS / closed-loop)

AGS avoids the seismic risk altogether by not asking the rock to carry water at all. Engineers drill down, then sideways, until two boreholes connect underground, then line and seal them with steel and cement. The result is essentially a buried radiator: fluid circulates but never touches the rock directly.

- **Eavor's Geretsried plant in Germany** (built by the Canadian company Eavor) began construction in 2022 and started producing electricity in 2025, the first commercial-scale AGS plant.
- **China Huaneng Group** has built the first demonstration plant using supercritical CO2 as the working fluid, in Zhengzhou, operational since May 2026.

The open question is whether closed-loop systems can circulate fluid and draw heat fast enough to be commercially useful. Eavor has already had to scale back its planned wells after rock debris clogged parts of the pipe network.

### Route 3: Superhot / supercritical geothermal

The deeper you go, the hotter the Earth gets. At about **374°C** under sufficient pressure, water crosses a threshold and becomes a **supercritical fluid**, neither quite gas nor quite liquid, that can carry far more heat per unit than ordinary hot water or steam. Supercritical conditions are usually found at depths of at least 5-20km.

- **The Iceland Deep Drilling Project's second well** (completed 2017) went to 4.7km and became the first well in the world where supercritical conditions were confirmed in a geothermal well. A third well is planned for the following year.
- **Quaise Energy** (Houston, Texas) plans to skip conventional drill bits altogether: high-powered millimetre-wave energy would be piped down a corrugated-steel-lined well and used to vaporise the deep hard rock into ash, purged with nitrogen. The company's first planned superhot geothermal plant is targeted for Oregon by 2030 (400°C at 3-5km), with the goal of a first superhot well producing energy at a depth greater than 10km by 2035.
- **GA Drilling** (Slovakia) is trialling laser- and plasma-based systems, with first field tests carried out in 2023.

Uncertainty remains over whether such deep wells can be kept open under the immense pressures found kilometres below ground. At supercritical temperatures, standard steel casings can expand, deform and fail, so Iceland is testing flexible connectors between casing sections.

### Why it matters for the wider transition

Geothermal is dispatchable, available on demand day or night whatever the weather, which makes it a natural complement to variable wind and solar. If any of these three routes reaches commercial scale outside the geologically lucky countries, geothermal could shift from a specialist niche into a global backbone technology.

- **Iain Staffell** (Imperial College London): geothermal is "really well placed to be the glue that will make low-carbon electricity systems work."
- **Sanjeev Kumar** (European Geothermal Energy Council): "It's very, very costly. But once you've drilled that hole in the ground, that lasts for over 100 years."
- **Bill Ellsworth** (Stanford), summing up the state of play: "We know that very deep geothermal energy would be fabulous if we could reach it. We just don't yet know if it's possible."
