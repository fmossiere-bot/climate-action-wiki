## What Is Geothermal Energy

Geothermal energy is heat stored beneath the Earth's surface. It comes from deep inside the planet, and the deeper you go, the hotter it gets.

The Earth has three main layers: the crust, the mantle and the core. Heat flows constantly from the core towards the surface. The centre of the Earth is the hottest part, at around 5,000°C, and temperatures fall towards the surface. This change with depth is called the **geothermal gradient**. Most of the heat comes from the natural decay of radioactive elements in rocks. Some is "primordial heat" left over from when the planet formed about 4.5 billion years ago.

Close to the surface, in the top few tens to hundreds of metres, most of the heat in the ground actually comes from the sun, not from the Earth's core.

### Where is it stored?

A geothermal reservoir usually has a heat source warming a layer of rock that holds water. That layer, the reservoir, can be tapped for its heat. An insulating layer above it keeps the heat in.

### What can geothermal energy be used for?

Geothermal energy has many uses, including heating and cooling buildings, industry, farming and generating electricity. Temperatures as low as 10°C can be used for heating, while electricity generation usually needs 150°C or more.

In volcanic places such as Iceland, geothermal can be a major energy source. But usable geothermal heat also exists far from the edges of tectonic plates, in areas with no volcanoes. It is already used for heating and cooling in countries including France, Denmark, Belgium, the Netherlands and Ireland.

Geothermal resources are usually split into two groups, shallow and deep, each with its own extraction methods and uses.

**Why choose geothermal energy?** It is a secure, sustainable and cost-effective source of renewable energy. Roughly a third of energy demand goes on heating, and most of that heat still comes from fossil fuels. Geothermal is renewable and produces little or no carbon, so tapping a country's geothermal potential could replace a real share of that fossil-fuelled heat with something secure and sustainable.

### Shallow geothermal energy

Heat is taken from just below the surface, usually from depths of 0-200 metres and occasionally up to 500 metres. There are two main types of collector system, or loop, used to draw heat from the ground, or to put heat back into it for cooling:

- **Closed loop** systems use a sealed pipe buried in the ground, filled with a fluid that carries heat. As the fluid circulates, it picks up heat, which is then passed through a heat exchanger.
- **Open loop** collectors take energy directly from groundwater, pumping it through a heat exchanger.

### Deep geothermal energy

Deep geothermal means drilling much further down than shallow systems, to reach the higher temperatures found deeper underground. That heat can be used to generate electricity or, more often, to supply district heating networks and buildings.

Deeper systems use technologies such as EGS and AGS:

- **EGS (Enhanced Geothermal Systems)** make rock more permeable, so water can flow through it, creating an artificial reservoir where the rock is hot but has few natural cracks.
- **AGS (Advanced Geothermal Systems)** are deep, large closed loops. One or more wells are drilled into hot rock and fluid is circulated through the loop to bring heat to the surface.

Higher temperatures also open the way to geothermal electricity. The most common method uses steam power plants: very hot fluids are pumped up from deep underground to make steam, which drives a turbine that generates electricity.

## Geothermal Power's Small but Growing Slice of the Energy Mix

Geothermal still provides only a small share of clean energy. The United States generates more geothermal electricity than any other country, but even there it is only about **0.4%** of utility-scale electricity generation, according to the US Energy Information Administration. Iceland, which sits across two tectonic plates that are pulling apart, is a geological exception and produces about a quarter of its electricity this way.

By contrast, solar power is on track to overtake coal worldwide in installed capacity by 2027, according to the International Energy Agency, and global wind capacity is expected to almost double over the same period. Geothermal still has its supporters. The US Department of Energy has said that cutting-edge geothermal technology "holds the potential to power more than 65 million American homes and businesses, and is the next frontier for renewable energy deployment."

The technology the DOE is most excited about is **Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS)**. Natural geothermal systems rely on a lucky combination of underground water, permeable rock and heat from below. EGS does not. As the DOE puts it: "In many areas, however, the underground rock is hot but there is not enough natural permeability or fluids present. In those cases, an enhanced geothermal system can be used to create a human-made reservoir to tap that heat for energy."

The idea uses drilling techniques developed by the oil and gas industry to help replace it. By carefully fracturing rock a few miles down and injecting water, engineers can create what journalist Gregory Barber, writing in *Wired*, called "an artificial hot spring that can drive steam turbines." The appeal is that geothermal is **dispatchable**: it is available whenever it is needed. Wind and solar vary with the weather and need storage to keep the lights on when the wind drops or the sun goes down.

Proving EGS can work at scale is hard, because running machinery miles underground at high temperatures is costly and difficult. The US Department of Energy has spent hundreds of millions of dollars in recent years on FORGE, the Frontier Observatory for Research in Geothermal Energy. This Utah-based project aims to reduce the risks of EGS, and it reported early success in proving a key part of the process. In Nevada, the geothermal start-up Fervo Energy announced a successful full-scale well test. "There's a virtually unlimited resource down there if we can get at it," Fervo CEO Tim Latimer told the New York Times. "Everyone who looks into it gets obsessed with it."

## Geothermal Energy in Ireland
<!-- tag: ireland -->

Ireland has excellent shallow geothermal reserves across the country. Shallow groundwater is a steady source of heat that can warm buildings very efficiently. Ground Source Heat Pumps (GSHPs) are becoming more popular, and in a well-insulated building they can be a very efficient way to heat homes and businesses (see the Geological Survey Ireland [homeowners manual](https://www.gsi.ie/en-ie/publications/Pages/Ground-Source-Heat-and-Shallow-Geothermal-Energy-Homeowner-Manual.aspx), which also includes a [video overview](https://youtu.be/mCRDf7QxjDk)).

Ireland also has recognised potential for deep geothermal resources at low to medium temperatures. These may suit large-scale or district heating and cooling in towns, housing estates and industrial areas. The Geological Survey Ireland (GSI) has mapped this potential in [An Assessment of Geothermal Energy for District Heating in Ireland](https://www.gsi.ie/en-ie/publications/Pages/Geothermal-Energy-for-District-Heating-in-Ireland.aspx).

In 2020, the Department of the Environment, Climate and Communications published "A roadmap for a policy and regulatory framework," an important step for geothermal energy in Ireland. In 2023, the then Minister for the Environment, Climate and Communications, Eamon Ryan TD, published the [Policy Statement on Geothermal Energy for a Circular Economy](https://www.gsi.ie/en-ie/events-and-news/news/Pages/GeothermalPolicy.aspx).

### Trinity College Dublin's geothermal refurbishment

The refurbishment of Trinity College Dublin's Rubrics building included a **188 kW Ground Source Heat Pump** system for heating and hot water. It is fed by a network of 21 sealed, closed-loop geothermal probes installed under New Square.

The work was done by GeoServ. It included drilling the boreholes, installing the geothermal collector, and connecting it with pre-insulated pipe to a central chamber and an underground plant room, as well as supplying the heat pumps and buffers. The collector feeds the heat pumps in the underground plant room, a renewable solution that is barely visible and helps preserve and upgrade the historic building. The work was carried out for main contractor Clancy, with Walls Well Drilling, Glenergy and Geolorn.

## July 2026: Three New Routes to Geothermal, Anywhere on Earth

Geothermal has always been "spectacular but geographically lucky," found mainly in places like Iceland, New Zealand, Kenya and Indonesia. A decade of advances in drilling could now free it from those limits. The International Energy Agency estimates that if heat down to about 8km could be used, geothermal's technical potential would be **around 150 times current global electricity demand**. Traditional plants only reach less than 2km deep, which leaves most of that potential out of reach.

A July 2026 explainer in New Scientist by Katharine Sanderson describes three new techniques engineers are working on to close that gap. They are listed here from the least to the most ambitious and difficult.

### Route 1: Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS)

EGS targets hot rock with no natural cracks at depths of 2-10km. Engineers pump water at high pressure into deep wells to open or widen fractures, creating an artificial reservoir. It is the most developed of the new techniques, but it carries a real risk.

**Pohang, South Korea, November 2017:** a nearby EGS project triggered a magnitude-5.5 earthquake. It injured over 80 people and caused around 300 billion Korean won (£148m) of damage, making it the most destructive earthquake in South Korean history. A South Korean government investigation concluded that EGS caused it. Pumping water into deep rock raises the water pressure inside the rock, which pushes the two sides of a fault apart and reduces the friction holding them together. If a fault is already close to slipping, that can be enough to set it off.

**Iceland's safer approach:** instead of forcing reservoirs open all at once, engineers at HS Orka pump cold water slowly and at low pressure over months. The rock cools, contracts and cracks naturally. Iceland also uses its geothermal wells as earthquake sensors. When magma starts to move, it squeezes the surrounding rock, and that shows up as a tell-tale signal in the well.

### Route 2: Advanced Geothermal Systems (AGS / closed-loop)

AGS avoids the earthquake risk altogether because water never has to flow through the rock. Engineers drill down, then sideways, until two boreholes meet underground, then line and seal them with steel and cement. The result is a kind of buried radiator: fluid circulates but never touches the rock directly.

- **Eavor's Geretsried plant in Germany** (built by the Canadian company Eavor) began construction in 2022 and started producing electricity in 2025, the first commercial-scale AGS plant.
- **China Huaneng Group** has built the first demonstration plant using supercritical CO2 as the working fluid, in Zhengzhou, running since May 2026.

The open question is whether closed-loop systems can move fluid and draw heat fast enough to pay their way. Eavor has already had to scale back its planned wells after rock debris clogged parts of the pipe network.

### Route 3: Superhot / supercritical geothermal

The deeper you go, the hotter the Earth gets. At about **374°C** and under enough pressure, water becomes a supercritical fluid, neither quite gas nor quite liquid. It can carry far more heat than ordinary hot water or steam. These conditions are usually found at depths of at least 5-20km.

- **The Iceland Deep Drilling Project's second well** (completed 2017) went to 4.7km and was the first geothermal well in the world where supercritical conditions were confirmed. A third well is planned for the following year.
- **Quaise Energy** (Houston, Texas) plans to do without conventional drill bits. High-powered millimetre-wave energy would be sent down a well lined with corrugated steel to vaporise the deep hard rock into ash, which is then flushed out with nitrogen. The company's first planned superhot geothermal plant is targeted for Oregon by 2030 (400°C at 3-5km). Its goal is a first superhot well producing energy from deeper than 10km by 2035.
- **GA Drilling** (Slovakia) is trialling laser- and plasma-based systems, with first field tests carried out in 2023.

It is still unclear whether wells this deep can be kept open under the huge pressures found kilometres underground. At supercritical temperatures, standard steel casings can expand, bend out of shape and fail, so Iceland is testing flexible connectors between casing sections.

### Why it matters for the wider transition

Geothermal is dispatchable: it is available on demand, day or night, whatever the weather. That makes it a natural partner for wind and solar, which come and go. If any of these three routes reaches commercial scale outside the geologically lucky countries, geothermal could grow from a specialist niche into a mainstay of the world's energy supply.

Iain Staffell of Imperial College London sees geothermal as "really well placed to be the glue that will make low-carbon electricity systems work." Sanjeev Kumar of the European Geothermal Energy Council points to the trade-off: "It's very, very costly. But once you've drilled that hole in the ground, that lasts for over 100 years." Bill Ellsworth of Stanford sums up where things stand: "We know that very deep geothermal energy would be fabulous if we could reach it. We just don't yet know if it's possible."
