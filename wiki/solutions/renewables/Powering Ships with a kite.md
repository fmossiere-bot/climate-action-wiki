# Powering Ships with a Kite

Using the wind to help power ships is becoming a popular way to cut shipping emissions. One method uses huge kites that tow cargo ships along, cutting fuel use by up to **40%**.

The [worldwide shipping industry is responsible for about 3% of the greenhouse gases](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/10/03/climate/wind-powered-ships-climate.html) heating the planet. That is roughly one billion metric tons of CO2 and other gases every year, and the figure keeps rising as global trade grows. Around 11 billion tons of cargo travel by sea each year, as much as 90% of the world's traded goods. At that scale, even small fuel savings from wind add up.

## Companies developing kite and sail propulsion

**Airseas**, a French company based in Bordeaux, has designed a kite system that could cut a ship's fuel consumption by 40% (see [airseas.com](https://airseas.com/en/)).

**Pyxis Ocean** takes a different approach, using a sail design rather than a kite (see its [vessel details on MarineTraffic](https://www.marinetraffic.com/en/ais/details/ships/shipid:5045111/mmsi:563021600/imo:9798856/vessel:PYXIS_OCEAN)).

**Seakite**, also a French company, operates as [Beyond the Sea](https://beyond-the-sea.com/en/), a project partly funded by [Team for the Planet](https://team-planet.com/en/innovations/125-beyond-the-sea).

These three are far from alone. [Dozens of other wind-assisted ship designs](https://www.wind-ship.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/IWSA-Newsletter-July-2023-General-Release.pdf) are in development, many from companies in Britain, France, Norway and the Netherlands. Almost all are highly automated and fitted with sensors. The designs range from sails and rotors to structures that look like aeroplane wings standing on end. Wind power has become one of the more popular ways to cut shipping emissions.
