# Powering Ships with a Kite

Wind-assisted shipping is becoming an increasingly popular solution to cutting maritime emissions, particularly through the use of huge kites that tow cargo ships, cutting fuel consumption by up to 40%.

The [worldwide shipping industry is responsible for about 3% of the greenhouse gases](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/10/03/climate/wind-powered-ships-climate.html) dangerously heating the planet, roughly one billion metric tons of CO2 and other gases every year, a figure that keeps rising as global trade grows. Around 11 billion tons of cargo are shipped by sea each year, accounting for as much as 90% of the world's traded goods, which is why even modest efficiency gains from wind assistance matter at scale.

## Companies developing kite and sail propulsion

**Airseas**, a French company based in Bordeaux, has designed a kite system that could cut a ship's fuel consumption by 40% (see [airseas.com](https://airseas.com/en/)).

**Pyxis Ocean** takes a different approach, using a distinct sail design rather than a kite (see its [vessel details on MarineTraffic](https://www.marinetraffic.com/en/ais/details/ships/shipid:5045111/mmsi:563021600/imo:9798856/vessel:PYXIS_OCEAN)).

**Seakite**, also a French company, operates as [Beyond the Sea](https://beyond-the-sea.com/en/), a project partly funded by [Team for the Planet](https://team-planet.com/en/innovations/125-beyond-the-sea).

These three are far from alone: [dozens of other wind-assisted ship designs](https://www.wind-ship.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/IWSA-Newsletter-July-2023-General-Release.pdf) are in development, many from companies in Britain, France, Norway and the Netherlands. Almost all are highly automated and fitted with sensors, with designs ranging from sails and rotors to structures that resemble vertical aeroplane wings. It has become one of the more popular approaches to decarbonising shipping.
