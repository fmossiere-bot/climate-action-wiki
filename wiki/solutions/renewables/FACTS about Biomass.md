## What Is Biomass Energy

Biomass energy is energy from organic matter, both plant material and animal waste. Plant-based biomass is often considered a "sustainable" source of energy because plants absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere as they grow, and release roughly the same amount when burned, making it theoretically carbon neutral. About 10% of global primary energy currently comes from biomass, according to [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/energy-mix).

Many rural communities across the developing world rely on wood, charcoal, animal dung and other biomass for household cooking and heating, and for small-scale rural industrial processes such as brick-making and food processing.

## Modern Bioenergy

Modern bioenergy sources include:

- Solid fuels, such as torrefied wood pellets or briquettes
- Biofuels, which break down into several generations:
  - **First-generation** fuels made from food crops (ethanol and biodiesel)
  - **Second-generation** fuels made from lignocellulosic biomass (non-food plant material)
  - **Third-generation** fuels made from algae
  - **Fourth-generation** fuels, such as electrofuels and photobiological solar fuels, still largely experimental

Heating homes with biomass, typically wood pellets and a stove, is one widely available sustainable heating option (see the [Energy Saving Trust's guide](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/advice/biomass/)), though as the sections below show, it is worth understanding how sustainably the biomass itself is sourced.

Modern bioenergy is also a key part of the aviation sector's bet on sustainable fuels; see [Sustainable Aviation Fuel: A Necessary but Complicated Solution](../electrification/Sustainable Aviation Fuel -  A Necessary but Complicated Solution.md) for more detail. This is not without controversy, and biomass shares some of the same land-use and emissions-accounting problems.

## Risks and Controversies

A five-year study of corn ethanol, a widely used fuel additive in the US, published in 2022, found it had led to increased fertiliser use and water pollution, and likely produced [at least 24% more emissions than petrol](https://civileats.com/2022/02/14/how-corn-ethanol-for-biofuel-fueled-climate-change/).

The [World Resources Institute](http://www.wri.org/research/avoiding-bioenergy-competition-food-crops-and-land) has warned that expanding biofuel cultivation takes land away from food production, and that ambitious biofuel targets could significantly widen the "food gap" between the crop calories available and those needed by 2050.

The world's largest maker of wood pellets burned for energy has also come under scrutiny: a December 2022 [Mongabay investigation](https://news.mongabay.com/2022/12/envivas-biomass-lies-whistleblower-account/) questioned the company's environmental claims, noting this matters particularly given the EU's ongoing debate over whether forest biomass burning should count as a "sustainable" form of renewable energy. A related [study commissioned by the US Southern Environmental Law Center](https://www.southernenvironment.org/news/new-study-confirms-harmful-impacts-of-biomass/) documented the environmental and climate harms the biomass energy industry inflicts on forests in the southern United States.

A related concern is **indirect land use change**: biocrop cultivation can displace other cultivation, such as food crops, onto other land, in the same country or overseas, which can encroach into former rainforest or other natural habitats and produce a net increase in greenhouse gas emissions. This has been studied in [Brazil](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0907318107), though the overall scale of the effect is [complex and difficult to assess](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/B9780128039540000073).

**BECCS** (bioenergy with carbon capture and storage), which features in IPCC scenarios for limiting warming to 1.5°C, has also been shown to be difficult to reconcile with sustainable land use at the scale envisioned in some of those scenarios.

### The Drax controversy in the UK

Subsidies can steer industry towards a particular type of energy, and that is broadly what happened with Drax's North Yorkshire power station, which burns wood pellets imported from North America to generate electricity. According to a 2024 report from the climate think tank Ember, reported by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/article/2024/aug/09/biomass-power-station-produced-four-times-emissions-of-uk-coal-plant-says-report), the plant produced roughly four times the emissions of a traditional coal plant and was revealed as Britain's single largest carbon emitter in 2023.

Drax, which has received billions in subsidies since it began switching from coal to biomass in 2012, was responsible for 11.5 million tonnes of CO2 in 2023, close to 3% of the UK's total carbon emissions. Some scientists estimate that burning wood pellets generates more emissions per unit of electricity produced than burning gas or oil. The company [plans to fit carbon capture technology](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2024/jan/16/drax-gets-go-ahead-for-carbon-capture-project-at-estimated-40bn-cost-to-bill-payers) at the plant, using further subsidies, aiming to become the first "carbon-negative" power plant in the world by the end of the decade.

A Drax spokesperson dismissed Ember's findings as "flawed," arguing the report ignored the company's "widely accepted and internationally recognised approach to carbon accounting," and pointed out that the IPCC is clear that biomass sourced in line with strict sustainability criteria can count as a low-carbon energy source. Critics, including the report's authors, counter that burning wood for power is an expensive risk that limits UK energy independence, and argue that true energy security comes from wind, solar, a healthy grid and flexible planning. The underlying tension is real: if genuinely sustainably sourced, burning wood can be a low-carbon energy source, but it is not a straightforwardly sustainable one at Drax's scale in the long run, since it requires planting far more trees than are currently being replaced, and high subsidies risk locking in the wrong long-term strategy.

## Small-Scale Biomass

While large-scale biofuel use has fallen out of favour in many quarters, biomass energy based on crop residues and animal waste can still be used sustainably at a small scale, and can be an excellent solution for rural energy needs. Many rice-growing regions produce large volumes of rice husk, which can fuel small gasifiers to generate power; see this [story on rice-husk-based electrification](https://daily.jstor.org/running-on-rice-husks-how-one-entrepreneur-brought-electricity-to-his-village/) from one of India's poorest states.

In France, one farmer in the Dordogne region runs all of his vehicles on biomethane fermented from cow manure and other animal waste, turning what would otherwise be a source of methane emissions into usable fuel. Other farmers store effluent to feed their own fuel reservoirs directly (source: Ecologie360).

**Biogas** is a feasible option for some rural communities too, though it has constraints: it needs a sufficient, year-round supply of crop residues and animal waste to work effectively. Even so, there are places where it has proven successful, such as in the Indian state of Chhattisgarh, documented in a [case study of biogas plants](https://www.cwejournal.org/vol11no2/biogas-plants-in-chhattisgarh-india-a-case-study/) there.

## Sustainable Feedstock: A Solution for the Chemical Industry?

The chemical industry, which is heavily present in Europe, is under growing pressure to decarbonise. As of early 2023, 66% of the largest chemical end users in Europe, including companies in the automotive, food and personal care industries, had committed to reducing greenhouse gas emissions by 2030, and 37% had pledged net-zero targets by 2050, according to [McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/chemicals/our-insights/sustainable-feedstocks-accelerating-recarbonization-in-chemicals). In Germany, Europe's largest economy, the industry aims to cut CO2 emissions by approximately 35% by 2030.

Manufacturing chemicals is energy intensive, and some critical processes produce large amounts of CO2 as a by-product; because many chemicals are carbon-based, products can also release CO2 or methane if incinerated or decomposed at the end of their life. One proposed solution for this hard-to-decarbonise sector is the use of sustainable feedstocks, such as biomass and captured CO2, in place of fossil-derived inputs. This approach is not without challenges, particularly around matching the right feedstocks and conversion technologies to the right products in the right regions of the world.

## About Biomethanol

Biomethanol is a biomass-derived alternative to conventional methanol, of growing interest as a marine and industrial fuel. IRENA's technology brief on [biomethanol production](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2013/IRENA-ETSAP-Tech-Brief-I08-Production_of_Bio-methanol.pdf) and ETIP Bioenergy's overview of [biomethanol production and use as a fuel](https://www.etipbioenergy.eu/images/ETIP_Bioenergy_Biomethanol_production_and_use_as_fuel.pdf) both cover the production routes and end uses in more detail.
