## What Is Biomass Energy

Biomass energy is energy from organic matter, both plant material and animal waste. Plant-based biomass is often described as "sustainable" because plants absorb carbon dioxide as they grow and release roughly the same amount when burned. In theory, that makes it carbon neutral. About 10% of global primary energy currently comes from biomass, according to [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/energy-mix).

Many rural communities in lower-income countries rely on wood, charcoal, animal dung and other biomass for cooking and heating at home. They also use it for small local industries such as brick-making and food processing.

## Modern Bioenergy

Modern bioenergy sources include:

- Solid fuels, such as torrefied wood pellets or briquettes
- Biofuels, which break down into several generations:
  - **First-generation** fuels made from food crops (ethanol and biodiesel)
  - **Second-generation** fuels made from lignocellulosic biomass (non-food plant material)
  - **Third-generation** fuels made from algae
  - **Fourth-generation** fuels, such as electrofuels and photobiological solar fuels, still largely experimental

Heating a home with biomass, usually wood pellets and a stove, is one widely available low-carbon heating option (see the [Energy Saving Trust's guide](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/advice/biomass/)). As the sections below show, it is worth knowing where the biomass itself comes from.

Bioenergy is also a large part of the aviation sector's plan for cleaner fuels. See [Sustainable Aviation Fuel: A Necessary but Complicated Solution](../electrification/Sustainable Aviation Fuel -  A Necessary but Complicated Solution.md) for more. That plan is contested too, and it runs into the same problems with land use and emissions accounting as other biomass.

## Risks and Controversies

Corn ethanol is a widely used fuel additive in the US. A five-year study published in 2022 found it had led to more fertiliser use and more water pollution, and likely produced [at least 24% more emissions than petrol](https://civileats.com/2022/02/14/how-corn-ethanol-for-biofuel-fueled-climate-change/).

The [World Resources Institute](http://www.wri.org/research/avoiding-bioenergy-competition-food-crops-and-land) has warned that growing more biofuel crops takes land away from food. Ambitious biofuel targets could widen the "food gap", the difference between the crop calories available and those the world will need by 2050.

The world's largest maker of wood pellets for energy has also come under scrutiny. A December 2022 [Mongabay investigation](https://news.mongabay.com/2022/12/envivas-biomass-lies-whistleblower-account/) questioned the company's environmental claims. This matters because the EU is still debating whether burning forest biomass should count as a "sustainable" form of renewable energy. A [study commissioned by the US Southern Environmental Law Center](https://www.southernenvironment.org/news/new-study-confirms-harmful-impacts-of-biomass/) documented the harm the biomass industry does to forests and the climate in the southern United States.

A related concern is **indirect land use change**. When farmland is switched to biofuel crops, the food crops it used to grow move elsewhere, in the same country or overseas. That can push farming into former rainforest or other natural habitats, and the overall result can be more greenhouse gas emissions, not fewer. This has been studied in [Brazil](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0907318107), though the overall scale of the effect is [complex and difficult to assess](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/B9780128039540000073).

**BECCS** (bioenergy with carbon capture and storage) appears in IPCC scenarios for limiting warming to 1.5°C. At the scale some of those scenarios assume, it has proved hard to square with sustainable land use.

### The Drax controversy in the UK

Subsidies can steer industry towards one type of energy. That is broadly what happened at Drax's power station in North Yorkshire, which burns wood pellets imported from North America to make electricity. A 2024 report from the climate think tank Ember, covered by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/article/2024/aug/09/biomass-power-station-produced-four-times-emissions-of-uk-coal-plant-says-report), found the plant produced roughly four times the emissions of a traditional coal plant. It was Britain's single largest carbon emitter in 2023.

Drax has received billions in subsidies since it began switching from coal to biomass in 2012. In 2023 it was responsible for **11.5 million tonnes of CO2**, close to 3% of the UK's total carbon emissions. Some scientists estimate that burning wood pellets produces more emissions per unit of electricity than burning gas or oil. The company [plans to fit carbon capture technology](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2024/jan/16/drax-gets-go-ahead-for-carbon-capture-project-at-estimated-40bn-cost-to-bill-payers) with the help of further subsidies, aiming to become the world's first "carbon-negative" power plant by the end of the decade.

A Drax spokesperson called Ember's findings "flawed," saying the report ignored the company's "widely accepted and internationally recognised approach to carbon accounting." The company also pointed out that the IPCC accepts biomass sourced under strict sustainability criteria as a low-carbon energy source. Critics, including the report's authors, reply that burning wood for power is an expensive risk that limits UK energy independence. In their view, real energy security comes from wind, solar, a healthy grid and flexible planning.

Both sides have a point. Wood from well-managed sources can be a low-carbon fuel. At Drax's scale, though, it is hard to sustain over the long run, because it needs far more trees planted than are currently being replaced, and high subsidies risk locking in the wrong long-term strategy.

## Small-Scale Biomass

Large-scale biofuels have fallen out of favour in many places. Biomass from crop residues and animal waste can still work well at a small scale, and it can meet rural energy needs very well. Rice-growing regions produce large volumes of rice husk, which can fuel small gasifiers to generate power. See this [story on rice-husk-based electrification](https://daily.jstor.org/running-on-rice-husks-how-one-entrepreneur-brought-electricity-to-his-village/) from one of India's poorest states.

In the Dordogne region of France, one farmer runs all of his vehicles on biomethane made by fermenting cow manure and other animal waste. Something that would otherwise give off methane becomes usable fuel. Other farmers store effluent to feed their own fuel tanks directly (source: Ecologie360).

**Biogas** can work for some rural communities too, but it needs a steady, year-round supply of crop residues and animal waste. Where that supply exists it has worked well, for example in the Indian state of Chhattisgarh, documented in a [case study of biogas plants](https://www.cwejournal.org/vol11no2/biogas-plants-in-chhattisgarh-india-a-case-study/) there.

## Sustainable Feedstock: A Solution for the Chemical Industry?

The chemical industry is a big presence in Europe, and it is under growing pressure to cut emissions. By early 2023, 66% of the largest chemical buyers in Europe, including car makers and food and personal care companies, had committed to reducing greenhouse gas emissions by 2030. 37% had pledged to reach net zero by 2050, according to [McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/chemicals/our-insights/sustainable-feedstocks-accelerating-recarbonization-in-chemicals). In Germany, Europe's largest economy, the industry aims to cut CO2 emissions by approximately 35% by 2030.

Making chemicals uses a lot of energy, and some essential processes give off large amounts of CO2 as a by-product. Many chemicals are carbon-based, so products can also release CO2 or methane when they are burned or break down at the end of their life. One proposed fix for this hard-to-clean-up sector is to use sustainable feedstocks (raw materials), such as biomass and captured CO2, instead of fossil-based ones. The difficulty lies in matching the right feedstocks and conversion technologies to the right products in the right parts of the world.

## About Biomethanol

Biomethanol is methanol made from biomass rather than fossil fuels. It is drawing growing interest as a fuel for ships and industry. IRENA's technology brief on [biomethanol production](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2013/IRENA-ETSAP-Tech-Brief-I08-Production_of_Bio-methanol.pdf) and ETIP Bioenergy's overview of [biomethanol production and use as a fuel](https://www.etipbioenergy.eu/images/ETIP_Bioenergy_Biomethanol_production_and_use_as_fuel.pdf) both cover the production routes and end uses in more detail.
