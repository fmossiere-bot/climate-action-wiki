# France's Biomethane Boom: Farm Waste Becomes a National Energy Source

> **Editorial summary:** Original research by Fabien Mossière drawing on Nathalie Mayer's Écologie 360 feature. France has built one of Europe's largest biomethane networks — 820+ farm and industrial digesters injecting purified biogas into the national gas grid, together the equivalent of three nuclear reactors of capacity. The Quaak brothers' Arcy farm in Seine-et-Marne was one of the first to switch from combined-heat-and-power to grid injection via GRDF. Biomethane emits about six times less CO2 than fossil gas (Ademe), covers 3.9% of French gas consumption today, and could plausibly reach ~20% at scale. Limits: feedstock is finite, projects depend on regulated purchase prices, and methane leaks remain a live concern. Big players — Shell (Nature Energy), TotalEnergies (BioBéarn), Engie, Waga Energy (landfill gas) — have entered, and start-up Sublime Énergie is testing an on-farm liquefaction / truck-collection model to reach digesters far from the grid.

## 1. Overview

France has built one of the largest biomethane production networks in Europe over the past fifteen years. What began as a side project on a handful of farms has grown into a national industry, with more than 820 injection sites now feeding purified biogas, known as biomethane, into the country's gas grid. The combined production capacity is roughly equivalent to three nuclear reactors. France now hosts close to half of all methanisation plants in Europe, making it a leader in this field.

## 2. How biomethane is produced

Biomethane starts as biogas, a mixture of methane, CO2 and other gases created when organic matter breaks down without oxygen. On farms, this organic matter includes manure, slurry, crop residues and local food waste. The material is placed in a sealed digester and heated to around 38°C, where bacteria break it down over time. This process, called methanisation, produces two things: biogas and a residue called digestate.

Before biogas can be injected into the gas network, it must be purified and given an odour, so it matches the properties of natural gas. Once treated, it becomes biomethane, pure enough to be used interchangeably with fossil gas.

## 3. A farm-level case study: the Quaak brothers

The Arcy farm in Chaumes-en-Brie, in the Seine-et-Marne region, illustrates how the sector developed. Thirteen years ago, the Quaak brothers were looking for a new source of income and considered methanisation. At the time, the standard model required using the heat produced during the process, something the brothers had no use for. The option to inject biomethane directly into the gas grid, managed by grid operator GRDF, changed their plans, and farmers in the region were among the first in France to adopt this approach.

Today, the farm supplies biomethane to several surrounding communes. Only a third of the material processed comes from outside the farm, which keeps outside traffic to about one truck a day. According to Mauritz Quaak, one of the farm's operators, the digester runs at over 99% availability, operating almost continuously throughout the year.

## 4. National growth and key figures

The shift from a niche practice to a national industry has been rapid. Some key figures for 2025:

> - Installed green gas production capacity in France reached 15.9 TWh, enough to supply close to 4 million homes
> - Biomethane represents 3.9% of French gas consumption
> - More than 3 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent were avoided, comparable to the emissions of 1.6 million passengers flying round trip between Paris and New York
> - More than one million households are now heated using biomethane
> - Over half of the roughly 40,000 gas-powered vehicles in France now run on bioGNV, a renewable fuel derived from biomethane

According to the French environment agency Ademe, biogas produces around six times less CO2 than fossil gas. The 2022 to 2023 energy crisis, marked by price volatility and reliance on gas imports, made local production more attractive from a strategic point of view. France's large agricultural sector and dense gas network are both seen as advantages that support this growth.

## 5. Strengths of the model

Beyond emissions savings, the sector offers a circular economy benefit. The digestate left over from methanisation is spread back on farmland as a natural fertiliser, largely replacing synthetic alternatives. Biomethane is also described as a "dispatchable" energy source, meaning it can be stored and used on demand, unlike some other renewables.

## 6. Limitations and challenges

Despite its growth, biomethane is expected to cover only a fraction of French gas demand, at most around 20%. This is because the amount of organic waste and agricultural residue available to use as feedstock is limited.

The sector also depends heavily on public support. Most projects rely on bank financing that is conditional on guaranteed purchase prices for the gas produced. Mauritz Quaak noted that regulatory uncertainty makes some investors hesitant, and that operators need long-term visibility to commit to projects.

Methane leaks remain a point of criticism, since methane is a powerful greenhouse gas. Producers argue that leaks represent a direct financial loss, giving them a strong incentive to prevent them. Other concerns raised locally include odours, increased truck traffic, and pressure on agricultural land, although land use for these projects is subject to strict rules.

## 7. Industrial scale-up

An industrial supply chain has developed around the agricultural biomethane sector. French company Naskeo, which designs methanisation units, now exports its expertise abroad. Industrial-scale plants are also being built in urban areas to process the 5.5 million tonnes of biodegradable waste produced by cities each year, much of which is still underused.

Landfill gas is increasingly captured as well. French company Waga Energy, which developed its technology at Air Liquide, is set to operate around fifty landfill gas recovery units across Europe and North America. Globally, the energy potential of landfill sites is estimated at close to three times France's annual gas consumption.

Large energy companies have entered the sector. In 2024, Shell, through its subsidiary Nature Energy, opened France's largest methanisation unit in Burgundy, with a capacity of 230 GWh of biomethane per year. This is larger than the 160 GWh BioBéarn plant operated by TotalEnergies in the Pyrénées-Atlantiques region. Engie has taken a different approach with its Montauban-de-Bretagne plant, which uses agricultural, industrial and municipal waste to produce both electricity and heat for nearby greenhouses, while distributing digestate across several thousand hectares of farmland.

France's multi-year energy plan targets 50 TWh of green gas production by 2030.

## 8. A new distribution model

Not all farms are close enough to the gas grid to inject their biomethane directly. French start-up Sublime Énergie is addressing this by liquefying biogas on-site and collecting it by truck, in a model compared to a traditional milk collection round. The company estimates this approach could unlock up to an additional 26 TWh of production in France. In 2026, it opened its first working demonstration site in the Côtes-d'Armor region.

## 9. Credibility and limitations of this summary

This article is based on a single magazine source (Écologie 360, issue 16) with no publicly available URL, so the figures cannot be cross-checked against other sources at this time. Production capacity, avoided emissions and household figures are all attributed to industry and government sources cited within the original article, but the specific reports behind these numbers were not identified. Some figures, such as the 2030 target of 50 TWh, describe policy goals rather than confirmed outcomes and should be read as such. Quotes from Mauritz Quaak are translated from French and may lose some nuance in translation.

## 10. Bottom line

France has built a substantial biomethane industry from a mix of small farm digesters and large industrial plants, supported by guaranteed gas grid access and public financing. The sector delivers real emissions savings and supports circular farming practices through digestate use, but it faces a hard ceiling: limited feedstock means it can only ever cover a modest share of national gas demand. Continued growth will likely depend on regulatory stability, new collection models for farms far from the grid, and further capture of landfill and urban organic waste.
