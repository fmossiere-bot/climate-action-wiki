## What is Green Hydrogen

**Hydrogen** is a naturally occurring gas, and the most abundant substance in the universe. What matters for the climate is how it is produced, because that determines how "clean" it is:

- **Clean hydrogen** is hydrogen produced with very low or zero carbon emissions.
- **Green hydrogen** is hydrogen derived from water through **electrolysis**, a process that uses electricity from renewable sources to split hydrogen molecules from oxygen molecules in water. When burned, hydrogen mainly releases water vapour.
- **Blue hydrogen** is created from natural gas, with the process claiming to capture the resulting CO2, though how reliably that happens in practice is contested.
- **Grey hydrogen** is the most polluting form, produced by burning fossil fuels: it takes around 10 tonnes of CO2 to generate one tonne of hydrogen.
- **White**, or **natural**, hydrogen comes directly from the ground rather than from an industrial process (see "Getting geologic hydrogen from the ground" below).

According to McKinsey analysis, hydrogen could contribute to [more than 20 percent of annual global emissions reductions by 2050](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/sustainability/our-insights/five-charts-on-hydrogens-role-in-a-net-zero-future). Investment in the sector is already substantial:

- As of May 2023, [more than 1,000 large-scale hydrogen projects](https://hydrogencouncil.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Hydrogen-Insights-2023.pdf) had been announced globally, amounting to $320 billion in direct investment.
- In Europe, $117 billion has been invested in hydrogen projects to date, and McKinsey expects hydrogen to play a significant role in meeting the region's decarbonisation targets.
- Green hydrogen production costs are expected to fall by [approximately 50 percent by 2030](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/oil-and-gas/our-insights/the-clean-hydrogen-opportunity-for-hydrocarbon-rich-countries). By 2050, grey hydrogen, including carbon costs, is projected to be significantly more expensive than blue or green.

## Green Hydrogen as an Energy Storage Solution

While attractive in theory, hydrogen is inefficient as a storage solution. Across electrolysis, transport and re-electrification, the round trip returns only about 42%: for every 100 units of electricity used to power an electrolyser, only 42 units come back as electricity when needed.

Even so, hydrogen can be an important solution to the seasonal storage problem for grids with high levels of renewable energy, providing long-duration storage that batteries cannot match. The basic process: solar or wind energy powers electrolysis, splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen. The hydrogen is then liquefied and stored. At times of low solar and wind output, it can be used to power a fuel cell and generate electricity. This also offers a way to use excess solar power generated during oversupply, storing it as hydrogen for times when renewable output is low.

This storage method applies specifically to **green** hydrogen. Blue hydrogen, produced from carbon capture at fossil fuel plants and promoted by oil and gas companies, can in some cases have [worse greenhouse gas emissions](https://cleantechnica.com/2021/08/12/theory-versus-reality-the-dirty-hydrogen-story/) than producing the equivalent energy from natural gas, or even coal. Research from Cornell's [Robert Howarth](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ese3.956) compared carbon dioxide equivalent emissions across grey hydrogen, two variants of blue hydrogen (capturing emissions from the production process only, versus from both the production process and the exhaust flue gases), and burning natural gas, diesel or coal directly for heat, assuming a methane leakage rate of 3.5%. Blue hydrogen with only partial carbon capture performed particularly poorly on this measure.

Hydrogen is now a major commercial interest, and governments worldwide have introduced support policies for its deployment. In the United States, Inflation Reduction Act incentives could make US green hydrogen the cheapest in the world, though the industry and its supporting policies are changing fast.

While long-term energy storage and seasonal grid balancing may be hydrogen's most promising application, it can also help decarbonise "hard-to-decarbonise" industries such as ammonia and steel production, shipping, and aviation, and possibly long-distance freight transport. Hydrogen is generally not considered competitive or well-suited for heating buildings or most forms of ground transport.

It's also worth noting that hydrogen is itself a potent greenhouse gas, and emissions or leaks during production, transport and use could undermine its climate benefits. Global trade in hydrogen may develop over time, but shipping liquid hydrogen across oceans is not expected to become a major pathway, given the energy losses involved.

## Interesting Developments in Hydrogen Production

### Solar-powered green hydrogen at the panel level

The company [Fusion Fuel](https://fusion-fuel.eu/tech/) has developed HEVO units that attach to the back of high-efficiency concentrated photovoltaic (CPV) panels, forming a "HEVO-Solar" generator. By capturing both the electrical and thermal energy from the sun, the system improves the efficiency of the electrolysis reaction and reduces the cost of the resulting green hydrogen. In a location with high direct solar irradiance, such as southern Portugal, each HEVO-Solar unit can produce around one metric tonne of green hydrogen per year using only solar power.

### Hydrogen from plastic waste

As green hydrogen has become a hot topic, alternative production routes are emerging. One example, developed by [James Tour at the University of Houston](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2394144-waste-plastic-can-be-recycled-into-hydrogen-fuel-and-graphene/), rapidly heats household plastic waste to produce hydrogen without releasing CO2. A useful by-product of the process is graphene, which can be sold separately, potentially covering the full cost of production and making the resulting hydrogen effectively free. Given how much of an ongoing challenge plastic waste and recycling remain (see [FACTS about Plastic](../../circularity-waste/FACTS about Plastic.md)), this kind of dual-purpose process is an encouraging direction.

### Getting geologic hydrogen from the ground

Naturally occurring "geologic" hydrogen is another promising route to clean hydrogen that avoids the industrial electrolysis process altogether. The [US Department of Energy has committed $20 million](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2393540-how-geologic-hydrogen-went-from-fringe-science-to-potential-green-fuel/) to exploring the concept, driven partly by growing interest in hydrogen for the transport sector. Significant questions remain about the viability and environmental impact of extracting this hydrogen, but the fact that the DOE is taking the idea seriously is a notable signal.

Other companies are active in this space too. Earlier in 2023, a huge natural hydrogen deposit was found in France, which researchers suggested could contain the equivalent of half the world's current natural gas production (see [Euractiv](https://www.euractiv.com/section/energy-environment/news/excitement-grows-about-natural-hydrogen-as-huge-reserves-found-in-france/)).

### The United States: $7 billion for Hydrogen Hubs

The Biden administration pledged $7 billion to establish regional "Hydrogen Hubs" across the US, from the East Coast to the West Coast, with dozens of regions competing for the funding (source: [New York Times, October 2023](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/10/13/climate/hydrogen-hubs-biden-climate.html)). Hydrogen is widely seen as a promising tool to fight climate change, provided it can be produced without creating greenhouse gases in the process, which raises questions given how many different ways hydrogen can be manufactured. Clean hydrogen produced with wind and solar sits at one end of the spectrum; hydrogen produced using natural gas can, as noted above, sometimes be worse than coal, and carries additional risks such as methane leaks.

In theory, hydrogen could be used to produce cement, steel, chemicals and fertilisers, and to power trucks, ships, aeroplanes and electricity generation. A major challenge is figuring out how to manufacture it cleanly at scale. The US is currently boosting hydrogen through infrastructure investment and tax credits for companies producing low-emissions hydrogen, with the aim of growing production to around 10 million tonnes per year by 2030, according to the [US National Clean Hydrogen Strategy roadmap](https://www.hydrogen.energy.gov/docs/hydrogenprogramlibraries/pdfs/us-national-clean-hydrogen-strategy-roadmap.pdf).

Concerns raised about the Hydrogen Hubs programme include the risk of promoting hydrogen use in sectors where it is not the preferred solution, and estimates that at least a third of the hydrogen produced through the hubs will still be blue hydrogen. Environmental groups and some hydrogen companies argue there need to be strong safeguards to ensure funding goes to genuinely low-emission projects.

### Spain: a major green hydrogen investment in Andalusia

Cepsa, a traditional global energy company historically focused on oil and gas, is developing a large green hydrogen project in Andalusia, targeting 2GW of capacity (source: [Cepsa](https://www.cepsa.com/en/businesses/commercial-clean-energies/green-hydrogen/andalusian-valley)). The project puts Spain at the forefront of green hydrogen development in Europe, at a time when broader uptake of the technology has been slow. Investors in the project include figures from the oil industry, and Cepsa's involvement is in part an effort to "green" its image as an oil company. Even so, this kind of transition from an established oil and gas firm, whose skillset is readily transferable to hydrogen infrastructure, is broadly the kind of shift the sector needs to see more of.

### France's hydrogen jackpot

In a striking discovery, scientists uncovered a massive 46-million-tonne deposit of natural ("white") hydrogen beneath Folschviller in Moselle, France (source: [Sustainability Times](https://www.sustainability-times.com/energy/france-just-hit-the-jackpot-worlds-largest-hydrogen-treasure-worth-92-billion-sparks-energy-race-and-global-power-upheaval/)). Key points:

- Scientists discovered the deposit at Folschviller, potentially positioning France as a leader in sustainable fuel production.
- White hydrogen offers a naturally occurring clean energy alternative, potentially reducing reliance on fossil fuels.
- The discovery could create jobs and boost the local economy, and might trigger a wider shift in global energy strategy and international energy markets.

Why this matters: green hydrogen is costly because it relies on energy-intensive electrolysis, while grey hydrogen contributes to pollution because it is derived from fossil fuels. White hydrogen, by contrast, occurs naturally and, if it can be harnessed at industrial scale, could reduce dependence on costly green hydrogen production. Researchers suggest there are many more untapped natural hydrogen deposits still to be discovered.

This is not just a French story. A study by researchers at the [University of Oxford, Durham University and the University of Toronto](https://scitechdaily.com/earths-secret-hydrogen-jackpot-enough-clean-power-for-170000-years/) identified the key geological ingredients needed to find natural clean hydrogen beneath the Earth's surface, and estimated that the conditions for trapping hydrogen exist in many parts of the world, making this a genuinely global opportunity. The research could help industries discover and tap into natural hydrogen reserves as a cleaner alternative to fossil-fuel-based hydrogen production.

## 2026: The Netherlands Reuses an Active Gas Platform to Make Green Hydrogen at Sea

The **PosHYdon** pilot project produced its first batch of green hydrogen in late July 2026 on the **Q13a-A platform**, an active natural gas platform run by Eni Energy Netherlands about 13km off Scheveningen in the Dutch North Sea. It is the world's first project to combine offshore wind power, green hydrogen production and ongoing natural gas extraction on the same platform — the first "fully electrified production platform" in the Dutch North Sea sector.

The process: offshore wind electricity converts seawater into demineralised water, which is then split into hydrogen via electrolysis, while the platform's gas extraction continues in parallel. Rather than building new infrastructure, the project **reuses an existing offshore gas platform**, which partners describe as a "watershed moment" for the wider energy transition. The consortium (Eni, TNO, Emerson and others, backed by the Dutch Ministry of Economic Affairs and Climate Policy) is now gathering real operational data on cost, maintenance and performance to help offshore hydrogen production scale up.
