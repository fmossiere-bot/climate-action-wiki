## What is Green Hydrogen

**Hydrogen** is a naturally occurring gas, and the most abundant substance in the universe. For the climate, what matters is how it is made, because that decides how "clean" it is:

- **Clean hydrogen** is hydrogen produced with very low or zero carbon emissions.
- **Green hydrogen** is made from water through electrolysis, which uses renewable electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. When burned, hydrogen mainly releases water vapour.
- **Blue hydrogen** is made from natural gas, and the CO2 from the process is meant to be captured. How reliably that happens in practice is contested.
- **Grey hydrogen** is the most polluting form, made from fossil fuels with no capture. Around 10 tonnes of CO2 are released for every tonne of hydrogen.
- **White**, or **natural**, hydrogen comes directly from the ground rather than from an industrial process (see "Getting geologic hydrogen from the ground" below).

McKinsey estimates that hydrogen could deliver [more than 20 percent of annual global emissions reductions by 2050](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/sustainability/our-insights/five-charts-on-hydrogens-role-in-a-net-zero-future). A lot of money is already going in:

- As of May 2023, [more than 1,000 large-scale hydrogen projects](https://hydrogencouncil.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Hydrogen-Insights-2023.pdf) had been announced globally, amounting to $320 billion in direct investment.
- In Europe, $117 billion has been invested in hydrogen projects to date, and McKinsey expects hydrogen to play a big part in meeting the region's climate targets.
- Green hydrogen production costs are expected to fall by [approximately 50 percent by 2030](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/oil-and-gas/our-insights/the-clean-hydrogen-opportunity-for-hydrocarbon-rich-countries). By 2050, grey hydrogen, once carbon costs are included, is expected to cost a good deal more than blue or green.

## Green Hydrogen as an Energy Storage Solution

Hydrogen looks attractive for storing energy, but it is inefficient. Across electrolysis, transport and turning it back into electricity, the round trip returns only about **42%**. For every 100 units of electricity fed into an electrolyser, only 42 units come back as electricity when needed.

Even so, hydrogen could help grids with a lot of renewable power store energy from one season to the next, over longer periods than batteries can manage. The basic process works like this. Solar or wind power runs electrolysis, splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen. The hydrogen is then liquefied and stored. When solar and wind output is low, it powers a fuel cell to generate electricity. This also puts surplus solar power to use, storing it as hydrogen for times when renewable output drops.

This only works for the climate with **green** hydrogen. Blue hydrogen, made at fossil fuel plants with carbon capture and promoted by oil and gas companies, can in some cases have [worse greenhouse gas emissions](https://cleantechnica.com/2021/08/12/theory-versus-reality-the-dirty-hydrogen-story/) than producing the equivalent energy from natural gas, or even coal. Research by [Robert Howarth](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ese3.956) at Cornell compared the total climate impact (in CO2 equivalent) of several options: grey hydrogen, two kinds of blue hydrogen, and burning natural gas, diesel or coal directly for heat. One kind of blue hydrogen captured emissions from the production process only, the other from both the process and the exhaust gases. The study assumed a methane leakage rate of 3.5%. Blue hydrogen with only partial carbon capture came out particularly badly.

Hydrogen is now big business, and governments around the world have brought in policies to support it. In the United States, Inflation Reduction Act incentives could make US green hydrogen the cheapest in the world, though the industry and the policies behind it are changing fast.

Long-term storage and seasonal grid balancing may be hydrogen's most promising use. It can also help clean up "hard-to-decarbonise" industries such as ammonia and steel production, shipping and aviation, and possibly long-distance freight. It is generally not seen as competitive or well suited to heating buildings or to most ground transport.

Hydrogen is also a potent greenhouse gas in its own right, so leaks during production, transport and use could eat into its climate benefits. A global trade in hydrogen may develop over time, but shipping liquid hydrogen across oceans is not expected to become a major route, because so much energy is lost along the way.

## Interesting Developments in Hydrogen Production

### Solar-powered green hydrogen at the panel level

The company [Fusion Fuel](https://fusion-fuel.eu/tech/) has developed HEVO units that attach to the back of high-efficiency concentrated photovoltaic (CPV) panels, forming a "HEVO-Solar" generator. Because it uses both the electricity and the heat from the sun, the system makes electrolysis more efficient and the green hydrogen cheaper. In a very sunny place such as southern Portugal, each HEVO-Solar unit can produce around one metric tonne of green hydrogen a year from solar power alone.

### Hydrogen from plastic waste

As interest in green hydrogen has grown, other ways of making it are emerging. One, developed by [James Tour at the University of Houston](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2394144-waste-plastic-can-be-recycled-into-hydrogen-fuel-and-graphene/), rapidly heats household plastic waste to produce hydrogen without releasing CO2. The process also yields graphene, a valuable material that can be sold separately. That could cover the full cost of production and make the hydrogen effectively free. Plastic waste and recycling are still a big problem (see [FACTS about Plastic](../../circularity-waste/FACTS about Plastic.md)), so a process that tackles both is an encouraging direction.

### Getting geologic hydrogen from the ground

Naturally occurring "geologic" hydrogen is another possible source of clean hydrogen, one that skips electrolysis altogether. The [US Department of Energy has committed $20 million](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2393540-how-geologic-hydrogen-went-from-fringe-science-to-potential-green-fuel/) to exploring the idea, partly because of growing interest in hydrogen for transport. Big questions remain about whether extracting it is workable and what it would do to the environment, but the DOE taking it seriously is a sign the idea has moved on from the fringe.

Others are active in this space too. In 2023 a huge natural hydrogen deposit was found in France, which researchers suggested could hold around 46 million tonnes of hydrogen, roughly half of the world's current yearly hydrogen production (see [Euractiv](https://www.euractiv.com/section/energy-environment/news/excitement-grows-about-natural-hydrogen-as-huge-reserves-found-in-france/)).

### The United States: $7 billion for Hydrogen Hubs

The Biden administration pledged $7 billion to set up regional "Hydrogen Hubs" across the US, from the East Coast to the West Coast, with dozens of regions competing for the money (source: [New York Times, October 2023](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/10/13/climate/hydrogen-hubs-biden-climate.html)). Hydrogen is widely seen as a useful tool against climate change, but only if it can be made without creating greenhouse gases, and there are many different ways to make it. Clean hydrogen from wind and solar sits at one end of the range. Hydrogen made from natural gas can, as noted above, sometimes be worse than coal, and it carries extra risks such as methane leaks.

In theory, hydrogen could be used to make cement, steel, chemicals and fertilisers, and to power lorries, ships, aeroplanes and power stations. The hard part is making it cleanly at scale. The US is backing hydrogen with infrastructure investment and tax credits for companies producing low-emission hydrogen, aiming to grow production to around 10 million tonnes a year by 2030, according to the [US National Clean Hydrogen Strategy roadmap](https://www.hydrogen.energy.gov/docs/hydrogenprogramlibraries/pdfs/us-national-clean-hydrogen-strategy-roadmap.pdf).

Critics of the Hydrogen Hubs programme worry that it could push hydrogen into sectors where other solutions work better. Estimates also suggest at least a third of the hydrogen produced through the hubs will still be blue hydrogen. Environmental groups and some hydrogen companies want strong safeguards so the money goes to projects with truly low emissions.

### Spain: a major green hydrogen investment in Andalusia

Cepsa, a global energy company that has traditionally focused on oil and gas, is developing a large green hydrogen project in Andalusia, aiming for 2GW of capacity (source: [Cepsa](https://www.cepsa.com/en/businesses/commercial-clean-energies/green-hydrogen/andalusian-valley)). The project puts Spain near the front of green hydrogen development in Europe, at a time when uptake elsewhere has been slow. Its investors include people from the oil industry, and Cepsa's involvement is partly an effort to "green" its image as an oil company. Even so, an established oil and gas firm moving into hydrogen, where its skills transfer readily, is broadly the kind of shift the sector needs more of.

### France's hydrogen jackpot

Scientists have found a 46-million-tonne deposit of natural ("white") hydrogen beneath Folschviller in Moselle, France (source: [Sustainability Times](https://www.sustainability-times.com/energy/france-just-hit-the-jackpot-worlds-largest-hydrogen-treasure-worth-92-billion-sparks-energy-race-and-global-power-upheaval/)). In short:

- The deposit at Folschviller could make France a leader in sustainable fuel production.
- White hydrogen offers a naturally occurring clean energy alternative, potentially reducing reliance on fossil fuels.
- The discovery could create jobs and help the local economy, and might shift global energy strategy and international energy markets.

This matters because green hydrogen is expensive, as electrolysis uses a lot of energy, and grey hydrogen pollutes because it comes from fossil fuels. White hydrogen occurs naturally. If it can be extracted at industrial scale, it could reduce the need for costly green hydrogen. Researchers think many more natural deposits are still waiting to be found.

The opportunity goes well beyond France. A study by researchers at the [University of Oxford, Durham University and the University of Toronto](https://scitechdaily.com/earths-secret-hydrogen-jackpot-enough-clean-power-for-170000-years/) identified the main geological ingredients needed to find natural clean hydrogen underground. They found that the conditions for trapping hydrogen exist in many parts of the world. The research could help industry find and tap natural hydrogen as a cleaner alternative to hydrogen made from fossil fuels.

## 2026: The Netherlands Reuses an Active Gas Platform to Make Green Hydrogen at Sea

The **PosHYdon** pilot project produced its first batch of green hydrogen in late July 2026 on the **Q13a-A platform**, an active natural gas platform run by Eni Energy Netherlands about 13km off Scheveningen in the Dutch North Sea. It is the world's first project to combine offshore wind power, green hydrogen production and ongoing natural gas extraction on the same platform. It is also the first "fully electrified production platform" in the Dutch North Sea sector.

Offshore wind electricity turns seawater into demineralised water, which is then split by electrolysis to make hydrogen. Gas extraction on the platform carries on at the same time. Instead of building new infrastructure, the project **reuses an existing offshore gas platform**, which the partners call a "watershed moment" for the wider energy transition. The consortium (Eni, TNO, Emerson and others, backed by the Dutch Ministry of Economic Affairs and Climate Policy) is now gathering real data on cost, maintenance and performance to help offshore hydrogen production grow.
