# First Cargo Ship Running on BioMethanol

A container ship sailing from South Korea to Denmark, refuelling in Egypt on the way, became the first cargo ship ever powered entirely by "green methanol". It is a dual-fuel vessel, so it can run on either methanol or traditional fuel. All the methanol on this voyage was made from methane captured from landfills and other biological sources. Using biomethanol instead of conventional fuel cut the journey's CO2 emissions by **65%**.

With a long list of methanol-powered ships already on order, the next question is whether there will be enough methanol to go around. Around **110 million tonnes of methanol** are produced worldwide each year. A large share comes from natural gas (see [methanol.org](https://www.methanol.org)). Methanol from gas is still less polluting than conventional shipping fuel, cutting emissions by roughly 10%. The rest comes from coal, biomass, waste, and even captured CO2.

## The different types of methanol

**Traditional methanol** is made from fossil fuels and is the most common form in use today.

**Biomethanol** is made from biomass. IRENA has published a [technical brief on biomethanol production](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2013/IRENA-ETSAP-Tech-Brief-I08-Production_of_Bio-methanol.pdf), and the European bioenergy technology platform has a similar [report on biomethanol production and use as fuel](https://www.etipbioenergy.eu/images/ETIP_Bioenergy_Biomethanol_production_and_use_as_fuel.pdf). Biomethanol is clearly better than fossil methanol for cutting emissions. The limit is having enough biomass to make it at scale, and that depends on the land and space needed to grow or collect the biomass. This trade-off is covered in [Carbon Footprint of the Various Energies](../../concepts/key-ideas/Carbon%20Footprint%20of%20Various%20Energies.md) and [Space Needed for Various Energy Sources](../../concepts/key-ideas/Space%20necessary%20for%20the%20various%20Energy%20Sources.md).

**E-methanol** is made by combining hydrogen produced with renewable energy and captured CO2. The Danish shipping company behind this voyage plans to use it at scale, aiming for a fully carbon-neutral fuel. A plant making it has recently opened in China. Not everyone thinks it can work at large scale, though. Some argue there is not enough sustainably sourced carbon to supply e-methanol widely. The debate is still open, but for now e-methanol looks like the best option available.

Another way to tackle the same problem is to cut how much fuel ships need in the first place. [Team for the Planet is investing in kite-assisted cargo shipping](https://team-planet.com/en/innovations/125-beyond-the-sea) through its Beyond the Sea project (see also [ecosources.org on ship traction by kite](https://www.ecosources.org/traction-de-navire-par-voile-libre-kite)), A kite alone can cut fuel use by close to 20%. Savings like that could ease some of the future pressure on methanol supply. For more on this approach, see [Powering Ships with a Kite](Powering%20Ships%20with%20a%20kite.md).

**Ammonia** made with renewable energy is another possible carbon-neutral shipping fuel, and one that could realistically be made at scale. The problem is that ammonia is poisonous. No ships in service run on ammonia today. Ammonia engines are not yet on the market, and no existing vessels are fitted to use it. Two-stroke and four-stroke ammonia engines are being developed, along with the rules needed to handle ammonia safely as a fuel. More detail is available from the [Global Maritime Forum](https://www.globalmaritimeforum.org/news/ammonia-as-a-shipping-fuel).
