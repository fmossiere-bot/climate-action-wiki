# First Cargo Ship Running on BioMethanol

A container ship travelling from South Korea to Denmark, refuelling along the way in Egypt, became the first cargo ship ever powered entirely by "green methanol". It is a dual-fuel vessel, able to run on either methanol or traditional fuel, and all the methanol used on this voyage was produced from methane captured from landfills and other biological sources. Using biomethanol instead of conventional fuel cut the journey's CO2 emissions by **65%**.

The success of this voyage raises an obvious question: with a long list of methanol-powered ships already on order, will there be enough methanol to go around? Around **110 million tonnes of methanol** are produced globally each year. A large share of that comes from natural gas (see [methanol.org](https://www.methanol.org)), which is still far less polluting than conventional shipping fuel, cutting emissions by roughly 10%. The rest comes from coal, biomass, waste, and even captured CO2.

## The different types of methanol

**Traditional methanol** is made from fossil fuels and is the most common form in use today.

**Biomethanol** is made from biomass. IRENA has published a [technical brief on biomethanol production](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2013/IRENA-ETSAP-Tech-Brief-I08-Production_of_Bio-methanol.pdf), and the European bioenergy technology platform has a similar [report on biomethanol production and use as fuel](https://www.etipbioenergy.eu/images/ETIP_Bioenergy_Biomethanol_production_and_use_as_fuel.pdf). Biomethanol is clearly better than fossil methanol for cutting emissions, but the constraint is having enough biomass to feed production at scale. That, in turn, runs into the land and space required to grow or source that biomass, a trade-off covered in [Carbon Footprint of the Various Energies](../../concepts/key-ideas/Carbon%20Footprint%20of%20Various%20Energies.md) and [Space Needed for Various Energy Sources](../../concepts/key-ideas/Space%20necessary%20for%20the%20various%20Energy%20Sources.md).

**E-methanol** is made using hydrogen produced with renewable energy, combined with captured CO2. This is the route the Danish shipping company behind this voyage plans to pursue at scale, aiming for a genuinely carbon-neutral e-methanol. A plant producing it has recently opened in China. Not everyone is convinced this can work at large scale, though: some argue there simply isn't a sufficiently sustainable source of carbon to supply e-methanol production widely. The debate is unresolved, but for now e-methanol looks like the strongest option available.

A different approach to the same problem is reducing how much fuel ships need in the first place. [Team for the Planet is investing in kite-assisted cargo shipping](https://team-planet.com/en/innovations/125-beyond-the-sea) through its Beyond the Sea project (see also [ecosources.org on ship traction by kite](https://www.ecosources.org/traction-de-navire-par-voile-libre-kite)), which alone can cut fuel consumption by close to 20%. That kind of efficiency gain could ease some of the future strain on methanol demand. For more on this approach, see [Powering Ships with a Kite](Powering%20Ships%20with%20a%20kite.md).

**Ammonia** made with renewable energy is another potentially carbon-neutral shipping fuel, and one that could plausibly be produced at scale. The problem is that ammonia is poisonous. There are currently no in-service ships running on ammonia: ammonia-fuelled engines are not yet commercially available, and no existing vessels are equipped for ammonia propulsion. Two-stroke and four-stroke ammonia engines are under development, alongside the regulations needed to handle ammonia safely as a fuel. More detail is available from the [Global Maritime Forum](https://www.globalmaritimeforum.org/news/ammonia-as-a-shipping-fuel).
