Aviation is one of the hardest sectors to decarbonise. Electric planes are still a long way from working commercially at scale, and hydrogen is years away from wide use. In the meantime, Sustainable Aviation Fuel, known as SAF, has become the most realistic short-term way to cut emissions from flying. It is promising, but it has real limits, and they are worth understanding.

---

### Key Numbers at a Glance

- Aviation accounts for around **2.5% of global CO2 emissions** (2019), a share expected to triple by 2050
- SAF can cut lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions by up to **70%** compared with conventional jet fuel
- SAF cuts particulate emissions at cruising altitude by **50 to 97%**
- Today SAF makes up less than **1% of global aviation fuel**
- The EU required 2% SAF by 2025, rising to **70% by 2050**
- The UK mandate required 2% by 2025, rising to **22% by 2040**
- SAF currently costs roughly **double** the price of conventional jet fuel

---

### What is SAF?

SAF is a fuel that can power aircraft in much the same way as conventional jet fuel, but with a much smaller carbon footprint. It is a **drop-in fuel**: it can be blended with conventional jet fuel and used in existing aircraft engines without any changes. Most current use is a blend of around 50% SAF and 50% traditional fuel, though the aim is to reach 100% SAF over time.

SAF covers two broad families. The first is **fuels made from organic feedstocks**, such as used cooking oil, farm waste, forestry leftovers, algae, household rubbish and crops grown for energy. The second is **synthetic fuels**, also called e-fuels, electrofuels or power-to-liquid fuels. These are made from renewable electricity, water and captured CO2 rather than from plants or waste.

---

### How is SAF Produced?

There are currently four main certified ways to make SAF. The most common today is HEFA (Hydroprocessed Esters and Fatty Acids), which uses fats, oils and greases such as used cooking oil. Advanced biofuels use farm and forestry leftovers. Alcohol-to-jet processes turn ethanol into jet fuel. The power-to-liquid route makes synthetic fuel from green hydrogen and captured CO2.

It helps to know where the climate benefit actually comes from. Burning SAF in an engine produces much the same emissions as burning conventional jet fuel. The gain comes earlier, in how the fuel is made. A **lifecycle analysis**, which counts the feedstock, production, transport and burning, shows large overall cuts compared with fossil fuel. SAF also cuts particulate emissions at cruising altitude by 50 to 97%, which improves air quality around airports too.

One new development to watch is solar SAF. Swiss company Synhelion has opened its first production site in Germany and is building a plant in Spain with a capacity of 1,000 tonnes, due to open in 2027. The process uses concentrated solar heat at more than 1,500 degrees Celsius to drive a chemical reaction that makes syngas, a mixture of hydrogen and CO, which is then turned into fuel. No electricity is needed along the way. It is the first solar fuel plant of its kind in the world.

---

### Why SAF Matters for Aviation

If global commercial aviation were a country, it would be the sixth largest emitter in the world, between Japan and Germany. The sector's emissions are expected to triple by 2050 as demand for flying keeps growing, especially in Asia.

Unlike cars or lorries, planes cannot simply be electrified with today's technology. Batteries hold far too little energy for their weight to power long-haul flights. Green hydrogen is a longer-term option being explored for shorter routes, but it needs entirely new aircraft and new refuelling systems. SAF works with the planes and airports we already have, which makes it **the only option that can scale up in the near term**.

---

### Regulatory Targets

The EU and the UK have both brought in binding mandates to push SAF use.

> The EU's ReFuelEU Aviation regulation requires 2% SAF at EU airports by 2025, rising to 6% in 2030, 20% in 2035 and 70% by 2050. Within this, there is a specific sub-mandate for synthetic fuels: 1.2% by 2030, rising to 35% by 2050. Airlines benefit from the EU Emissions Trading Scheme, under which SAF emissions are zero-rated, and SAF allowances allow airlines to reclaim the price difference between SAF and conventional fuel from 2024 to 2030.

> The UK's SAF mandate came into effect on 1 January 2025, requiring 2% of total jet fuel demand to be met by SAF in 2025, increasing to 10% in 2030 and 22% by 2040.

### The Controversies

SAF has serious problems, and they deserve a clear look.

**Land use and food competition**

The biggest worry is feedstocks. When biofuels are made from crops grown on farmland, they compete directly with food, push up food prices and, at worst, encourage people to clear forests for new fields. Turning land that holds a lot of carbon, such as rainforest or peatland, into biofuel fields can release hundreds of times more CO2 than the fuel will ever save. That is why the EU has capped biofuels made from food and feed crops under its renewable energy rules, and is phasing them out.

**The corn ethanol problem**

In the United States, corn ethanol has become central to the SAF debate. Airlines are signing deals with corn growers, and ethanol makers are lobbying for clean energy tax credits. The trouble is that nearly **40% of America's corn crop** already goes to ethanol, up from 10% in the mid-2000s. Meeting the US government's target of 35 billion gallons of SAF a year by the middle of the century would mean doubling that production. That would sharply increase the groundwater pumped for irrigation and put aquifers that are already under strain at serious risk. Scientific studies have repeatedly questioned whether corn ethanol is really better for the climate than fossil fuel once everything that goes into it is counted.

**Scale and cost**

Even with more sustainable feedstocks such as farm leftovers, cooking oil and algae, making enough SAF to keep up with aviation's growing demand is a big challenge. Supply is limited and costs are roughly double those of conventional fuel. Reaching the 2050 targets would need production on a scale that does not exist yet. The 2030 targets look achievable. The road to 70% SAF by 2050, though, means either a huge expansion of biomass production, with all the land risks that brings, or a big scale-up of synthetic fuel. Synthetic fuel needs large amounts of green hydrogen and renewable electricity, which the rest of the energy transition also needs.

---

### Where this leaves SAF

SAF is a real and necessary part of cutting aviation emissions. It works in existing aircraft, cuts lifecycle emissions by a lot and reduces harmful particulate pollution around airports. EU and UK rules are pushing investment in the right direction.

But it is not a simple fix. The choice of feedstock matters a great deal, and not all SAF is equally sustainable. Corn ethanol, for example, carries real risks for water and land. The most promising long-term routes, especially synthetic fuels and solar-based production, are still at a very early commercial stage.

SAF buys aviation time while longer-term solutions are developed. It is best treated as a transition tool rather than the final answer.

### New Feedstock Pathway: Food Waste as Jet Fuel

A 2026 study from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, published in *Nature Sustainability*, shows a practical new way to turn food waste into jet-grade SAF. The process uses **hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL)**, which applies high heat and pressure to wet organic waste to make a biocrude oil. It copies the natural way crude oil forms, but in a fraction of the time.

The research stands out for keeping things simple and cheap. Instead of heavy catalytic refining, the Illinois team focused on distillation, a more familiar and cheaper industrial process. The resulting fuel is lower in quality than fully refined SAF and has to be blended with conventional jet fuel, but it meets ASTM and FAA jet fuel standards at a 50-50 blend. Even 10% or 20% blends would help, given how short SAF supply is today.

Lead researcher Prof Yuanhui Zhang compared the approach to blending biodiesel into car fuel: "It would be very difficult to produce enough SAF to meet industry needs, so it makes sense to take a biodiesel approach with a percentage blend."

The lifecycle analysis found that both the baseline and the improved treatment scenarios achieve **negative carbon emissions** overall. The biggest practical hurdle is still logistics: collecting food waste from landfills and wastewater treatment plants at scale. HTL can use treated wastewater as feedstock, which opens a route through existing municipal waste systems. A toxic by-product (HTL aqueous phase) can be treated electrochemically to recover nutrients, though for now this adds a lot to the cost.

The research is still at small laboratory scale. The next step is producing fuel by the litre, before engine testing.
