# The Rise of Batteries

## What is it

The world is moving inexorably towards renewable energy. Over the past decade, solar prices dropped by 90%, and batteries are now poised for a similarly dramatic price decline. This matters because cheap, high-quality batteries can replace natural gas as the dispatchable power source of choice, moving the grid closer to a genuinely clean energy future. RMI's report *X-Change: Batteries* concludes that battery demand is growing exponentially, driven by a **domino effect of adoption** that cascades from country to country and sector to sector, and that this domino effect is set to enable the rapid phase-out of **half of global fossil fuel demand**.

## Battery sales are growing exponentially

Battery sales are following a classic S-curve, the growth pattern typical of disruptive new technologies. For thirty years, sales have doubled every two to three years, an average growth rate of around 33%. Over the past decade, as electric cars have taken off, that rate has climbed closer to 40%.

## Costs keep falling while quality rises

As battery volumes have increased, costs have plummeted and energy density, a key measure of battery quality, has risen steadily. Over the past 30 years, battery costs have fallen by a dramatic **99%**, while the density of top-tier cells has risen fivefold. As with many modular technologies, the more batteries get deployed, the cheaper they become, which in turn drives further deployment: for every doubling of deployment, battery costs have fallen by 19% and density has risen by 7%, making batteries the fastest-improving clean energy technology tracked by RMI.

## The battery domino effect

As costs fall and density improves, one market after another tips towards battery-electric. RMI calls this the **battery domino effect**: one market going battery-electric brings the scale and technological improvement needed to tip the next. Batteries first became dominant in consumer electronics, then two- and three-wheelers, then cars. Trucks and stationary battery storage are next in line, and by 2030 batteries are expected to start taking market share in shipping and aviation too.

## Forecasters keep underestimating the pace of change

Modellers have consistently underestimated how fast batteries would grow. Superior technologies undergoing rapid cost decline tend to grow exponentially, and analysts tend to miss this, instead projecting roughly linear growth. In practice, actual battery sales keep outrunning such linear forecasts, forcing repeated upward revisions. RMI's own outlook to 2030 anticipates top-tier battery density reaching **600 to 800 Wh/kg**, costs falling to **US$32 to 54 per kWh**, and global battery sales rising to **5.5 to 8 TWh per year**. The lower end of that range already runs roughly in line with BNEF's Net Zero scenario; RMI's faster scenario exceeds it.

## Enabling the phase-out of fossil fuels

The most effective strategy to rapidly phase out fossil fuels is to accelerate deployment of technologies that reduce fossil fuel demand directly, and batteries are doing exactly that. RMI estimates batteries are on track to displace **86 exajoules of fossil fuel demand from road transport** (avoiding around 6 gigatonnes of CO2 per year), and to put a further **23 exajoules** (1.6 GtCO2/year) at risk in shipping and aviation. In electricity generation, by synchronising the variable rhythms of solar and wind supply with the timing of demand, batteries could help displace a further **175 exajoules** of fossil fuel demand, or almost 15 gigatonnes of CO2 per year. Batteries have got this far through sustained effort from companies, governments, researchers and climate advocates, and continued growth will require that effort to continue, whatever the underlying motivation: lower prices, geopolitical advantage, or climate.

## Understanding the lithium battery: the mining myth

A common objection to the battery transition is that mining the materials for batteries, particularly lithium, will end up being worse for the climate and environment than continuing to burn fossil fuels. This claim does not hold up: even accounting for the mining and processing required, batteries deliver a large net reduction in lifecycle emissions compared with the fossil fuels they displace, and battery materials can be recycled repeatedly, unlike coal, oil or gas, which are burned once and gone.
