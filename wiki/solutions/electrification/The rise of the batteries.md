# The Rise of Batteries

## What is it

The world is moving steadily towards renewable energy. Over the past decade, solar prices dropped by 90%, and batteries now look set for a similarly steep price fall. That matters because cheap, good-quality batteries can take over from natural gas as the power source the grid calls on when it needs it, which brings a clean grid much closer.

RMI's report *X-Change: Batteries* finds that demand for batteries is growing exponentially. It is driven by a **domino effect of adoption** that spreads from country to country and from sector to sector. RMI expects this domino effect to make it possible to phase out half of global fossil fuel demand quickly.

## Battery sales are growing exponentially

Battery sales are following an S-curve, the growth pattern typical of new technologies that upend a market. For thirty years, sales have doubled every two to three years, an average growth rate of around 33%. Over the past decade, as electric cars have taken off, that rate has climbed closer to 40%.

## Costs keep falling while quality rises

As more batteries have been made, costs have dropped sharply and energy density, a key measure of how good a battery is, has risen steadily. Over the past 30 years, battery costs have fallen by **99%**, while the density of the best cells has risen fivefold.

As with many technologies built from small, repeatable units, the more batteries are deployed, the cheaper they get, and cheaper batteries in turn get deployed more. Each time deployment has doubled, battery costs have fallen by 19% and density has risen by 7%. That makes batteries the fastest-improving clean energy technology RMI tracks.

## The battery domino effect

As costs fall and density improves, one market after another tips over to battery-electric. RMI calls this the **battery domino effect**: each market that goes battery-electric brings the scale and the technical progress needed to tip the next one. Batteries first took over consumer electronics, then two- and three-wheelers, then cars. Lorries and stationary battery storage are next, and by 2030 batteries are expected to start winning market share in shipping and aviation too.

## Forecasters keep underestimating the pace of change

Forecasters have consistently underestimated how fast batteries would grow. Better technologies whose costs are falling fast tend to grow exponentially, but analysts tend to miss this and project roughly straight-line growth instead. Real battery sales keep outrunning those forecasts, which then have to be revised upwards again and again.

RMI's own outlook to 2030 expects the best batteries to reach a density of **600 to 800 Wh/kg**, costs to fall to US$32 to 54 per kWh, and global battery sales to rise to 5.5 to 8 TWh a year. The lower end of that range is roughly in line with BNEF's Net Zero scenario. RMI's faster scenario goes beyond it.

## Enabling the phase-out of fossil fuels

The most effective way to phase out fossil fuels quickly is to speed up the technologies that cut demand for them directly, and batteries are doing exactly that. RMI estimates batteries are on track to replace **86 exajoules of fossil fuel demand in road transport**, avoiding around 6 gigatonnes of CO2 a year. They could also put a further 23 exajoules (1.6 GtCO2/year) at risk in shipping and aviation.

In electricity, batteries can match the ups and downs of solar and wind with the times when people actually use power. That could help replace another 175 exajoules of fossil fuel demand, or almost 15 gigatonnes of CO2 a year. Batteries have got this far thanks to years of effort from companies, governments, researchers and climate campaigners. Further growth needs that effort to continue, whatever the reason behind it: lower prices, geopolitical advantage, or the climate.

## The one-decade price fall, in one line

Cédric Philibert is an associate researcher at IFRI, formerly at Ademe and the IEA, and the author of *Pourquoi la voiture électrique est bonne pour le climat* (2024). He sums up the lithium-ion cost curve in one phrase: **"the price of batteries has been divided by ten in ten years"**. That is why fully electric vehicles have taken over the new-car market in Europe, including buses and lorries. It is also why plug-in hybrids are now a fading stopgap. They are heavier than ordinary petrol or diesel cars and in practice do not emit less CO2, because drivers rarely plug them in to charge.

## A second chemistry: sodium-ion goes commercial

For a decade, talk about energy storage was about one battery chemistry. That changed in 2024-2026. **Sodium-ion batteries** moved from pilot projects to commercial-scale use. They use sodium instead of lithium, iron-based cathodes instead of nickel and cobalt, and aluminium current collectors instead of copper. The world's largest battery maker, CATL, launched a field-tested sodium grid storage system in June 2026. BYD broke ground on a 30 GWh plant just for sodium batteries, and GM is building prototype sodium cells for US data centres through its partnership with Peak Energy.

Sodium-ion stores less energy per kilo than lithium (~160-175 Wh/kg, against 205 for LFP and 255+ for NMC), so it will not replace lithium in long-range EVs. It suits **stationary grid storage**, entry-level city EVs and home storage in cold climates. There, its strengths matter more than its weight: cheap and plentiful raw materials, no cobalt or nickel, 90% of its capacity kept down to -40°C, and safe shipping at zero volts. The result is that storage, half of the energy transition, no longer depends on a single battery chemistry. See the [sodium-ion batteries](sodium-ion-batteries.md) page for more.

## Lithium-air: CATL's long-term bet
**Lithium-air batteries** do away with heavy metals such as cobalt and nickel in the cathode. In their place is a light carbon structure that takes in oxygen directly from the air around it. In theory this lets the battery store about as much energy for its weight as fossil fuels do, which could let electric cars travel very long distances on one charge (up to 3000km!!).

There are still big engineering problems to solve, such as protecting the inside of the battery from humidity in the air and preventing lithium dendrites, tiny metal spikes that can grow inside a battery and make it dangerous. Recent lab results using **solid electrolytes** are helping to close the gap between solid-state and air-breathing designs. Industry leaders such as CATL see this chemistry as a long-term strategic goal that will not reach ordinary cars before the mid-2030s, but research around the world suggests it could one day put an end to range anxiety. ## Understanding the lithium battery: the mining myth

A common objection to batteries is that mining their materials, especially lithium, will end up doing more harm to the climate and environment than carrying on burning fossil fuels. The claim does not hold up. Even after counting the mining and processing, batteries bring a large net cut in lifecycle emissions compared with the fossil fuels they replace. Battery materials can also be recycled again and again, unlike coal, oil or gas, which are burned once and gone. See [Myth: All that mining is worse for the planet than fossil fuels](../../myths/mining-worse-than-oil.md) for a fuller look at this objection.
