# Active Travel and Human-Powered Transport

## What is it

Around 60% of car trips in the UK are under two miles, short journeys that are strong candidates for replacing with walking or cycling instead. **Active travel** means exactly this: choosing to walk, cycle or otherwise move under your own power for short journeys rather than defaulting to the car. It is good for the climate, for your wallet, and for your health.

## Why it matters

Transport is the largest emitting sector in the UK, producing 24% of the country's total emissions in 2020. These emissions break down into three types: direct emissions from the vehicle itself, indirect emissions from extracting, refining and transporting the fuel (for electric vehicles, this includes generating and transmitting the electricity), and indirect effects such as contrails from aircraft reflecting sunlight. The average diesel vehicle in the UK produced 169.8 grams of CO2 per kilometre driven in 2023, and vehicle emissions are trending in the wrong direction as cars grow larger: a decade ago SUVs made up 17% of global yearly car sales, but they now account for 39%, and larger cars emit up to 85% more than a small vehicle. In the US, the Environmental Protection Agency attributes roughly 29% of national greenhouse gas emissions to transport, and walking or cycling for just half of car trips under a mile could plausibly save hundreds of millions of dollars a year in fuel costs alone.

## A cleaner, calmer way to get around

Reducing car noise has real benefits for wildlife and people alike. Noisy environments are linked to decreased insect health, lower bird birth rates and loss of wildlife populations, while noisy roads and airports are linked to stress and to impairments in memory, attention and reading skill in children. Active travel is also a chance to reconnect with your surroundings: walking supports local high streets, with people who walk there spending 40% more than those who drive. A generation ago 70% of British children walked to school; now it is under half, despite evidence that children who walk or cycle to school tend to be more focused and less likely to be overweight. Time spent walking has also been shown to increase pro-social and pro-environmental behaviours such as kindness and empathy, yet surveys suggest we rarely make time for it: around 80% of people say they rarely or never watch wildlife, smell wildflowers, or draw or photograph nature, and 62% rarely or never stop to listen to birdsong or notice butterflies and bees.

**Tips:** look for local walking paths, gardens or parks, and use resources such as London's Great Green Routes to find green walking and cycling trails. Look for small joys along the way, stop and admire the view, and use those minutes to properly reconnect with your surroundings.

## The economics of car dependence

In the Netherlands, 26% of journeys are made by bike, compared with 18% in Denmark and 10% in Germany, and the savings from this are substantial: a Copenhagen study found the individual cost of driving is six times greater than the cost of cycling, and when the same study looked at costs and benefits to society as a whole, it found that one kilometre driven by car costs society €0.15, while society actually gains €0.16 for every kilometre cycled. Cars also degrade infrastructure far faster than bikes or pedestrians: road damage follows a "fourth-power law", meaning a vehicle carrying twice the weight per axle does sixteen times more damage. One Californian study calculated that a kilometre of road costs 13 times more to build than a kilometre of cycling infrastructure, and it would take around 10,000 cyclists to do as much damage to a road surface as a single car does in passing.

## Saving your wallet and your health

Cutting car use for short trips means no more petrol costs, parking fees or as much vehicle maintenance, and people who cycle regularly can save hundreds of pounds a year. A 2017 study found that switching short car journeys under five miles to a bike could save the average person in Scotland nearly £2,000 a year, roughly equivalent to a 9% pay rise in take-home income; time is money too, since motorists in congested areas can spend nearly five days a year stuck in traffic.

The health case is just as strong. Just 30 minutes of moderate exercise most days can significantly cut the risk of heart disease, and regular physical activity can also reduce the risk of type 2 diabetes by up to 58%, strengthen muscles and bones, and reduce the risk of falls as we age. Air pollution has also been linked to raised cancer and dementia risk. A University of Glasgow study published in the British Medical Journal in 2017 found that people who commuted by bike had a 41% lower risk of premature death, a 45% lower risk of developing cancer and a 46% lower risk of developing heart disease. The Royal College of Physicians estimates up to 40,000 early deaths a year in the UK are attributable to air pollution, second only to smoking as a cause of early death.

**Tips:** start small with your closest, most frequent journeys, the school run, the bakery, the pub with friends, and build stamina from there. Connect with neighbours to find the best local routes, and consider getting involved with pedestrian and cycling advocacy groups active in your area.

## A four-week action guide

**Week 1: learning and preparing.** Plan routes using a map or app that highlights pedestrian and cycling paths, favouring quieter streets and good signage. Brush up on cycling or walking safety and local traffic rules, for example Transport for London's cycling safety guidance or Madrid's official cycling advice. Start small, such as a ten-minute walk to the local shop instead of driving, and if you plan to cycle after a long break, try a bike rental scheme before committing to buying a bike.

**Week 2: taking action and exploring further.** Once comfortable on an initial route, venture further afield, to a park you have not visited in a while, or to meet a friend in a different neighbourhood. If cycling, consider learning basic maintenance skills such as fixing a flat tyre or adjusting brakes. Combine active travel with public transport, for example cycling to the train station, to extend your reach.

**Week 3: building habits.** Make active travel a normal part of your routine, for example scheduling grocery shopping for a day you can walk or cycle there and back, and keep discovering hidden gems along familiar routes.

**Week 4: maintaining momentum.** On days when the car feels easier, use music or a podcast to stay motivated, and plan scenic routes to keep things interesting. If cycling, consider a safety course to build confidence further, and share your progress to help inspire others.
