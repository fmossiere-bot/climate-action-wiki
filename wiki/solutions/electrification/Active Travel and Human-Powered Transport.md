# Active Travel and Human-Powered Transport

## What is it

Around 60% of car trips in the UK are under two miles. Short journeys like these could often be walked or cycled instead. That is what **active travel** means: choosing to walk, cycle or otherwise move under your own steam for short trips, rather than taking the car by default. It is good for the climate, for your wallet and for your health.

## Why it matters

Transport is the UK's biggest source of emissions, producing 24% of the country's total in 2020. These emissions come in three types. There are direct emissions from the vehicle itself. There are indirect emissions from extracting, refining and transporting the fuel (for electric vehicles, this means generating and transmitting the electricity). And there are indirect effects, such as contrails from aircraft reflecting sunlight.

The average diesel vehicle in the UK produced 169.8 grams of CO2 for every kilometre driven in 2023. Vehicle emissions are heading the wrong way as cars get bigger: a decade ago SUVs made up 17% of car sales worldwide each year, but now they make up 39%, and larger cars emit up to 85% more than a small one. In the US, the Environmental Protection Agency puts roughly 29% of national greenhouse gas emissions down to transport. Walking or cycling for just half of car trips under a mile could plausibly save hundreds of millions of dollars a year on fuel alone.

## A cleaner, calmer way to get around

Less traffic noise is good for wildlife and people alike. Noisy places are linked to poorer insect health, fewer birds being born and shrinking wildlife populations. Noisy roads and airports are linked to stress, and to problems with memory, attention and reading in children.

Active travel is also a chance to reconnect with where you live. Walking supports local high streets: people who walk there spend 40% more than those who drive. A generation ago, 70% of British children walked to school. Now it is under half, even though children who walk or cycle to school tend to concentrate better and are less likely to be overweight. Time spent walking has also been shown to encourage behaviour that is good for other people and for the environment, such as kindness and empathy. Yet surveys suggest we rarely make time to notice nature: around 80% of people say they rarely or never watch wildlife, smell wildflowers, or draw or photograph nature, and 62% rarely or never stop to listen to birdsong or notice butterflies and bees.

**Tips:** look for local walking paths, gardens or parks, and use resources such as London's Great Green Routes to find green walking and cycling routes. Look out for small pleasures along the way, stop to enjoy the view, and use those minutes to reconnect with your surroundings.

## The economics of car dependence

In the Netherlands, 26% of journeys are made by bike, compared with 18% in Denmark and 10% in Germany, and this saves a lot of money. A Copenhagen study found that driving costs an individual six times as much as cycling. When the same study looked at the costs and benefits to society as a whole, it found that each kilometre driven by car costs society €0.15, while society gains €0.16 for every kilometre cycled.

Cars also wear out roads far faster than bikes or people on foot. Road damage follows a "fourth-power law": a vehicle carrying twice the weight on each axle does sixteen times more damage. One Californian study calculated that a kilometre of road costs 13 times more to build than a kilometre of cycle path, and that it would take around 10,000 cyclists to do as much damage to a road surface as a single passing car.

## Saving your wallet and your health

Driving less on short trips means no petrol costs or parking fees, and lower maintenance bills. People who cycle regularly can save hundreds of pounds a year. A 2017 study found that switching car journeys under five miles to a bike could save the average person in Scotland nearly £2,000 a year, roughly the same as a 9% rise in take-home pay. Time is money too: drivers in congested areas can spend nearly five days a year stuck in traffic.

The health case is just as strong. Only 30 minutes of moderate exercise on most days can greatly cut the risk of heart disease. Regular exercise can also cut the risk of type 2 diabetes by up to 58%, strengthen muscles and bones, and reduce the risk of falls as we get older. Air pollution has also been linked to a higher risk of cancer and dementia. A University of Glasgow study published in the British Medical Journal in 2017 found that people who commuted by bike had a 41% lower risk of dying early, a 45% lower risk of developing cancer and a 46% lower risk of developing heart disease. The Royal College of Physicians estimates that up to 40,000 early deaths a year in the UK are caused by air pollution, second only to smoking as a cause of early death.

<!-- tag: individual-actions -->
**Tips:** start small with your nearest and most frequent journeys, such as the school run, the bakery or the pub with friends, and build up from there. Talk to neighbours to find the best local routes, and think about joining a walking or cycling campaign group in your area.

## A four-week action guide
<!-- tag: individual-actions -->

**Week 1: learning and preparing.** Plan routes with a map or app that shows paths for walking and cycling, choosing quieter streets with good signs. Brush up on cycling or walking safety and local traffic rules, for example Transport for London's cycling safety guidance or Madrid's official cycling advice. Start small, such as a ten-minute walk to the local shop instead of driving. If you plan to cycle after a long break, try a bike rental scheme before buying a bike.

**Week 2: taking action and exploring further.** Once you are comfortable on a first route, go a bit further, to a park you have not visited in a while, or to meet a friend in another part of town. If you cycle, think about learning basic repairs such as fixing a puncture or adjusting brakes. Combine active travel with public transport, for example by cycling to the train station, to go further.

**Week 3: building habits.** Make active travel a normal part of your week, for example by doing the food shopping on a day you can walk or cycle there and back. Keep looking for hidden corners along familiar routes.

**Week 4: keeping it going.** On days when the car feels easier, use music or a podcast to keep yourself going, and plan scenic routes to keep things interesting. If you cycle, think about a safety course to build your confidence, and share your progress to encourage others.
