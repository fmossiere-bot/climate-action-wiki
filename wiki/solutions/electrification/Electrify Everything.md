# Electrify Everything

## What is it

**Electrify everything** is the idea that runs through most credible decarbonisation plans: wherever possible, convert energy use in buildings, transport and industry from direct fossil fuel burning to electricity, while cleaning up the electricity supply itself as fast as possible. It matters because only around **17% of final energy use worldwide is currently electricity** (REN21), meaning the vast majority of the energy we use, for driving, heating, and manufacturing, still comes directly from burning oil, gas or coal. Global renewable power capacity has reached **3,146 gigawatts**, but that growth needs to be matched by a parallel shift of non-electric energy demand onto the grid.

Mark Jacobson, a leading researcher on deep decarbonisation at Stanford University, and his team have compiled a widely cited list of technologies that, in different combinations, can be used to build 100% clean energy systems anywhere in the world. Biofuels are generally excluded from these pathways as they are less reliable and can, in some cases, create more emissions than they save. The International Energy Agency's World Energy Outlook 2022 sets out a similar roadmap to net zero emissions in the energy sector by 2050.

## Mobility and transport

Sustainable transport policy still lags behind renewable energy policy in most countries, but momentum is building. In 2023 the EU [approved a ban](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/eu-lawmakers-approve-effective-2035-ban-new-fossil-fuel-cars-2023-02-14/) on all new fossil fuel car sales from 2035. In the United States, large states including [California](https://ww2.arb.ca.gov/resources/fact-sheets/governor-newsoms-zero-emission-2035-executive-order-n-79-20) and [New York](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/new-york-sets-2035-zero-emission-passenger-car-goal-2021-09-09/) have set zero-emissions goals for new passenger vehicles by 2035. The [UK plans](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-electric-vehicle-infrastructure-strategy) to end the sale of cars running solely on internal combustion by 2030, with all new cars and vans fully zero emission at the tailpipe by 2035. China has set a goal for EVs to make up 40% of cars sold by 2030.

See [CARS: Life Cycle Emissions, EV vs Combustion](../../sectors/transport/Car%20Life%20Cycle%20Emissions%20-%20EV%20vs%20Combustion.md) for a detailed look at how electric and combustion vehicles compare over their full lifetime.

## Cleaning up buildings

Buildings account for a striking **40% of all energy-related greenhouse gas emissions** ([RMI](https://rmi.org/our-work/buildings/)). These emissions come from two sources: **operational carbon**, the day-to-day energy used for lighting, heating and cooling, and **embodied carbon**, the emissions created by manufacturing building materials, transporting them to site, and the construction process itself.

Decarbonising buildings can also make financial sense. The main levers are energy efficiency measures and decarbonised heating, notably electric heat pumps, alongside newer solutions such as digitally controlled active efficiency and onsite solar with storage. There is also significant opportunity to cut embodied carbon through electrified construction transport and better cement production or material substitution. One estimate suggests that by 2030, **60 to 70% of carbon emissions in the buildings segment could be abated**, generating savings of 15 to 50% on annual energy spend, with paybacks below ten years for retrofits and an additional cost for new construction of under 4%.

## Decarbonising heavy industry

Heavy industry contributes **24% of global greenhouse gas emissions** and is one of the hardest sectors to decarbonise. As Rebecca Dell, who runs the industry programme at ClimateWorks, [told energy journalist Dave Roberts](https://www.volts.wtf/p/volts-podcast-rebecca-dell-on-decarbonizing), the industrial sector is highly heterogeneous, but **two-thirds of industrial emissions come from just four sectors: steel, cement, plastics and fertilisers**. These four dominate because they are produced in immense quantities globally, and the first step in each process, extracting a raw material and transforming it into a useful molecule, is extremely energy-intensive compared with everything that follows.

Broadly, the typology of decarbonisation solutions across these industries includes: improving materials efficiency (using less raw material and less final product), carbon capture and storage, green hydrogen, direct electrification, new forms of storage such as thermal heat batteries, and bioenergy, which will likely always remain a niche option given the limited supply of sustainable biomass.

For more on cement specifically, see [Cement and Concrete: A Hidden Climate Problem](../../sectors/industry/Cement%20and%20Concrete%20-%20A%20Hidden%20Climate%20Problem.md).

## Fertilisers

The chemical industry produces around 100,000 different products, yet 80% of its greenhouse gas emissions come from just 20 of them, the largest being nitrogen fertilisers and plastic. **Nitrogen fertilisers, on which global food security depends, account for around 5% of global emissions, more than aviation and shipping combined.** Research has found that two-thirds of fertiliser emissions occur after they are applied to cropland, rather than during manufacturing. Synthetic nitrogen fertilisers are made using hydrogen derived from fossil fuels, and their rise has been an environmental problem well beyond climate change, contributing to water pollution and associated health impacts, poor air quality and premature deaths, and the hypoxic "dead zone" in the Gulf of Mexico caused by fertiliser runoff. New evidence also suggests heavy fertiliser use can intensify the effects of drought on farmland.

A Cambridge study found that by 2050, fertiliser emissions could be reduced by **80%** using technology that already exists, partly by decarbonising the production process. Other solutions include replacing synthetic fertilisers with biochar-based fertilisers, and even fertilisers made from recycled human urine and faeces. Food system approaches that eliminate synthetic fertilisers entirely, such as regenerative agriculture, are covered separately in the wiki's food systems content.

## Plastics

Plastics are made from fossil fuel feedstock and are expected to [outpace coal](https://www.reuters.com/business/cop/plastics-outpace-coals-greenhouse-gas-emissions-by-2030-report-2021-10-21/) in greenhouse gas emissions by 2030. Despite decades of hype around recycling, only a fraction of plastics are collected for recycling, and only a fraction of that is actually recycled, with the process itself downgrading plastic into lower-quality material. Left unchecked, a 2023 report found that plastic use could nearly double by 2050. Bioplastics are not a meaningful alternative at scale: 2020 research calculated that replacing all plastic packaging with bioplastics would require land larger than the whole of France, and 60% more freshwater than the EU's entire annual freshwater withdrawal.

A UN report on plastics outlines how pollution could be cut by 80% through increased reuse, dramatically expanded recycling, and reorienting and diversifying materials. Beyond climate change, plastic pollution causes serious environmental harm: ocean garbage patches keep growing, and rivers such as Thailand's Chao Phraya are choked with plastic waste. Some plastics are also far "dirtier" in emissions terms than others. There is a dream of pulling carbon directly out of the air as an alternative way to make plastics, but at current cost and energy requirements, doing so at scale looks unrealistic for now. It is worth minimising single-use plastics, but reusable alternatives such as tote bags also carry a footprint and need to be reused many times before they actually reduce emissions overall, according to Our World in Data.

## Fashion and packaging

The fashion industry alone accounts for around **10% of global CO2 emissions** ([Zurich Insurance](https://www.zurich.com/en/media/magazine/2021/fast-fashion-5-practical-ways-to-cut-the-carbon-from-your-closet)), with McKinsey's report on climate and fashion a useful starting point for further reading. Staying alert to greenwashing matters here too: fast fashion giants such as H&M have come under fire for [misrepresenting their environmental credentials](https://qz.com/2180075/hm-showed-bogus-environmental-higg-index-scores-for-its-clothing/) through the Higg Index. Packaging overlaps significantly with the plastics problem, and McKinsey's report on sustainability in packaging is a useful place to start for anyone looking at that space specifically.
