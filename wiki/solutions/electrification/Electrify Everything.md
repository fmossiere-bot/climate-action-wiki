# Electrify Everything

## What is it

**Electrify everything** is the idea behind most credible plans to cut emissions. Wherever possible, energy used in buildings, transport and industry switches from burning fossil fuels directly to electricity, while the electricity supply itself is cleaned up as fast as possible.

This matters because only around 17% of the energy used worldwide is currently electricity (REN21). Most of the energy we use for driving, heating and making things still comes straight from burning oil, gas or coal. Global renewable power capacity has reached 3,146 gigawatts, but that growth has to be matched by moving the rest of our energy demand onto the grid.

Mark Jacobson, a leading researcher on deep decarbonisation at Stanford University, and his team have put together a widely cited list of technologies. In different combinations, they can be used to build 100% clean energy systems anywhere in the world. Biofuels are usually left out of these pathways because they are less reliable and can, in some cases, create more emissions than they save. The International Energy Agency's World Energy Outlook 2022 sets out a similar roadmap to net zero emissions in the energy sector by 2050.

## Mobility and transport

In most countries, policy on clean transport still lags behind policy on renewable energy, but things are moving. In 2023 the EU [approved a ban](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/eu-lawmakers-approve-effective-2035-ban-new-fossil-fuel-cars-2023-02-14/) on sales of all new fossil fuel cars from 2035. In the United States, large states including [California](https://ww2.arb.ca.gov/resources/fact-sheets/governor-newsoms-zero-emission-2035-executive-order-n-79-20) and [New York](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/new-york-sets-2035-zero-emission-passenger-car-goal-2021-09-09/) have set zero-emission goals for new passenger cars by 2035. The [UK plans](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-electric-vehicle-infrastructure-strategy) to end sales of cars that run only on a combustion engine by 2030, and to make all new cars and vans fully zero emission at the tailpipe by 2035. China wants EVs to make up 40% of cars sold by 2030.

See [CARS: Life Cycle Emissions, EV vs Combustion](../../sectors/transport/Car%20Life%20Cycle%20Emissions%20-%20EV%20vs%20Combustion.md) for a closer look at how electric and combustion cars compare over their whole lifetime.

## Cleaning up buildings

Buildings account for **40% of all energy-related greenhouse gas emissions** ([RMI](https://rmi.org/our-work/buildings/)). These emissions come from two places. Operational carbon is the day-to-day energy used for lighting, heating and cooling. Embodied carbon is the emissions from making building materials, bringing them to site and the construction work itself.

Cutting emissions from buildings can also save money. The main levers are energy efficiency and clean heating, especially electric heat pumps, along with newer options such as digitally controlled efficiency systems and solar panels with batteries on site. Embodied carbon can also be cut a lot, through electric construction vehicles and better cement production or swapping in other materials. One estimate suggests that by 2030, **60 to 70% of carbon emissions from buildings could be avoided**. That would save 15 to 50% on yearly energy bills, with retrofits paying for themselves in under ten years, and new buildings costing under 4% more to build.

## Decarbonising heavy industry

Heavy industry produces **24% of global greenhouse gas emissions** and is one of the hardest sectors to clean up. Rebecca Dell runs the industry programme at ClimateWorks. As she [told energy journalist Dave Roberts](https://www.volts.wtf/p/volts-podcast-rebecca-dell-on-decarbonizing), industry is very varied, but two-thirds of its emissions come from just four sectors: steel, cement, plastics and fertilisers. These four dominate because the world makes them in enormous quantities. In each case the first step, extracting a raw material and turning it into a useful molecule, uses far more energy than everything that comes after.

The broad types of solution across these industries are: using materials more efficiently (less raw material and less final product), carbon capture and storage, green hydrogen, direct electrification, new kinds of storage such as thermal heat batteries, and bioenergy. Bioenergy will probably stay a niche option because there is only so much sustainable biomass to go round.

For more on cement, see [Cement and Concrete: A Hidden Climate Problem](../../sectors/industry/Cement%20and%20Concrete%20-%20A%20Hidden%20Climate%20Problem.md).

## Fertilisers

The chemical industry makes around 100,000 different products, yet 80% of its greenhouse gas emissions come from just 20 of them. The biggest are nitrogen fertilisers and plastic. Nitrogen fertilisers, which global food security depends on, account for **around 5% of global emissions**, more than aviation and shipping combined. Research has found that two-thirds of fertiliser emissions happen after the fertiliser is spread on fields, not while it is being made.

Synthetic nitrogen fertilisers are made with hydrogen that comes from fossil fuels. Their rise has caused harm well beyond climate change: water pollution and the health problems that follow, poor air quality and early deaths, and the "dead zone" in the Gulf of Mexico, where fertiliser runoff has stripped the water of oxygen. New evidence also suggests heavy fertiliser use can make the effects of drought on farmland worse.

A Cambridge study found that by 2050, fertiliser emissions could be cut by **80%** with technology that already exists, partly by cleaning up how fertiliser is made. Other options include replacing synthetic fertilisers with biochar-based ones, and even fertilisers made from recycled human urine and faeces. Ways of farming that drop synthetic fertilisers altogether, such as regenerative agriculture, are covered separately in the wiki's food systems pages.

## Plastics

Plastics are made from fossil fuels and are expected to [overtake coal](https://www.reuters.com/business/cop/plastics-outpace-coals-greenhouse-gas-emissions-by-2030-report-2021-10-21/) in greenhouse gas emissions by 2030. Despite decades of talk about recycling, only a fraction of plastic is collected for recycling, and only a fraction of that is actually recycled. The process also degrades the plastic into lower-quality material. If nothing changes, a 2023 report found that plastic use could nearly double by 2050.

Bioplastics are not a realistic replacement at scale. Research from 2020 calculated that replacing all plastic packaging with bioplastics would need an area of land bigger than France, and 60% more fresh water than the EU takes from its rivers, lakes and aquifers in a whole year.

A UN report on plastics shows how pollution could be cut by **80%** through more reuse, far more recycling, and a shift to different and more varied materials. Plastic pollution also does serious damage beyond the climate: ocean garbage patches keep growing, and rivers such as Thailand's Chao Phraya are choked with plastic waste. Some plastics also carry much higher emissions than others. Some hope to make plastics from carbon pulled straight out of the air, but at today's costs and energy needs, doing that at scale looks unrealistic for now. Cutting single-use plastic is worthwhile, but reusable alternatives such as tote bags have a footprint too. According to Our World in Data, they need to be reused many times before they actually lower emissions overall.

## Fashion and packaging

Estimates of the fashion industry's share of global emissions range from **around 4% to 10%**, depending on what is counted. The often-quoted 10% figure appears in sources such as [Zurich Insurance](https://www.zurich.com/en/media/magazine/2021/fast-fashion-5-practical-ways-to-cut-the-carbon-from-your-closet), while McKinsey's report on climate and fashion put it at about 4% (2.1 billion tonnes of greenhouse gases in 2018). That report is a good starting point for further reading. It pays to watch out for greenwashing here: fast fashion giants such as H&M have been criticised for [misrepresenting their environmental credentials](https://qz.com/2180075/hm-showed-bogus-environmental-higg-index-scores-for-its-clothing/) through the Higg Index. Packaging overlaps a lot with the plastics problem, and McKinsey's report on sustainability in packaging is a good place to start for anyone interested in that area.
