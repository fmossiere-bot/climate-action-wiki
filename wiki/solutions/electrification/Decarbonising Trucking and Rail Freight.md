# Decarbonising Trucking and Rail Freight

## What is it

Road freight, meaning medium and heavy-duty lorries, is one of the most polluting parts of transport. The sector is now in the early stages of switching to zero-emission vehicles. Some argue that long-haul trucking will run on hydrogen. But battery costs are falling and range is improving fast, so it looks more likely that both regional and long-haul trucking are getting ready to go electric, even though the most polluting long-haul routes are especially hard. Electrofuels (fuels made using electricity) add another twist: Amazon has tested running its delivery lorries on a new ultra-low-carbon electrofuel. The Global Drive to Zero initiative keeps a worldwide list of zero-emission medium and heavy-duty vehicle models, which tracks progress across manufacturers.

The alternative to trucking altogether is rail. Rail is already the most electrified form of transport. India alone electrified enough railway, over 18,000 km between 2014 and 2020, to go round the world. Battery-powered trains are also emerging as a possibly cleaner and cheaper option on routes where electrifying the whole track does not pay.

## Why it matters

Medium and heavy-duty lorries make up only around **9% of the world's vehicles**. But big, inefficient diesel engines and long distances driven every year mean they produce 39% of transport's greenhouse gas emissions, roughly 5% of all CO2 from fossil fuels worldwide. Trucking is the backbone of freight and of major economies, moving the goods that keep businesses running. Online shopping, growing cities and falling car ownership are all pushing up demand for home delivery, and so for freight and commercial vehicles. Regulators and industry increasingly agree that zero-emission lorries, either battery electric (BEVs) or fuel cell electric (FCEVs), are the best way to cut emissions from road freight.

## Battery-electric versus hydrogen trucks

Most major manufacturers, including Tesla, Daimler, Volkswagen and Volvo, are investing heavily in all-electric lorries. These were expected to cost less than diesel over their lifetime (the purchase price plus running costs) by the end of the 2020s. Studies still disagree on how close that is: in the EU, a 2026 report finds they have roughly caught up, while a 2025 analysis still found a large gap (see below). A smaller group, including Toyota, Hyundai and Nikola, has focused on fuel cell electric engines instead. Hydrogen wastes more energy as a fuel, but FCEVs are still in the running for long-haul work that needs a longer range, as long as cheap, low-carbon hydrogen is available.

Chinese manufacturers are also entering the electric lorry market, building on their experience with electric buses and battery production. With strong backing from the Chinese government for the EV industry, China is likely to see the most electric lorries on its roads in the near term. IDTechEx forecasts that electric lorries will make up 9.4% of the global medium and heavy-duty market by 2030. Research from Allied Market Research expects fuel cell electric lorries, and models with a range over 300 miles, to be the fastest-growing parts of the electric lorry market, at around 30% a year.

## Barriers to adoption

The biggest obstacle is **battery technology and range**. Heavy-duty lorries need large, high-output batteries that pack in a lot of energy, and these are still expensive. Fast-charging networks along long-haul routes also need to grow a great deal. The second big obstacle is the purchase price. Electric lorries cost more upfront than diesel or petrol ones, which puts off fleet operators, even though the total cost over the vehicle's life can be lower thanks to cheaper fuel and maintenance. Government incentives and subsidies help close this gap where they exist, but not everywhere has them.

Over the next decade, stricter emissions rules will make diesel lorries more expensive (because they need costlier equipment to control emissions), while battery prices and manufacturing costs keep falling. The lifetime cost is expected to tip firmly in favour of electric lorries. A 2022 study in the journal *Transportation Research Interdisciplinary Perspectives* looked at these obstacles as a connected system. Using a method called Grey-DEMATEL, along with feedback from US fleet managers, it mapped how the obstacles feed into one another, to help guide policy.

A 2025 McKinsey analysis found that zero-emission lorries still cost **30 to 50%** more over their lifetime than diesel ones. A September 2026 report by lorry maker MAN Truck & Bus and the McKinsey Center for Future Mobility is more upbeat for the EU: it puts battery-electric lorries at 109 euro cents per km against 108 for diesel, roughly the same, helped by lower road tolls for zero-emission vehicles. Cost is still the main thing holding back faster take-up, even as the technology matures.

## Regional policy and environmental justice

In June 2020, California passed the first Electric Truck Standard in the US. It set targets for manufacturers' sales of zero-emission lorries from 2024, and aims for 100% electric lorry sales by 2045. The rule is expected to put 17,000 zero-emission articulated lorries on the road by 2035. At the start of 2024, California's Advanced Clean Fleets law came into force, requiring any fleet doing business in the state to take on more zero-emission vehicles. Because so much freight moves through California, this affects the whole country.

This is also a question of environmental justice, meaning who bears the harm. Lorries are the largest source of diesel particulate matter (fine soot) in California, responsible for an estimated 70% of the state's cancer risk from toxic air pollutants. This pollution does not fall equally. Diesel lorry emissions are concentrated in low-income communities and communities of colour that sit next to motorways, ports and other freight infrastructure. IDTechEx research also shows China is fully committed to electrifying its lorry fleet at scale, with battery demand for medium and heavy-duty lorries expected to grow considerably between 2020 and 2030.

## Battery technology outlook

Lorries today mostly use lead-acid batteries (35 to 50 Wh/kg), nickel-based batteries such as Ni-Fe, Ni-Zn, Ni-Cd and Ni-MH (50 to 110 Wh/kg), and various lithium chemistries including Li-Ion, LiFePO4 and others (90 to 200 Wh/kg). Wh/kg measures how much energy a battery stores for its weight. Research into graphene polymer batteries suggests they could clearly outperform today's best commercial cells, charging faster and lasting longer, though the exact gains in energy storage reported in early research are still uncertain. Companies including Nanotech Energy and ZapGo have been working to bring graphene-enhanced battery parts to market, while universities and research institutes are exploring how graphene could boost the capacity and charging speed of lithium-ion batteries and supercapacitors.

A study by TNO, cited by Transport & Environment, found that by 2035 all new electric freight lorries in Europe will be cheaper to run than diesel lorries, and will drive as far and carry as much. That would mean all new urban, regional and long-haul lorries could be zero emission across European markets by 2035, at reasonable cost and in time to meet EU climate targets, with some Northern European markets moving even faster.

## The role of fleet charging management

As regulation forces fleets to switch, managing charging is becoming a core skill in its own right. Flipturn, a platform that manages EV charging for fleets, was featured on the *My Climate Journey* podcast. Its co-founder and CEO, Katie Siegel, previously helped grow Samsara (a tracking and data platform for trucking and logistics) through its stock market listing. She explained how California's Advanced Clean Fleets law is pushing fleet managers to treat buying power and charging efficiently as central to running a freight business, rather than an afterthought.
