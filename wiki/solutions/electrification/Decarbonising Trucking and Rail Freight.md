# Decarbonising Trucking and Rail Freight

## What is it

Road freight, medium and heavy-duty trucks, is one of the most polluting corners of transport, and the sector is now in the early stages of a shift towards zero-emission vehicles. There is a case to be made that the future of long-haul trucking is hydrogen-powered, but with battery costs falling and range improving rapidly, it is more likely that both regional and long-haul trucking are becoming primed for electrification, even though the most polluting long-haul routes pose particular challenges. Electrofuels offer another twist on the electrification story: Amazon has piloted running its delivery trucks on a new ultra-low-carbon electrofuel. The Global Drive to Zero initiative maintains a global inventory of zero-emission medium- and heavy-duty vehicle models that tracks progress across manufacturers.

An alternative to trucking altogether is rail. Rail is already the most electrified mode of transport: India alone has electrified enough rail line, over 18,000 km between 2014 and 2020, to circle the globe. Battery-powered trains are also emerging as a potentially cleaner and cheaper option on routes where full electrification of the track is not economical.

## Why it matters

Medium and heavy-duty trucks make up only around **9% of the global vehicle stock**, but large, inefficient diesel engines combined with high average annual mileage mean the sector contributes **39% of transport-sector greenhouse gas emissions**, equivalent to roughly 5% of all global fossil-fuel CO2 emissions. Trucking is the backbone of freight and of major economies, moving the goods that keep businesses running, and the rise of online shopping, increasing urbanisation and declining personal car ownership are all pushing demand for home delivery, and therefore for freight and commercial vehicles, upward. There is growing consensus among regulators and industry that zero-emission trucks, battery electric (BEVs) and fuel cell electric (FCEVs), are the optimal way to decarbonise road freight.

## Battery-electric versus hydrogen trucks

Most major manufacturers, including Tesla, Daimler, Volkswagen and Volvo, are investing heavily in all-electric trucks, which are on track to win the total cost of ownership battle against diesel by the end of the 2020s. A smaller group, including Toyota, Hyundai and Nikola, has focused instead on fuel cell electric powertrains. Despite efficiency drawbacks with hydrogen as a fuel, FCEVs remain in the conversation for long-haul applications requiring greater range, though their viability depends on the availability of cheap, low-carbon hydrogen production. Chinese manufacturers are also entering the electric truck market, leveraging their existing experience in electric buses and battery production, and given strong Chinese government support for the EV industry, China is likely to see the most significant near-term deployment of electric trucks. IDTechEx forecasts electric trucks will reach 9.4% penetration of the global medium and heavy-duty market by 2030, while research from Allied Market Research projects fuel cell electric trucks, particularly heavy-duty models with a range over 300 miles, as the most lucrative segment of the electric truck market.

## Barriers to adoption

Two barriers dominate. The first is **battery technology and range**: heavy-duty trucks need large, powerful, energy-dense batteries that remain expensive, and fast-charging infrastructure for long-haul routes still needs significant expansion. The second is **cost of acquisition**: electric trucks carry a higher upfront price than diesel or petrol equivalents, which is a major barrier for fleet operators even though total cost of ownership can be lower thanks to reduced fuel and maintenance costs. Government incentives and subsidies help offset this gap where available, but are not universal. Over the next decade, as tightening emissions regulation raises the cost of diesel trucks (through more expensive emissions control equipment) while battery prices and manufacturing costs continue to fall, the total cost of ownership balance is expected to swing firmly in favour of electric trucks. A 2022 study in the journal *Transportation Research Interdisciplinary Perspectives* examined these adoption barriers as an interconnected system, using Grey-DEMATEL analysis alongside direct feedback from US fleet managers, to map how the barriers reinforce one another and to help inform policy.

A 2025 McKinsey analysis found the total cost of ownership gap between zero-emission trucks and diesel equivalents still runs at **30 to 50%**, underlining that cost remains the central obstacle to faster adoption even as the technology matures.

## Regional policy and environmental justice

California passed the nation's first Electric Truck Standard in June 2020, setting zero-emissions truck sales targets for manufacturers beginning in 2024, and is aiming for 100% electric truck sales by 2045; the regulation is estimated to put 17,000 zero-emission semi-trucks on the road by 2035. At the start of 2024, California's Advanced Clean Fleets law came into effect, creating further zero-emissions vehicle adoption mandates for any fleet doing business in the state, with nationwide implications given how much freight moves through California.

This is also an environmental justice issue. Trucks are the largest source of diesel particulate matter in California, responsible for an estimated 70% of the state's cancer risk linked to air toxins, and this pollution is not felt equally: diesel truck emissions are concentrated in low-income communities and communities of colour that sit alongside freeways, ports and other goods-movement infrastructure. IDTechEx research also shows China is fully committed to electrifying its truck fleet at scale, with battery demand for medium and heavy-duty trucks projected to grow substantially between 2020 and 2030.

## Battery technology outlook

Trucks today mostly rely on lead-acid batteries (35 to 50 Wh/kg), nickel-based batteries such as Ni-Fe, Ni-Zn, Ni-Cd and Ni-MH (50 to 110 Wh/kg), and various lithium chemistries including Li-Ion, LiFePO4 and others (90 to 200 Wh/kg). Research into graphene polymer battery technology suggests it could meaningfully outperform today's best commercial cells, offering faster charging and a longer service life, though the precise energy density gains reported in early research remain uncertain. Companies including Nanotech Energy and ZapGo have been working on commercialising graphene-enhanced battery components, alongside academic and research institutions exploring graphene's use in lithium-ion batteries and supercapacitors to boost capacity and charge-discharge rates.

A study by TNO, cited by Transport & Environment, found that by 2035 all new electric freight trucks in Europe will be cheaper to run, drive as far, and carry as much as diesel trucks, meaning all new urban, regional and long-haul trucks could be zero emission across European markets by 2035, cost-effectively and in time to meet EU climate targets, with some Northern European markets moving even faster.

## The role of fleet charging management

As regulation forces fleets to switch, managing charging is becoming a core operational competency in its own right. Flipturn, an EV charging management platform for fleets, was profiled on the *My Climate Journey* podcast: co-founder and CEO Katie Siegel, who previously helped grow Samsara (a telematics and IoT platform for trucking and logistics) through its IPO, discussed how California's Advanced Clean Fleets law is forcing fleet managers to treat power procurement and efficient charging as central to running a freight business, not an afterthought.
