# Decarbonising Aviation and Shipping

## What is it

Aviation and shipping each contribute less to climate change than road transport, but they are far from minor: each accounts for roughly **2% of energy-related CO2 emissions globally**, according to the International Energy Agency. Unlike cars, where swapping an internal combustion engine for a battery is now a mature solution, decarbonising planes and ships is much less straightforward, and both sectors are working through several competing pathways at once.

## Why it matters

Getting aviation and shipping onto a credible decarbonisation path matters because both sectors are hard to abate: they need extremely energy-dense fuels, operate over long distances, and rely on infrastructure and fleets that last for decades. The choices made now, over batteries, hydrogen, wind assistance and sustainable fuels, will shape how quickly these emissions can fall.

## Batteries and wind for shipping

In some cases, batteries are already a solid option for shipping. Rapidly falling battery costs have made electrification the most economical choice for ships travelling routes shorter than 1,500 km. China has launched a battery-electric container ship with room for 700 containers, running on batteries that can be swapped out as it travels hundreds of miles up the Yangtze River.

Wind power, a solution that has been around for millennia, is also making a comeback as a shipping decarbonisation tool, with several companies now building or retrofitting cargo ships with modern sailing rigs and kites. It is also worth noting that a future free of fossil fuels would likely need less shipping overall: fossil fuels accounted for almost 40% of total global shipping volumes in 2017, so much of the sector's current cargo would simply disappear as the energy transition progresses. See [Powering Ships with a Kite](../renewables/Powering%20Ships%20with%20a%20kite.md) for more on wind-assisted shipping.

## Hydrogen for shipping and aviation

Green hydrogen is another major piece of the puzzle for both sectors. In simple terms, electrolysis uses solar or wind electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen; liquefying and storing that hydrogen then provides a way to power a fuel cell and generate electricity later, wherever it is needed. Both hydrogen and batteries may have a role in decarbonising not just shipping but aviation too: a 40-passenger hydrogen-electric plane completed a successful maiden flight, though it will still be some years before hydrogen-electric aircraft are carrying passengers commercially. See [Sustainable Flights with Hydrogen](../renewables/Sustainable%20FLighs%20with%20Hydrogen.md) for more detail.

## Sustainable aviation fuel

In the meantime, many airlines are turning to SAF, or sustainable aviation fuel, a "drop-in" replacement that can be made from corn stalks or municipal solid waste, but which most often derives from used cooking oil. Climate Action 100+ has reported that a massive, rapid scale-up of SAF production is crucial to keeping global warming below 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels. For a fuller treatment of SAF's promise and its real limitations, see [Sustainable Aviation Fuel: A Necessary but Complicated Solution](Sustainable%20Aviation%20Fuel%20-%20%20A%20Necessary%20but%20Complicated%20Solution.md).
