# Decarbonising Aviation and Shipping

## What is it

Aviation and shipping each do less harm to the climate than road transport, but they are far from small. Each accounts for roughly **2% of energy-related CO2 emissions worldwide**, according to the International Energy Agency. With cars, swapping a petrol or diesel engine for a battery is now a well-established fix. With planes and ships it is much harder, and both sectors are trying out several competing routes at once.

## Why it matters

Aviation and shipping are hard to clean up. They need fuels that pack a lot of energy into a small space, they travel long distances, and their fleets and infrastructure last for decades. That is why it matters to put them on a believable path to lower emissions now. The choices made today, between batteries, hydrogen, wind and sustainable fuels, will shape how quickly these emissions can fall.

## Batteries and wind for shipping

For some ships, batteries are already a solid option. Battery prices have fallen so fast that electric power is now the cheapest choice for ships on routes shorter than 1,500 km. China has launched a battery-electric container ship with room for 700 containers. Its batteries can be swapped out as it travels hundreds of miles up the Yangtze River.

Wind power, which has moved ships for thousands of years, is also making a comeback. Several companies are now building cargo ships with modern sails and kites, or fitting them to existing ships. A future without fossil fuels would also probably need less shipping overall. Fossil fuels made up almost 40% of all the world's shipping volumes in 2017, so much of the cargo ships carry today would simply disappear as the energy transition goes on. See [Powering Ships with a Kite](../renewables/Powering%20Ships%20with%20a%20kite.md) for more on wind-assisted shipping.

## Hydrogen for shipping and aviation

Green hydrogen is another big part of the answer for both sectors. Put simply, electrolysis uses solar or wind electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. The hydrogen can then be liquefied and stored, and later used in a fuel cell to make electricity wherever it is needed. Hydrogen and batteries may both help cut emissions in aviation as well as shipping: a 40-passenger hydrogen-electric plane has completed a successful first flight, though it will still be some years before hydrogen-electric aircraft carry paying passengers. See [Sustainable Flights with Hydrogen](../renewables/Sustainable%20FLighs%20with%20Hydrogen.md) for more detail.

## Sustainable aviation fuel

In the meantime, many airlines are turning to SAF, or sustainable aviation fuel. It is a "drop-in" replacement, meaning it can go straight into existing planes. It can be made from corn stalks or household rubbish, but most often comes from used cooking oil. Climate Action 100+, a group of investors pressing big emitters to act, has reported that a huge, fast increase in SAF production is essential to keep global warming below 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels. For a fuller look at what SAF can and cannot do, see [Sustainable Aviation Fuel: A Necessary but Complicated Solution](Sustainable%20Aviation%20Fuel%20-%20%20A%20Necessary%20but%20Complicated%20Solution.md).
