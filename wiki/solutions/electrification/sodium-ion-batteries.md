# Sodium-ion batteries: a second chemistry comes of age

## What is it

A **sodium-ion battery** works on the same "rocking chair" principle as a lithium-ion battery: ions shuttle between a positive and a negative electrode as the cell charges and discharges. The only change is the ion, sodium (Na) instead of lithium (Li). Sodium sits just below lithium in the periodic table and behaves in a similar way, but its atoms are bigger, so the electrodes have to be different. Sodium batteries use a hard carbon anode instead of graphite, and an iron-based cathode (often a Prussian blue analogue, or a layered oxide) instead of chemistries based on lithium, cobalt and nickel. They can also use cheap aluminium foil for both current collectors instead of expensive copper.

The result is a battery that stores less energy per kilo than a top-end lithium cell. In return it is cheaper, made from far more plentiful materials, much cleaner to produce, and copes much better with cold weather.

## Why it matters

For a decade, almost every part of the clean energy transition, from grid batteries to electric cars to home storage, has relied on a single family of battery chemistry. That reliance brings three problems: lithium prices spike, cobalt supply is tied to human rights abuses in the Democratic Republic of Congo, and lithium batteries struggle in real cold. Sodium-ion solves all three. It does not suit every use, but it suits enough of them to change the strategic picture.

The big shift in 2024-2026 is that **sodium-ion moved from pilot projects to commercial use at scale**. The world's largest battery maker, CATL, has launched a field-tested sodium-ion grid storage system. BYD is building a plant just for sodium-ion. GM is building prototype sodium-ion cells for US data centres. That is a long way from "promising lab research".

## Where it fits best

### Grid-scale stationary storage (strongest case)

For an electricity company, battery weight does not matter. What matters is the cost per kilowatt-hour over the battery's life, how many times it can be charged, safety and running costs. Sodium-ion does well on most of these:

- Cheap raw materials (sodium costs roughly 1/40 of lithium per tonne)
- Copes with big temperature swings without active cooling, which cuts the power a battery farm uses to run itself
- Cannot catch fire while being shipped (see below)
- No cobalt or nickel in the cleanest versions

Short-duration battery storage (3-8 hours) is the right tool for most grid balancing in a system that runs mainly on renewables. Sodium-ion fits that role well, working alongside lithium-ion rather than replacing it. For longer storage, pumped hydro, compressed air, hydrogen and other technologies take over.

### Entry-level and urban electric vehicles

An average SUV running entirely on sodium-ion batteries has a **range of around 250 to 350 km**, compared with 400 to 600 km on a top-end lithium pack. That is a serious limit for long journeys, but it works well enough for:

- City cars (the daily commute, the school run, errands)
- Light delivery vans
- E-bikes, mopeds, scooters
- Industrial forklifts
- City buses

Chinese manufacturers (BYD, Hina, Yiwei) are already producing small sodium-powered EVs.

### Dual-chemistry hybrid packs

One sensible compromise is to put sodium-ion and lithium-ion cells **inside the same battery casing**. The sodium cells handle starting in sub-zero cold and winter range, where lithium struggles. The lithium cells provide the dense energy needed for long-distance driving. The battery management system switches between them without the driver noticing. CATL has already shown this design in public.

### Cold-climate home storage

Sodium-ion keeps around **90% of its capacity** down to -40°C. For home solar batteries kept in a garage, shed or outhouse (common in Ireland, the UK and Scandinavia), that is a real advantage over lithium systems, which lose capacity in the cold or use energy to keep themselves warm.

## Where it does not fit

- **Premium long-range EVs** (Tesla, luxury SUVs, long-haul freight): the gap in energy density is too wide
- **Portable electronics** (phones, laptops): weight and size matter too much
- **Grid storage lasting more than ~8 hours**: pumped hydro, compressed air, hydrogen and others take over for storage over weeks or months (see [intermittency-storage-problem](../../myths/intermittency-storage-problem.md))

## Three advantages beyond cost

### 1. Shipping safety

Lithium batteries have to keep some charge while being transported, or they are permanently damaged. So they always travel with stored energy, which brings a risk of thermal runaway, a fire that feeds itself, during sea freight. **Sodium-ion cells can be shipped at zero volts**: fully discharged, chemically inert and non-flammable. For island nations like Ireland that import batteries by sea, this means real savings on logistics and insurance.

### 2. Ethics

Roughly **75% of the world's cobalt** comes from the Democratic Republic of Congo, where small-scale, informal mining has been widely documented for unsafe conditions, forced labour and child labour. Standard high-density lithium batteries (NMC chemistry) use cobalt and nickel. Sodium-ion batteries, in their cleanest versions, use neither: iron and sodium are the main ingredients. The ethical case for sodium is strongest where it replaces NMC batteries (see [clean-energy-child-labour](../../myths/clean-energy-child-labour.md)).

### 3. Water and ecosystems

Lithium extraction, especially by evaporating brine in South America's "Lithium Triangle", uses millions of litres of water for every tonne of lithium produced. That puts pressure on local communities and desert ecosystems. Sodium comes from soda ash or salt beds, using much simpler and less damaging processes.

## The main obstacle: China does ~95% of planned capacity

The weak point in the sodium-ion story is that production is concentrated in one place. **Over 95% of the manufacturing capacity** planned up to 2030 is in China. Companies outside China have struggled: Natron Energy, a US sodium-ion company, has closed, and LG Energy Solution set up its own sodium pilot in Nanjing rather than at home. For now, Europe and North America are buying sodium-ion batteries from China. That carries the same supply-chain risk as the last generation of solar panels.

## Technology readiness and cost

Sodium-ion was at **Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 3-4 in 2020**, meaning lab-scale pilots. It reached TRL 8 by 2024, meaning the first commercial systems of their kind. Industry projections expect global sodium-ion stationary storage capacity to grow roughly 15× between 2025 and 2030, from around 10 GWh to around 150 GWh. The target pack cost is ~$40 per kWh once manufacturing matures. At that level it would be cheaper than today's lithium batteries for grid-scale use.

## Ireland angle

<!-- tag: ireland -->
Ireland's existing grid-scale storage (Lumcloon, Shannonbridge, Cushaling) uses lithium batteries. Early work with sodium-ion in Ireland has come through:

- The **TRIDENT project**, supported by the Disruptive Technologies Innovation Fund and the International Energy Research Centre, which has worked on connecting sodium-ion storage to inverters for home microgrids.
- The **Galvani home battery** from Eleven Energy, a sodium-ion home storage system reportedly sold in Ireland through Midsummer Ireland and marketed for cold garages and sheds.

These details come from the current source material and have not been independently checked. If you are thinking of a sodium-ion battery at home in Ireland and want to put it outdoors, the cold-weather advantage is real. For large-scale use, the question is less about the chemistry and more about which Chinese supplier Ireland buys from, and when.

## What this does to the storage picture

In short, **sodium-ion means storage, half of the energy transition, no longer depends on a single battery chemistry**. Critics worried that scaling up lithium batteries would run into limits on lithium supply, cobalt ethics, or cobalt-free options that still needed nickel. The answer now shipping at scale is to use sodium batteries for jobs that stay put, lithium batteries for jobs that move, and dual-chemistry packs where cold is a problem.

That strengthens the answer to the [intermittency-storage-problem](../../myths/intermittency-storage-problem.md) myth. It weakens the "renewables need too much mining" argument in [renewables-more-mining](../../myths/renewables-more-mining.md). It takes some of the heat out of [clean-energy-child-labour](../../myths/clean-energy-child-labour.md), because cobalt is no longer built into every battery. And it adds a second tool alongside sand batteries (see [sand-battery-finland-pornainen-2026](../../sources/sand-battery-finland-pornainen-2026.md)) and the existing lithium-ion batteries described on [The rise of the batteries](The%20rise%20of%20the%20batteries.md).
