Over a 20-year period, methane is around 80 times more potent as a greenhouse gas than CO2. One of its largest sources is waste rotting in landfills. Landfills alone account for around 12% of all methane emissions caused by people (Project Drawdown), and landfills and wastewater together for about 20% (UNEP Global Methane Assessment, 2021). When organic waste breaks down without oxygen, it releases methane straight into the atmosphere.

Several solutions already exist. Landfill operators can capture methane on site and turn it into energy. Food waste can go through methane digesters, which turn it into biogas instead of leaving it to rot in landfill. And wasting less food, on farms, in factories and at home, cuts the problem at its source.

A new approach now being tested could add to all of these.

---

### Methane-Eating Bacteria

Researchers are developing portable bioreactors (containers where living organisms do the work) filled with a carefully chosen strain of bacteria that feed on methane. The bacteria eat the methane as it leaks from landfill sites or coal mines, converting it before it reaches the atmosphere. The system is designed to work off-grid, so it can be used in places that lack the infrastructure for conventional capture.

If scaled up, these bioreactors could capture the equivalent of 24 million tonnes of CO2 a year by the middle of the century. That will not change things much on its own, but as one part of a wider set of measures to cut methane, it is a useful contribution.

The technology is still at an early stage, and it has yet to prove itself at scale in the real world. But the approach is practical, fairly low-tech to set up, and aimed at a source of emissions that is hard to deal with in other ways.
