The ocean has absorbed roughly one third of all the CO2 humans have emitted since the industrial revolution. It is one of the planet's most important carbon sinks, and researchers are looking more and more at how marine life, especially seaweed and kelp, could be used to pull extra carbon out of the atmosphere.

It is a promising area, but the science is still developing and it pays to be cautious.

---

### What Marine Algae Can Do

Seaweed, kelp and other marine algae absorb CO2 as they grow and store the carbon in their tissue. Studies suggest kelp forests can lock away much more carbon per acre than forests on land. Worldwide, seaweed is estimated to store around 175 million tonnes of CO2 a year naturally. According to Project Drawdown, scaling up seaweed farming could store between 2.5 and 4.7 billion tonnes of CO2 equivalent by 2050, roughly 1 to 2% of current global emissions.

Marine algae offer other benefits too:

- They help slow ocean acidification by absorbing CO2 and releasing oxygen
- Kelp forests act as a natural barrier against coastal erosion and storm surges
- Farmed alongside shellfish such as oysters and mussels, they help clean the water and remove excess nitrogen
- They can be a sustainable source of food, animal feed, fertiliser, biomaterials and biofuel

Several companies are already working in this field. Greenwave supports a worldwide network of small regenerative ocean farms. Coast 4C, based in the Philippines, works with local seaweed farmers to combine climate benefits with community development. Keel Labs is making biomaterials from seaweed. Others are looking at uses in animal feed, cosmetics and food additives.

---

### The Challenges Are Real

For all the promise, there are big uncertainties that need to be taken seriously.

**Keeping the carbon locked away is not simple.** Some kinds of kelp have gas bladders that help them float. When they break off, they can drift ashore and rot in shallow water, releasing their carbon back into the atmosphere instead of storing it on the seabed. Seaweed habitats that attract plankton and shellfish also release carbon back into the water over time.

**The effect of farming on a large scale on sea life is not well understood.** Dumping large amounts of dead seaweed on the seabed could have serious consequences for marine life. Seaweed farming today also relies on a small number of species, which reduces genetic diversity and leaves crops more exposed to disease, pests and environmental stress.

**Measuring and checking the carbon is difficult.** As with removing carbon through forests, confirming how much carbon is really stored, and for how long, is still a major challenge. Without reliable monitoring, the climate benefit may be overstated.

### 2026 Update: Growing Seaweed Between Wind Turbines

One practical answer to the questions of space and scale comes from Denmark. Researchers at Aarhus University, led by Annette Bruhn, have spent 15 years improving ways to grow **sugar kelp** in Danish waters. They have shown that offshore wind farms can also be seaweed farms. Seaweed lines are strung between turbines in the North Sea, sharing the service boats and infrastructure already built for energy, so no extra sea space is needed.

Conditions offshore (strong water flow, stability, plenty of nutrients) turned out to be among the most productive the researchers have tested. As well as its uses for food and biomaterials, the kelp removes nitrogen and phosphorus from coastal waters, which helps efforts to cut nutrient pollution. Denmark's offshore wind expansion will set aside large new areas of the North Sea for energy by 2030, and the researchers argue that the empty space between turbines is too large to leave unused.

The bottleneck, Bruhn says, is the market rather than the science. Food manufacturers, retailers, meal-kit companies and canteens need to create demand, as they did for beans and lentils, which went from niche to mainstream in European diets in roughly a decade.

### September 2026 Update: Preservation, not cultivation, is the priority

A PLOS Biology study led by Karen Filbee-Dexter (University of Western Australia), published in September 2026, argues that seaweed farming and restoration are **unlikely to be a major climate solution**. It says the priority should shift to protecting the kelp forests we already have.

The main numbers:

- Seaweeds absorb ~3.7 Gt CO2/yr through photosynthesis, but only a small fraction ends up stored for the long term in the deep sea as "blue carbon"
- **~60% of the world's kelp forests are in decline** because of ocean heat. Kelp loss now releases an estimated 14 million tonnes of CO2 a year, while an ambitious restoration project in Norway (70 hectares) only took up 50,000 tonnes/year. That is a 280:1 ratio in the wrong direction
- **Protecting kelp is at least 8× cheaper per hectare than restoring it**, yet only 2% of kelp forests are in protected areas
- **9 of 11 seaweed farms studied emit more CO2 than they store** once boat fuel and plastic lines are counted
- Running Tide (a US start-up that raised $70m to sink kelp buoys in the open ocean) shut down in 2025 after failing to grow kelp at scale

The study does not argue against kelp restoration as such. Farming still supports low-carbon alternatives to fossil-based products (fertiliser), and restoration is still valuable for wildlife. But **the "seaweed will sequester hundreds of millions of tonnes of CO2" claim**, used by aquaculture firms such as Kelp Blue, does not stand up to scrutiny. Pamela Walsh of Queen's University Belfast takes a different view: she says the science on carbon storage is not yet strong enough to write the sector off, only that it needs checking more carefully.

The lesson matches what shows up for mangroves, wetlands and peatlands: **"don't lose what you have" beats "restore what you lost", every time**, and both work better than "grow it in new places". This puts the climate role of marine algae somewhere between Project Drawdown's 2.5-4.7 Gt CO2/year upside and the evidence on the ground that most farms are net emitters. Carbon claims from seaweed that can be trusted will need much better measurement and checking, and should put protecting existing kelp first.

*Source: [Why growing seaweed isn't a climate solution (source summary)](../../sources/seaweed-not-a-climate-solution-newscientist-2026.md); Alec Luhn, New Scientist, 8 September 2026; Filbee-Dexter et al., PLOS Biology, DOI 10.1371/journal.pbio.3003949*

### Questions of Equity

Another side of this topic deserves attention: who benefits, and who makes the decisions.

Indonesia and the Philippines together grow over 32% of the world's seaweed, yet they get far less funding and support for new ideas than producers in Europe and North America. In 2022, Europe had over 176 seaweed start-ups, compared with just 37 across Asia. This imbalance matters. It is unfair, and it means the communities with the most experience of growing seaweed at scale are left out of decisions about how the sector develops.

More broadly, the way the ocean is governed is fragmented and complicated, and depends heavily on voluntary commitments. This tends to favour bigger, better-resourced players, and pushes aside coastal and indigenous communities who depend on the ocean and have the most at stake.

Any serious effort to scale up marine algae for carbon removal needs to tackle these imbalances as well as the technical challenges.

---

### Where This Leaves Marine Algae

Marine algae have long been seen as one of the more promising and versatile tools for carbon removal, although the September 2026 PLOS Biology study above suggests that protecting existing kelp forests matters more than farming new seaweed. Algae bring several benefits beyond carbon, they can support coastal livelihoods, and they do not compete with food production on land. But the science on how much carbon they really store, and for how long, is not yet settled.

This is an area worth watching and investing in carefully, with proper monitoring, a wide range of species, and real involvement of the communities that know these ecosystems best.
