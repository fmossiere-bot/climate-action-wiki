This article looks at four examples of companies and research projects working on it, using very different approaches. Terra Fertilis uses biochar to store carbon in soil while improving its health. Silicate, an Irish start-up, spreads cement dust on farmland to trigger natural mineral absorption of CO2. Heirloom Carbon pulls CO2 directly from the air using processed limestone. A separate research team is developing an electrically charged mist to capture carbon from power plant emissions. Each approach has promise, and each has limits.

This is a selection of companies working on carbon removal, each with a different approach. The technologies behind them will be covered separately. What these companies share is that they are trying to take out carbon that is already in the atmosphere or the environment, rather than only cutting new emissions.

### Terra Fertilis - Biochar

Terra Fertilis is one of the leading producers of biochar, a kind of charcoal made from plant material. Added to soil, biochar can do several jobs at once:

- It improves soil health and fertility
- It helps soil hold on to water
- It stores carbon for a very long time

The carbon storage is considerable. Roughly 23 tonnes of CO2 can be captured for every 30 tonnes of biochar spread per acre. Biochar has over 55 documented uses, which makes it unusually versatile. One example: when it is added to the grass cattle eat, it can cut the methane cows produce by around 10 to 15%.

Biochar could also replace some synthetic fertilisers, which are responsible for around 5% of global emissions (University of Cambridge, 2023), roughly as much as aviation and shipping combined.

### Silicate - Enhanced Rock Weathering
#ireland 

Silicate is an Irish start-up with an unusual approach. It uses cement dust, a waste product from the construction industry, to improve soil and capture CO2. The process is based on mineral weathering, a natural process in which certain rocks slowly absorb carbon dioxide. Silicate speeds this up by spreading fine mineral particles on farmland, which also makes the soil more fertile.

It is worth a mention because it comes from Ireland, and because it pairs carbon removal with a practical benefit for farmers, without needing new or unusual materials.

---

### Heirloom Carbon - Direct Air Capture (USA)

Heirloom Carbon, based in San Francisco, uses limestone to soak up CO2 directly from the air. Limestone is a common natural mineral, available in large amounts. Heirloom treats it so that it works like a sponge for CO2 in the air. Once the limestone is full, the carbon is taken out and stored permanently.

The company has signed a deal with Microsoft as part of Microsoft's carbon commitments. Its stated goal is to remove one billion tonnes of CO2 a year by 2035.

Heirloom is also clear about how it works. It does not sell captured CO2 to businesses that would release it back into the atmosphere. Its founders have openly criticised greenwashing in the carbon removal sector, especially by oil and gas companies that use vague capture promises as a way to avoid cutting emissions.

---

### Research Direction: Electrically Charged Mist for Power Plants

This is not a company yet, but a piece of research worth noting. A team reported in New Scientist has designed a system that sprays a fine mist of electrically charged droplets containing potassium hydroxide to capture CO2 from power plant exhaust. The CO2 reacts with the mist to form potassium carbonate, which can then be processed so the carbon can be extracted and stored. The researchers estimate the system could reach 95% efficiency. For now, cost is the main thing holding it back.

---

These examples show that carbon removal is not one single technology. It ranges from farming practices like biochar to industrial processes like direct air capture. Each has its own limits, costs and potential. None of them is enough on its own, but together they show the range of tools being developed.
