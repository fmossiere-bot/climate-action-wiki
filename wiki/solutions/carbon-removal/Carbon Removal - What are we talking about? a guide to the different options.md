Cutting emissions is essential, but it is no longer enough on its own. Action has been delayed for so long that we now also need to remove carbon that is already in the atmosphere. This article gives an overview of the main approaches, natural and technological, and what each can and cannot do.

Carbon removal does not replace cutting emissions. It sits alongside it, and we need both.

---

### What is Carbon Removal?

Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR), also called carbon sequestration, means taking CO2 out of the atmosphere and storing it so that it cannot get back. It covers a wide range, from protecting forests to industrial machines that pull CO2 straight out of the air.

The IPCC accepts that CDR has to be part of the plan to keep warming within 1.5 degrees. This is partly because emissions are not falling fast enough, and partly because some emissions, from certain industries and from gases like methane, will be very hard or far too expensive to get rid of completely.

But the scale needed is enormous. Reaching even 25% of the IPCC's removal target would take more than 100,000 times today's yearly capacity. For comparison, the largest carbon capture facility in the US aims to remove around 1 million tonnes a year. The IPCC scenarios need 10 to 20 billion tonnes removed every year. We are nowhere near that.

The EU has recognised this and has limited the part carbon removal can play in its 2030 climate targets to around 2%, equal to 225 million tonnes.

---

### The Cost Problem

Cost is one of the biggest obstacles for every kind of CDR. Today, removing one tonne of CO2 with direct air capture costs around $600 to $800. Some forecasts say this could fall to $200 to $300 by 2030, but experts disagree on whether even that is realistic.

Forest carbon credits, by comparison, sell for around $5 to $15 per tonne. That price gap explains why so many companies choose tree-planting offsets. But cheaper does not mean the same. Forest carbon carries real risks: trees die, forests burn, and carbon stored in wood is not removed for good. In carbon accounting, how long the carbon stays locked away matters a great deal.

## Natural Approaches

### Forests

Forests are the best-known natural carbon sink. In a typical year, forests and other plants absorb around 30% of the CO2 humans emit. But that share has been falling, and measuring exactly how much forests absorb is harder than it looks.

Tree age, species, size, soil type and local climate all affect how much carbon a forest stores. Recent research has also found that replanting forests can have opposing effects: tree canopies are darker than open ground, so they absorb more heat, which cancels out part of the carbon benefit.

One of the clearest findings of recent research is that **protecting existing forests is far more effective than planting new ones**. The overall carbon cost of losing untouched tropical forest is now estimated to be six times higher than previously thought. Yet carbon loss from tropical forests has roughly doubled over the past twenty years.

**On tree planting:** New plantations and replanting projects have a part to play, but only alongside deep cuts in emissions, and only when done well. Single-species plantations make up most land restoration work today, even though the evidence shows they work far less well than varied, mixed forests. A large international study found that mixed-species forests capture up to 25% more carbon than single-species plantations, and are healthier overall.

Tree planting can also be done badly. Poor choice of species, destroyed habitats and forest communities pushed off their land are documented problems in countries including India and Indonesia.

**On accounting risks:** Forest carbon credits are especially prone to being overstated or misrepresented. Satellite estimates of the plant matter above ground can overstate the carbon in tropical replanting projects by up to ten times. Inflated or entirely made-up forest credits have been found in several countries. This is an area to approach with caution.

---

### Peatlands

Peatlands are among the most overlooked carbon stores on the planet. They hold more carbon than all other types of vegetation combined, forests included. Yet around 15% of the world's peatlands have already been drained, and drained peatlands give off roughly two billion tonnes of CO2 equivalent a year (about 1.9 billion tonnes, around 5% of human-caused greenhouse gas emissions, according to IUCN).

Peatlands work by holding water, which stops dead plants from rotting away completely. As the climate warms and peatlands dry out, they catch fire more easily. That creates a vicious circle: drying leads to burning, which leads to more drying.

The Congo Basin is home to one of the world's largest peatlands, but only 8% of its peat carbon sits within protected areas.

---

### Soils and Agriculture

Soils hold a lot of carbon, and better farming can increase how much they store. Regenerative farming, cover crops and ploughing less all help. As with forests, there are problems with measurement, permanence and additionality (see below). But there are other good reasons to improve soil health beyond the climate, including food security and relying less on synthetic fertilisers.

---

### Oceans

The oceans have absorbed around one third of all the CO2 humans have emitted since the industrial revolution. They are a major carbon sink, but they are also under threat. Rising CO2 has made surface water around 25% more acidic, which harms sea life, especially corals and shellfish.

Ocean-based carbon removal includes seaweed farming and enhanced marine alkalinity (adding minerals to seawater so it absorbs more CO2). Seaweed farming is promising, but the science on how much carbon it really removes is not settled. Ocean CDR is still a research field rather than a solution ready to use.

---

### Key Risks Across All Natural Approaches

All nature-based solutions share a set of common risks:

- **Additionality**: Would the carbon have been stored anyway, without the project? If so, no extra carbon is removed.
- **Leakage**: Protecting a forest in one place can simply push the damage somewhere else, with no overall benefit.
- **Permanence**: How long will the carbon stay stored? A tree that burns releases its carbon immediately. Rock storage lasts millennia.
- **Social harm**: Putting a price on natural land can shut out indigenous communities with long-standing rights to it. This has already happened, and is well documented.

## Technological Approaches

### Point-Source Carbon Capture (CCS and CCU)

Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) catches CO2 where it is released, such as the chimney of a power plant or factory, before it reaches the atmosphere. Carbon Capture and Utilisation (CCU) goes a step further and turns the captured CO2 into useful products, including synthetic fuels.

These approaches are cheaper than removing CO2 from open air, because the gas is far more concentrated at industrial sources than in the atmosphere. But costs are still high, and the technology has proved especially hard to make work at coal plants.

---

### Direct Air Capture (DAC)

DAC removes CO2 directly from the open air, wherever it was emitted. It is the most flexible technology, and also the one that uses the most energy.

The first commercial DAC plant, called Orca, was built by the Swiss company Climeworks in Iceland. It runs on geothermal energy, and the captured CO2 is pumped into basalt bedrock, where it turns into rock within two to three years. That is truly permanent storage. Climeworks has since opened a larger plant called Mammoth, which started capturing CO2 in May 2024 and is designed to remove up to 36,000 tonnes a year.

In Texas, Occidental Petroleum is building a facility targeting 500,000 tonnes of CO2 per year.

To give a sense of the energy involved: removing 40 billion tonnes of CO2 a year with DAC would take close to half of all the energy the world currently produces. That energy would need to be clean, or the process would create as many problems as it solves.

---

### BECCS (Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage)

BECCS is a hybrid approach. Plants absorb CO2 as they grow. When they are burnt for energy, the emissions are captured and stored underground, so in theory more CO2 is removed than released.

In practice, BECCS at large scale is seen as a form of geoengineering. It needs enormous amounts of land and water. The Land Gap Report, by an international research team, found that the land countries' climate pledges would need for biological carbon removal is almost as large as all the cropland in the world today. That is not realistic.

---

### Challenges Across Technological Approaches

- **Cost**: Costs are high today, and it is unclear how they will come down.
- **Energy demand**: DAC in particular needs large amounts of clean energy that does not yet exist at scale.
- **Standards**: There are no agreed standards yet, and the rules are unclear.
- **Risk of misuse**: Some carbon capture is already used for enhanced oil recovery (pumping CO2 underground to push out more oil), which keeps fossil fuel operations going for longer. There is a real risk that carbon removal becomes an excuse to delay emissions cuts, instead of working alongside them.

---

## Priorities

Carbon removal is necessary. It is also difficult, expensive and far from the scale needed. No single approach comes close to being enough on its own.

The priorities, in order, are:

1. Reduce emissions as fast as possible
2. Protect what already exists, particularly primary forests and peatlands
3. Restore degraded ecosystems carefully and with proper safeguards
4. Invest in technological removal as a complement, not a substitute

Be sceptical of any plan, from a company or a country, that leans heavily on future carbon removal to justify slower action today. The science does not support it, and the amount of removal promised in many net-zero pledges goes well beyond what can be done today.
