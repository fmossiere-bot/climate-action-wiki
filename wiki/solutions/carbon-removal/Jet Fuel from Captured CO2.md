One promising use for captured carbon is turning it into jet fuel. The process combines CO2 with green hydrogen (hydrogen made with renewable electricity) to make what is called a synthetic fuel, or electrofuel. These fuels work in existing aircraft engines, which makes them more practical than switching to a whole new technology, and they can be up to 70% less carbon intensive than ordinary jet fuel.

Aviation is famously hard to decarbonise, and a few alternatives are being explored. Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) is the umbrella term for these options. Today, SAF makes up only around 0.2% of aviation fuel, roughly 300 million litres, though some forecasts suggest it could reach 30 billion litres by 2030.

Not all SAF is the same. Most of what is made today comes from biomass or ethanol, which can compete with food production and sometimes cause emissions of their own elsewhere in the supply chain.

Electrofuels, also called Power-to-Liquid fuels, are seen as easier to scale up because they do not depend on a limited supply of biomass. The process combines captured CO2 with hydrogen to make syngas (a mix of gases), which is then turned into fuel. The problem is the energy cost. Making one litre of electrofuel takes three to four and a half times more energy than making a litre of ordinary fuel. With the amount of clean energy available today, doing this at scale is still a major challenge.

It is a promising direction, but the numbers make clear it is not a quick fix.
