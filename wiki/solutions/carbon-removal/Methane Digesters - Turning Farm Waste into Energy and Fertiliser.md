Livestock farming and organic waste give off a lot of methane as they rot. Over a 20-year period, methane is around 80 times more potent than CO2, which makes it one of the most urgent greenhouse gases to tackle. Methane digesters offer a practical way to deal with it at the source, and they can also make money for farmers.

---

### How They Work

A methane digester, or anaerobic digester, is a sealed tank where microbes break down organic waste, such as animal manure, food waste or sewage sludge, without oxygen. This controlled rotting produces two useful things:

- **Biogas**: a gas that burns and can be used for heating, making electricity or as a vehicle fuel, in place of fossil fuels
- **Digestate**: a nutrient-rich material that can be spread as fertiliser, replacing synthetic fertiliser and improving soil health

The process captures methane that would otherwise escape straight into the atmosphere as the waste rots in the open. By turning that methane into usable energy, digesters turn a climate problem into a resource.

---

### The Potential at Scale

According to Project Drawdown, large methane digesters could be producing between 376 and 395 terawatt-hours of electricity a year by 2050, around 1% of all the electricity generated in the world. Over that period, using them widely could avoid between 6 and 7 gigatons (billion tonnes) of greenhouse gas emissions. The estimated net cost of getting there is between $138 and $182 billion worldwide.

Digesters are already widely used. China, the EU and parts of Southeast Asia have taken them up especially fast in recent decades. They can be installed at dairy farms, pig farms, sewage works and landfills, and they supply electricity or gas either on site or to the grid.

---

### Why This Matters for Farmers

As well as helping the climate, methane digesters give farmers a real extra source of income. A digester on a farm can:

- Make electricity or heat for use on the farm, cutting energy bills
- Supply gas or electricity to the grid, bringing in income
- Produce digestate to use instead of bought fertiliser, cutting costs
- Help handle waste in a way that meets stricter environmental rules

On dairy and pig farms in particular, where managing manure is already a big day-to-day challenge, digesters solve several problems at once. The raw material, animal waste, comes in steady amounts and does not have to be brought in from elsewhere.

The technology is proven and on sale. The main obstacles to wider use are the cost of installing one and the need for the right policy support to make the sums add up for smaller farms.

---

Methane digesters will not solve the methane problem on their own, but they are one of the more practical and affordable tools available, especially for farming. They turn a source of emissions into a source of energy and fertiliser, which is a rare combination among climate solutions.

---

## 2026 Update: The Limits of Digesters

A 2026 New Scientist analysis, citing a study of 98 California dairies published in *Environmental Research Letters*, makes the picture above more complicated. Under controlled conditions, digesters do cut methane from stored manure by 91%. On real farms, things are messier.

**What the study found:**

Installing a digester cut methane from the farm's main sources from 91 kg per hour to 68 kg per hour on average, at two-thirds of the dairies in the study. But the other third saw no improvement, and at some sites leaks were above **1,000 kg per hour**, so those digesters released more methane than they saved. Emissions also jumped for a while during construction, when manure slurry had to be moved and stirred.

**The perverse incentive problem:**

California's Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS) gives farms carbon credits they can sell for biogas made in digesters. One study found that farms getting these payments grew their dairy **herds by 860 cows on average**. In other words, money meant to support digesters has been encouraging factory farms to get bigger. Brent Kim of Johns Hopkins University, who studies food systems, put it this way: "Taxpayer dollars are being used to inflate the value of manure so it begins to compete with the value of milk, and that creates a perverse incentive structure."

**Pollution swapping:**

Digesters speed up the formation of **ammonia** from manure, which raises concerns that they swap one pollutant for another. And if the biogas cannot be sold and is burnt off instead, this can in some cases produce sulphur dioxide, which harms people's lungs.

**The overall verdict:**

Most of the research suggests that well-run digesters, with proper checks for leaks and repairs, can cut methane from manure by roughly **half**. California's alert programme found that 20% of farms with leaks fixed their digesters after being told about them. Researchers such as Rebecca Larson of the University of Wisconsin-Madison call digesters "one of the highest performing mitigation measures we have" for livestock, but only when they are well run.

The bigger open question is whether public money spent on digesters does more good than the same money spent on solar panels or on programmes to reduce livestock numbers. That comparison has not been properly studied.
