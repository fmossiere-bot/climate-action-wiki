# Solar foods and electro-agriculture: making food directly from electricity

## What is it

**Solar foods** and **electro-agriculture (electro-ag)** are a family of emerging technologies that produce food directly from renewable electricity, water, CO2 and nitrogen, rather than from sunlight falling on fields. The simplest version uses electricity to make hydrogen or acetate, feeds those to microbes in vats, and dries the microbes into a high-protein powder. A more ambitious version grows plants on acetate instead of sunlight, so fruits and vegetables could in principle be grown in dark buildings covered in solar panels.

The core efficiency insight: **plants capture less than 1% of the solar energy that reaches them**, while **standard solar panels convert more than 20%** of it into electricity. If you can turn that electricity into food with reasonable efficiency, you need far less land than a conventional farm.

## Why it matters

Agriculture is one of the single largest pressures on climate and biodiversity: it drives most tropical deforestation, uses roughly 70% of fresh water, and generates about a quarter of greenhouse gas emissions. **Even organic farming adds pressure because lower yields per hectare mean more land is cleared somewhere.** A technology that decouples food production from land would ease all of those pressures at once, and electro-ag is one of the first serious candidates. It will not replace fields and farms anytime soon, but even producing a meaningful share of global protein this way would make a real difference, especially in a world that is simultaneously eating more meat and losing crop yields to climate change.

## The three main routes

### 1. Gas fermentation (microbial protein from H2 or methane)

The oldest and most proven route. Electricity splits water to make **hydrogen**, which is fed to naturally-occurring bacteria in vats, along with **CO2 as a carbon source** and **ammonia as a nitrogen source**. The bacteria are dried into a yellow protein powder.

- **Solar Foods (Finland)** — the lead example. Its product **Solein** is up to 80% protein and is already **approved for sale in the US and Singapore**, with EU approval pending (five years and counting). CEO Pasi Vainikka says the demonstrator factory is "five times more productive than we thought early on". Second factory planned at **6,400 tonnes a year**.
- **Jooules, Air Protein, Aerbio** — other start-ups chasing the same approach.
- **Already scaled historically**: Norwegian company **Norferm** produced **110,000 tonnes** of microbial protein from methane between 1999 and 2005 (sold as fish food). The programme stopped because protein prices were too low, not because the technology failed.
- **Scaled now, but on fossil methane**: US firm **Calysta** operates a **20,000 tonnes/year factory in China**; Danish **Unibio** is building a **50,000 tonnes/year plant in Saudi Arabia**. Both currently use fossil-fuel methane as feedstock. That makes the land case strong but puts the fossil carbon back in the atmosphere. Calysta says it is looking at biogas instead.

### 2. Acetate-based microbes (and acetate-grown plants)

A newer route that gets around a chemistry problem: **hydrogen barely dissolves in water, so microbes struggle to use it**. **Acetate** is highly soluble, which allows faster growth, at the cost of needing a bit more energy to make.

- **UC Riverside / Robert Jinkerson (2022)**: showed that solar → acetate → yeast is **about 18 times more efficient than standard farming** at turning sunlight into food. Nearly 4× for algae.
- **A 2024 follow-up paper**: with further improvements, this efficiency could rise another **tenfold** — meaning around **10× as much food per hectare** as conventional agriculture.
- **Acetate Consortium (University of Copenhagen, Remko Boom)**: works with microbes already approved for food use, such as yeast, to avoid the slow novel-food approval process. Says "we can decouple this type of food production from the use of land."
- **Nolux**: Jinkerson spin-out company genetically modifying plants to grow on acetate in the dark. The problem to solve: plants can only use acetate efficiently through a chemical pathway that is active in seedlings. If this can be unlocked in mature plants, the long-term vision is **growing fruits and vegetables in buildings covered in solar panels** — the electricity makes acetate on site, the plants grow in the dark below.

### 3. Chemical synthesis of specific food components

A completely different approach: skip living cells altogether and build food molecules directly.

- **Savor** (US start-up): converts **syngas** (a mix of hydrogen and carbon monoxide) into **edible fats**, producing a butter-like product. Currently uses fossil-derived syngas, but the gases could be made from renewable electricity instead.
- **GM microbes making specific proteins** (e.g. casein found in cheese) — more expensive than growing whole cells, so best for high-value products.

## Why vertical farming is not the answer

A common alternative is **vertical farming**: growing conventional crops in closed buildings under artificial lights. Michael Le Page (New Scientist) and others argue this cannot work at scale because of the same photosynthesis-efficiency problem:

- Every hectare of vertical growing area needs **about 13 hectares of solar panels** to power the lights
- So overall land use per unit of food goes **up**, not down
- Company after company in the sector has gone bust

Solar foods sidestep this by cutting plants out of the energy-capture step entirely.

## The honest caveats

- **This will not replace conventional farming anytime soon.** It would require far more renewable electricity than we currently generate. The question is whether it can produce a meaningful share.
- **Regulatory approval is painfully slow.** Solar Foods has been waiting five years for EU novel-food approval of Solein. The Acetate Consortium picked already-approved microbes (yeast) specifically to dodge this.
- **The gas-fermentation industry currently runs on fossil methane.** The climate case only works if those feedstocks switch to green hydrogen or biogas.
- **Culture-medium footprint.** Plant-cell approaches (such as cultured chocolate or coffee) still use sugars from conventional crops in their growth media. Replacing those sugars with electricity-derived ingredients is a separate problem to solve.
- **Consumer acceptance is uncertain.** Even if the technology works and the regulators approve, people have to want to buy it. Taste is one of the biggest remaining engineering problems.

## Where this fits in the food-transition story

Solar foods are not a replacement for cutting meat and dairy demand, nor for scaling plant-based diets. They are a potential **third track** alongside:

- **Reducing demand** for the most land-intensive foods ([beef and climate change](../../sectors/agriculture-food/beef-and-climate-change.md), [EAT-Lancet planetary diet](../../sectors/agriculture-food/eat-lancet-planetary-diet-2025.md))
- **Shifting supply** to plant proteins ([not-enough-land-for-plant-based](../../myths/not-enough-land-for-plant-based.md), [meat-substitutes-too-expensive](../../myths/meat-substitutes-too-expensive.md))
- **Decoupling production from land** entirely (electro-ag and gas fermentation)

If any serious share of global protein can be produced this way in the next few decades, it opens up large tracts of farmland for rewilding and takes pressure off the climate-biodiversity-food nexus flagged in the [IPBES Nexus Assessment](../../sources/ipbes-nexus-assessment-2024.md).
