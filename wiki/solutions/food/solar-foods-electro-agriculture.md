# Solar foods and electro-agriculture: making food directly from electricity

## What is it

**Solar foods** and electro-agriculture (electro-ag) are a family of new technologies that make food directly from renewable electricity, water, CO2 and nitrogen, instead of from sunlight falling on fields. The simplest version uses electricity to make hydrogen or acetate, feeds that to microbes in vats, and dries the microbes into a high-protein powder. A more ambitious version grows plants on acetate instead of sunlight, so fruit and vegetables could in principle be grown in dark buildings covered in solar panels.

The idea rests on efficiency. Plants capture **less than 1%** of the solar energy that reaches them, while standard solar panels turn more than 20% of it into electricity. If that electricity can be turned into food reasonably efficiently, you need far less land than a conventional farm does.

## Why it matters

Agriculture is one of the largest single pressures on climate and nature. It drives most tropical deforestation, uses roughly 70% of fresh water and produces about a quarter of greenhouse gas emissions. Even organic farming adds to the pressure, because lower yields per hectare mean more land gets cleared somewhere else. A technology that separates food production from land would ease all of these pressures at once, and electro-ag is one of the first serious candidates. It will not replace fields and farms any time soon. But producing a meaningful share of the world's protein this way would make a real difference, in a world that is eating more meat while losing crop yields to climate change.

## The three main routes

### 1. Gas fermentation (microbial protein from H2 or methane)

This is the oldest and best-proven route. Electricity splits water to make hydrogen, which is fed to naturally occurring bacteria in vats, along with CO2 as a carbon source and ammonia as a nitrogen source. The bacteria are then dried into a yellow protein powder.

- **Solar Foods (Finland)** is the lead example. Its product Solein is up to 80% protein and is already approved for sale in the US and Singapore, with EU approval still pending after five years. CEO Pasi Vainikka says the demonstrator factory is "five times more productive than we thought early on". A second factory is planned at 6,400 tonnes a year.
- Jooules, Air Protein and Aerbio are other start-ups taking the same approach.
- It has been done at scale before. Norwegian company Norferm produced 110,000 tonnes of microbial protein from methane between 1999 and 2005, sold as fish food. The programme stopped because protein prices were too low, not because the technology failed.
- It is being done at scale now, but on fossil methane. US firm Calysta runs a 20,000 tonnes/year factory in China, and Danish Unibio is building a 50,000 tonnes/year plant in Saudi Arabia. Both use fossil-fuel methane as feedstock. That gives a strong case on land use but puts the fossil carbon back into the atmosphere. Calysta says it is looking at biogas instead.

### 2. Acetate-based microbes (and acetate-grown plants)

This newer route gets round a chemistry problem: hydrogen barely dissolves in water, so microbes struggle to use it. **Acetate** dissolves easily, which allows faster growth, though it takes a little more energy to make.

- UC Riverside, Robert Jinkerson (2022): showed that solar to acetate to yeast is about 18 times more efficient than standard farming at turning sunlight into food, and nearly 4× more efficient for algae.
- A 2024 follow-up paper: with further improvements, this efficiency could rise another tenfold, which would mean around 10× as much food per hectare as conventional agriculture.
- Acetate Consortium (University of Copenhagen, Remko Boom): works with microbes already approved for food, such as yeast, to avoid the slow novel-food approval process. It says "we can decouple this type of food production from the use of land."
- Nolux: a spin-out company from Jinkerson's lab that is genetically modifying plants to grow on acetate in the dark. The difficulty is that plants can only use acetate efficiently through a chemical pathway that is active in seedlings. If it can be switched on in mature plants, the long-term vision is growing fruit and vegetables in buildings covered in solar panels. The electricity would make acetate on site, and the plants would grow in the dark below.

### 3. Chemical synthesis of specific food components

This is a completely different approach: skip living cells altogether and build food molecules directly.

- Savor (US start-up) converts **syngas** (a mix of hydrogen and carbon monoxide) into edible fats, producing a butter-like product. It currently uses fossil-derived syngas, but the gases could be made from renewable electricity instead.
- GM microbes can make specific proteins, such as the casein found in cheese. This costs more than growing whole cells, so it suits high-value products best.

## Why vertical farming is not the answer

A common alternative is **vertical farming**: growing ordinary crops in closed buildings under artificial lights. Michael Le Page (New Scientist) and others argue it cannot work at scale, because it runs into the same photosynthesis-efficiency problem:

- Every hectare of vertical growing area needs about 13 hectares of solar panels to power the lights
- So overall land use per unit of food goes up, not down
- Company after company in the sector has gone bust

Solar foods avoid this by taking plants out of the energy-capture step altogether.

## The caveats

- It will not replace conventional farming any time soon. That would need far more renewable electricity than the world currently generates. The real question is whether it can produce a meaningful share.
- Regulatory approval is very slow. Solar Foods has been waiting five years for EU novel-food approval of Solein. The Acetate Consortium chose already-approved microbes (yeast) specifically to avoid this.
- The gas-fermentation industry currently runs on fossil methane. The climate case only works if those feedstocks switch to green hydrogen or biogas.
- The culture medium has its own footprint. Plant-cell approaches (such as cultured chocolate or coffee) still use sugars from conventional crops in their growth media. Replacing those sugars with ingredients made from electricity is a separate problem.
- Nobody knows yet whether people will buy it. Even if the technology works and the regulators approve it, people have to want to eat it, and taste is one of the biggest problems still to solve.

## Where this fits in the food transition

Solar foods do not replace cutting meat and dairy demand, or scaling up plant-based diets. They are a possible **third track** alongside them:

- Reducing demand for the most land-hungry foods ([beef and climate change](../../sectors/agriculture-food/beef-and-climate-change.md), [EAT-Lancet planetary diet](../../sectors/agriculture-food/eat-lancet-planetary-diet-2025.md))
- Shifting supply to plant proteins ([not-enough-land-for-plant-based](../../myths/not-enough-land-for-plant-based.md), [meat-substitutes-too-expensive](../../myths/meat-substitutes-too-expensive.md))
- Separating production from land entirely (electro-ag and gas fermentation)

If a serious share of global protein can be produced this way in the next few decades, large areas of farmland could be freed for rewilding. That would take pressure off the links between climate, biodiversity and food described in the [IPBES Nexus Assessment](../../sources/ipbes-nexus-assessment-2024.md).
