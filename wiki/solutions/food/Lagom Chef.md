Food waste is one of the less visible parts of the climate problem, but one of the biggest. Around 30 percent of all food produced globally is wasted each year, according to WRAP, the UK-based resource and waste charity. Food loss and waste are responsible for 8 to 10 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions, roughly five times the emissions of the entire aviation sector, according to figures from the UN and the IPCC. All this happens while hundreds of millions of people worldwide still face hunger or food insecurity.

That makes cutting food waste one of the more direct things an individual can do about climate change, and it is closely tied to food justice too. This is the space that chef and content creator Martyn Odell, known online as Lagom Chef, has built his brand around.

## 2. Who is Lagom Chef

Martyn Odell has worked as a chef since the age of 15, in restaurants, street food, cookery schools and recipe development. Instead of staying in professional kitchens, he moved into short, fast-paced cooking videos. He uses humour and an informal style to make food waste feel like an everyday, practical topic rather than a moral lecture. His stated aim is to get past the idea that avoiding food waste is a "hippy" message about sustainability, and to present it as simple, useful cooking advice.

His approach rests on one idea, which he sums up in his own words: eat the food you buy. If people cook and use what they already have at home before it goes off, waste is avoided almost automatically. No specialist knowledge or equipment is needed.

## 3. Simplicity over perfection

A theme that comes up again and again in Odell's content is that cooking has become too tied to getting things perfectly right. His advice is not to overthink it. He argues that people do not need advanced technique to cook well, and that personal taste counts: a simple plate of beans on toast with Marmite is as valid as anything more elaborate.

This matters for food waste in particular. A lot of food gets thrown away when people feel they lack the "correct" recipe or ingredient to use it up. Odell's content focuses instead on using what is already in the fridge or cupboard, adapting recipes rather than following them to the letter, and treating small amounts of leftovers as a starting point rather than a problem.

## 4. His debut book and platform

Odell's debut cookbook, Eat The Food You Buy: 15 Everyday Ingredients, Endless Delicious Meals, is published by Penguin Life on 16 July 2026. It turns his philosophy into a structured set of recipes built around a small number of everyday ingredients. The idea is that each ingredient can be used in many different ways, instead of being bought for a single dish and then forgotten.

Alongside the book, he runs a YouTube channel and a newsletter under the same "Eat The Food You Buy" name, in the same style: short, practical and deliberately unpretentious.

## 5. The person behind the brand

In a recent podcast conversation about ADHD and creativity, Odell talked about being diagnosed with ADHD while writing his first cookbook. Growing up in professional kitchens, where pressure and intensity are often the norm, made it harder for him to tell his personality apart from his coping mechanisms and from traits linked to neurodivergence. He also talked about the pressure of constantly turning his personal life and creativity into social media content, and how cooking has helped him reconnect with himself.

This background shapes his brand. Odell presents cutting food waste as something built on permission and flexibility, rather than as a rigid set of rules, and that approach seems to reflect how he has come to understand his own way of working.

## Why his approach works

Martyn Odell's Lagom Chef brand takes a serious issue, food waste, and makes it practical and approachable instead of preachy. His central message, eat the food you buy, is simple enough to act on straight away. It also connects to the bigger picture: less food wasted at home means fewer emissions from food that was grown, transported and refrigerated for nothing, and more food left for people who need it. His work suggests that people are more likely to change their habits on sustainability when the message is about everyday practicality rather than guilt.
