Food waste is one of the less visible but most significant parts of the climate problem. Around 30 percent of all food produced globally is wasted each year, according to WRAP, the UK based resource and waste charity. At the same time, food loss and waste are responsible for 8 to 10 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions, roughly five times the emissions from the entire aviation sector, according to figures from the UN and the IPCC. This waste happens while hundreds of millions of people worldwide still face hunger or food insecurity.

Reducing food waste is therefore one of the more direct actions individuals can take against climate change, alongside its clear link to food justice. This is the space that chef and content creator Martyn Odell, known online as Lagom Chef, has built his brand around.

## 2. Who is Lagom Chef

Martyn Odell has worked as a chef since the age of 15, across restaurants, street food, cookery schools and recipe development. Rather than staying in professional kitchens, he moved into short, fast paced cooking videos, using humour and an informal style to make food waste feel like an everyday, practical topic rather than a moralising one. His stated aim is to break down the idea that avoiding food waste is a "hippy" message about sustainability, and instead present it as simple, useful cooking advice.

His approach is built on one central idea, summed up in his own words as: eat the food you buy. The philosophy is straightforward. If people cook and use what they already have at home, rather than letting it go off, waste is avoided almost automatically, without needing any specialist knowledge or equipment.

## 3. Simplicity over perfection

A recurring theme in Odell's content is that cooking has become too tied to the idea of getting it perfectly right. His guiding thought is not to overthink it. He argues that people do not need advanced technique to cook well, and that personal taste, such as a simple plate of beans on toast with Marmite, is just as valid as more elaborate cooking.

This matters for food waste specifically, because a lot of food ends up thrown away when people feel they lack the "correct" recipe or ingredient to use something up. Odell's content instead focuses on using what is already in the fridge or cupboard, adapting recipes rather than following them exactly, and treating small amounts of leftover food as a starting point rather than a problem.

## 4. His debut book and platform

Odell's debut cookbook, Eat The Food You Buy: 15 Everyday Ingredients, Endless Delicious Meals, is published by Penguin Life on 16 July 2026. The book takes his central philosophy and builds it into a structured set of recipes based around a small number of everyday ingredients, designed to be used in many different ways rather than bought for a single dish and then forgotten.

Alongside the book, he runs a YouTube channel and a newsletter under the same "Eat The Food You Buy" name, continuing the same approach: short, practical, and deliberately unpretentious.

## 5. The person behind the brand

In a recent conversation on a podcast covering ADHD and creativity, Odell spoke about being diagnosed with ADHD while writing his first cookbook, and how growing up in professional kitchens, an environment often defined by pressure and intensity, made it harder for him to separate his personality from coping mechanisms and from traits linked to neurodivergence. He also discussed the pressure of constantly turning personal life and creativity into content for social media, and how cooking has helped him reconnect with himself.

This background is relevant to his brand, since Odell frames food waste reduction not as a rigid set of rules, but as something rooted in permission and flexibility, an approach that appears to reflect how he has come to understand his own way of working.

## Bottom line

Martyn Odell's Lagom Chef brand takes a genuinely important issue, food waste, and translates it into something practical and approachable rather than preachy. His central message, eat the food you buy, is simple enough to apply immediately, and it connects directly to the wider picture: less food wasted at home means fewer emissions from food that was grown, transported and refrigerated for nothing, and more food available for people who need it. His approach shows that behaviour change on sustainability topics can work well when it is framed around everyday practicality rather than guilt.
