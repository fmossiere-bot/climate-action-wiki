## Key facts at a glance

The main numbers first:

- Agriculture produces about **12 Gt CO₂-equivalent per year**, around 21% of all human-caused greenhouse gas emissions. Animal agriculture accounts for more than half of that.
- Ruminant animals (cud-chewing animals like cattle, sheep and goats) produce methane, a greenhouse gas with 80 times the warming potential of CO₂ in the near term.
- Eating less ruminant meat could cut between 1.4 and 2.8 Gt CO₂-equivalent per year, which makes it one of the fastest and most achievable climate solutions available today.
- A widespread shift to plant-rich diets could prevent between about 5 million and 11 million deaths a year, depending on the study and how far diets change.
- Animal agriculture currently uses 80% of all agricultural land and 70% of global freshwater.

What we eat, and in particular how much red meat we eat, is one of the biggest levers any individual has on climate change.

- A widespread transition to plant-rich diets could prevent between about 5 million deaths a year by 2050 (Springmann and colleagues, 2016) and about 11 million a year (EAT-Lancet Commission, 2019), depending on how far diets change.
- Plant-rich diets can improve health for people who eat too much red meat and do not depend on meat as a key source of nutrients.
- Plant-rich diets are linked to better nutrition and more food security, which can reduce heart and circulatory disease, nutrient deficiency and obesity, and improve mental health.
- Eating less animal-based food can reduce how much land and water farming needs.


> By switching to a mostly plant based diet, you can:
> - Decrease emissions and slow deforestation by reducing meat demand
> - Improve your health by lowering the risk of heart disease, diabetes, obesity, and high blood pressure
> - Save money on food and healthcare costs

## Animal Agriculture Is a Major Driver of Climate Change

Ruminant animals produce methane through a digestive process called enteric fermentation. Clearing forests and grasslands for pastures and cropland to feed livestock also emits CO₂, while livestock manure releases both methane and nitrous oxide.

Ruminants make up 30% of food-related emissions but provide only 5% of the calories people eat worldwide. In other words, a huge amount of resources and emissions goes into a fairly small share of our food.

> **If cattle were a country, they would rank as the third largest emitter of greenhouse gases in the world**. Beef produces around 5 times more emissions than chicken, and up to 60 times more than legumes.

The EAT-Lancet Commission, an international team of scientists, estimated that red meat (beef, lamb and pork) should ideally be limited to around 14 grams per day per person, or roughly 5 kg per person per year. Most people in high-income countries eat far more than that.

## Animal Agriculture Depletes Natural Resources

### Land and Water

Animal agriculture accounts for 80% of all agricultural land use and uses 70% of global freshwater. In the United States, livestock production is the largest user of freshwater, and **producing 1 kg of animal protein uses 100 times more water** than producing 1 kg of grain protein.

By 2050, nearly half of the world's population is expected to live in water-stressed areas, in part as a direct result of food animal production. Each year, approximately 13 million hectares of forest, which wildlife and carbon storage depend on, are cleared for agriculture, mainly to support meat production.

In the European Union, research has estimated that halving consumption of meat, dairy and eggs would cut cropland use per person by 23%.

### Pollution and Emissions

The environmental footprint of animal farming goes well beyond land and water. Water running off farms carries nutrients, antibiotics and other contaminants into rivers, lakes and coastal areas, creating dead zones where little aquatic life can survive.

Livestock production contributes 7.1 billion tonnes of CO₂-equivalent greenhouse gases, around **14.5% of global emissions**. Methane from animal agriculture warms the planet many times more than CO₂ over a 20-year period.

### Biodiversity Loss

Expanding farmland for livestock is a major driver of deforestation, which harms wildlife by damaging habitats and breaking them into smaller, isolated patches. Livestock farming can make landscapes more uniform and contribute to the loss of large carnivores, herbivores and birds. Since 1970, monitored wildlife populations have fallen by an average of 73%, according to WWF's Living Planet Index, and habitat loss driven mainly by farming is the biggest threat. This is an average fall in population sizes, not a loss of 73% of species.

## Plant-Rich Diets Improve Health

The health case for eating less meat is well established and getting stronger.

Diets high in red meat have been linked to increased risk of overall mortality, cancer, cardiovascular disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, colorectal cancer, and weight gain. Diets that include other sources of protein, such as fish, poultry, nuts, legumes, low-fat dairy and whole grains, are associated with a lower risk of death.

> Vegetarians in one study had a 24% lower rate of ischaemic heart disease deaths compared to non-vegetarians. A shift to vegan or vegetarian diets could reduce global mortality by 6–10% by 2050.

People who follow plant-based diets tend to have lower body weight, blood pressure, blood sugar levels, and cholesterol. There is also evidence that plant-based eating can reduce the number of medications needed to manage chronic conditions.

## It Also Makes Financial Sense

Plant-rich diets are better for the planet and for your health, and they can save money too.

> Diets that favour plant foods over animal products are up to 29% less expensive than diets heavy in meat. One study found grocery savings of around €1.60 per day on meat, and €0.65 per day on dairy. Add in savings from eating out, and the numbers add up quickly.

Research on the economic impact of plant-based eating in the UK found that if just 10% of the population shifted toward a diet high in vegetables and low in red meat, societal savings could reach £7.83 billion over two decades, thanks to lower healthcare costs and less spending on repairing environmental damage.

## Where to start

You do not need to become fully vegan overnight. Moving away from ruminant meat can happen gradually, through small changes in habit that, added up across many people, make a big dent in global greenhouse gas emissions.

Project Drawdown classifies improving diets as an "emergency brake" climate solution, meaning it can bring climate benefits quickly and should be treated as a high priority.

Some practical starting points:

- Limit red meat to a few servings per week, working towards the EAT-Lancet guideline of 14 grams a day, roughly one 100 g serving a week.
- Build meals around plant-based proteins. Lentils, chickpeas, black beans, tofu, tempeh and quinoa are all filling, affordable and low in emissions.
- When eating out, check the menu in advance and look for plant-based or fish options. If nothing fits, pick the least meat-heavy dish.
- Use herbs and spices generously. Cumin, coriander, smoked paprika, rosemary and garlic can turn simple vegetables and legumes into tasty, filling meals.
- Shop seasonally and locally where possible. Seasonal produce tends to be cheaper, fresher and lower in emissions.



# Calculating the impact of a plant-rich diet
These calculations show how Project Drawdown worked out the impact of a better, plant-rich diet as a climate solution.


> Using country-level data from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, Project Drawdown scientists estimated the growth in global food consumption by 2050, assuming that people in lower-income countries will consume more food and higher quantities of meat as economies grow. If 50–75 percent of people adopt a healthy diet of an average 2,300 calories per day and reduce meat consumption overall, they estimate at least 54.19–78.48 gigatons of emissions could be avoided from dietary change alone. Since agriculture, particularly for cattle and animal feed production, is the leading driver of tropical deforestation, reducing meat consumption can avoid additional forest loss and associated greenhouse gas emissions.
