# Summary
This page looks at the part hedgerows play in supporting wildlife and limiting climate change. Hedgerows have a central place in the EU's land-use strategy, and the page looks in particular at the situation in Ireland and France.

> Precious allies in the fight against climate change, hedgerows are now better protected and valued, with incentives for replanting. But uprooting continues.


# Why are hedgerows so important?
Planting hedgerows and trees in Irish grasslands brings benefits for the environment, for wildlife and for farming:

### 1. Carbon sequestration and climate mitigation

- Irish hedgerows store on average about 58 tonnes of carbon per hectare in their above-ground growth, according to Teagasc, and wider, taller hedges hold much more than tightly trimmed ones. That makes them useful natural carbon sinks on farms
- Trees absorb CO2 and store the carbon in their wood, leaves, roots and the soil, which helps limit climate change


### 2. Biodiversity

- Hedgerows give many native plants and animals, including birds, bats, insects and small mammals, a place to live, feed, shelter, nest and hibernate
- They act as wildlife corridors, letting animals move between habitats and keeping populations from becoming isolated
- A single mature tree can support hundreds of species, a big boost for local wildlife

### 3. Soil and water quality

- The roots of hedgerows and trees hold soil in place, which reduces erosion and stops soil washing into streams and rivers
- They help filter out pollutants and excess nutrients, improving water quality in nearby rivers and aquifers (underground water stores)
- Hedgerows slow the flow of water across the land, which helps control flooding

### 4. Benefits for farming

- Hedgerows shelter livestock and crops and cut wind speed by up to 50%, which helps prevent heat stress in animals and damage to crops
- If well managed, they make cheap, long-lasting barriers that keep livestock in
- Shelter from hedgerows can improve animal welfare, especially in harsh weather and during lambing

### 5. Cultural and landscape value

- Hedgerows are part of Ireland’s cultural and historical heritage. They often mark ancient boundaries and give rural landscapes their particular character
- They make the countryside more attractive and provide natural screening and privacy

### 6. Human wellbeing

- Spending time around trees and hedgerows lowers stress and is good for mental wellbeing
- Trees and hedgerows can raise property values and make rural and urban areas more pleasant to look at

Put together, planting hedgerows and trees in Irish grasslands helps capture carbon and supports wildlife, healthy soil and water, farm productivity, cultural heritage and people's wellbeing. That makes hedgerows one of the main nature-based solutions for helping Ireland's environment cope with climate change.


# What is the situation and plan in Ireland

> Climate Action Plan 2024 has a target to plant 40,000km of new hedgerows and improve management on 75,000km of existing hedgerows. However, there's been concern that this isn't being implemented fast enough with proper training and financial support for farmers.

Current laws: the Wildlife Act already protects hedgerows from cutting between 1st March and 31st August (to protect nesting birds), but there are many exemptions for agriculture, so the protection is not very strong.

> The situation: Ireland has about 689,000km of hedgerows, but they're being removed at an alarming rate. Less than one third of remaining hedgerows are in good condition. The Irish Government's Climate Action Plan explicitly integrates hedgerows into its broader goals to reduce emissions and improve land use.

### How hedgerows are cut matters as much as whether they exist

Hedgerows Ireland (via the Farmers Journal, August 2026) warns about two trends: **annual flail-cutting of every farm hedge to the same low height**, and heavy-duty mulching heads on tracked machines that cut mature, tall hedges down to stumps. Both are thinning hedges out and shortening their lives. Cutting to the same height every year damages the growing points and thins the base of the hedge. Mulching heads shatter stems and let rot and disease in. In heatwaves, including the 2026 European summer, hedgerows give livestock the shade and shelter that prevents heat stress. So how hedges are managed is now a question of farm income as well as conservation.

Hedgerows Ireland's practical recommendations:

- Draw up a management plan for your farm's hedges covering several years
- Identify historically significant hedges (townland or barony boundaries, double ditches) and treat them as irreplaceable
- Let some hedges grow into treelines. They may never need cutting, or only side-cutting for access
- Cut some hedges on a two- or three-year rotation, so more plants can flower and fruit
- For hedges cut every year, raise the cutting height by a few centimetres each year to avoid the damaging "knuckle" of regrowth at the cut
- Leave the odd bush to grow into a tree at intervals along the hedge
- Put off cutting as long as you can, ideally to late winter, so birds and other wildlife have food and shelter
- Do not use mulching heads: "they have no place in hedge management"
- Manage hedges on a 20-40 year rejuvenation cycle, using coppicing (cutting to the base to regrow) or hedge-laying
- Leave veteran hedges alone, with as little intervention as possible

In intensively farmed landscapes, two-thirds of Ireland's songbird species feed and breed in hedgerows. After peatland, hedgerow management is one of the biggest levers Ireland has for nature right now.

# Hedgerows in EU climate and biodiversity policy

### The EU policy framework

The main EU policies that cover hedgerows are the **Biodiversity Strategy for 2030** and the **Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) 2023–27**, both part of the European Green Deal.

Key EU targets:

- At least **10% of agricultural land** across the EU should be under "high-diversity landscape features", a category that explicitly includes hedgerows, along with flower strips, fallow land, ponds and fruit trees.
- The Biodiversity Strategy also calls for restoring 25,000 km of rivers to a free-flowing state, increasing organic farming, reducing pesticide use by 50% by 2030, and planting 3 billion trees by 2030.
- The EU also has a target for net greenhouse gas removals of 310 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent by 2030, and nature restoration, including hedgerows, helps reach it.

There is no single EU-level target expressed in km of hedgerows specifically, but hedgerows are explicitly included in the landscape features target and supported through the CAP and the Nature Restoration Law (which entered into force in 2024).

# What about France in particular

### The scale of the loss

France has a long history of bocage landscape (the French word for hedgerow countryside), but it has suffered heavy damage since the mid-20th century. About 70% of France's hedgerows have disappeared since 1950, mainly because small fields were merged into larger ones, leaving around 750,000 km today.

More recently, losses have been running at around 23,500 km per year, a rate six times higher than what is being replanted each year. This pace has doubled compared to the 2006–2014 period.

The French biodiversity agency (OFB) estimates that only about 30% of the hedgerows that existed at their peak remain today.

### Why they matter in France specifically

Hedgerows are seen as a central part of France's shift towards farming that works with nature (the agro-ecological transition). They improve agricultural production, help tackle climate change, store carbon, clean water and reduce soil erosion.

Specifically, hedgerows filter runoff water, store organic carbon in the soil, protect against erosion and flooding, and serve as wildlife corridors for bats, insects and mammals.

The northwest of France is the heartland of bocage, with the highest hedgerow density:

- **61,813 km** in Manche (Normandy)
- **53,152 km** in Finistère (Brittany)
- **49,602 km** in Aveyron (South)

A turbulent history:

|Period|What happened|
|---|---|
|19th century|Farmers plant hedgerows to mark boundaries, contain livestock and produce firewood|
|1900–1950|Bocage reaches its peak|
|1950–1999|Massive uprooting as fields were merged and livestock farming declined: **70% of bocage hedgerows disappear**|
|2017–2021|23,500 km lost per year, while only **3,000 km are replanted**|

> **Today: only about 750,000 km of hedgerows remain in France.**

### What France has put in place

France has been quite active here, with several overlapping programmes:

**Pacte en faveur de la haie (Hedgerow Pact)**, launched in September 2023 as part of the _France Nation Verte_ plan:

- It sets a target of a **net gain of 50,000 km of hedgerows** between 2024 and 2030, building on an earlier "Plantons des haies" programme from the post-Covid recovery plan.
- The ambition is to protect, plant, manage and add economic value to hedgerows, through a coordinated effort involving all relevant actors.

Longer-term target: the agricultural orientation law (LOA) sets a more ambitious long-term goal of **500,000 km of sustainably managed hedgerows by 2048**, with the 50,000 km net gain by 2030 as a first step. A national observatory involving IGN (mapping agency), OFB (biodiversity agency) and INRAE (agricultural research institute) is being set up to track progress.

Carbon credits and finance: some areas are experimenting with local carbon markets, where farmers are paid for the carbon stored in their hedgerows. Mature multi-layered hedgerows can store up to 3 tonnes of CO₂ per km per year.

Label Haie (Hedgerow Label): France has also developed a national certification scheme for good hedgerow management practices and the supply chains for wood coming from bocage.
