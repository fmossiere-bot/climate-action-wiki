# Summary
Bamboo plantations do a lot for the land around them. Their wide-spreading underground stems hold the soil, which reduces erosion and stops sediment washing into streams and rivers. Fallen leaves carpet the ground and cut evaporation. The hollow stems hold water into the dry season, which keeps the surrounding air more humid. Dense bamboo can even catch water droplets from fog.

Bamboo Production is one of the solutions put forward by Project Drawdown because:
- Bamboo quickly stores carbon in its stems, leaves, roots and the soil, and it can thrive on degraded land. Long-lived bamboo products can keep that carbon stored over time.
- Bamboo is one of the fastest-growing plants and absorbs carbon quickly. It has thousands of uses, from buildings to food and paper (or socks). It is strong for its weight, but its average tensile strength is less than half that of steel, so it cannot simply replace steel in structures. After being cut, bamboo resprouts and grows again.

> **Bamboo Sequestration Model**
> Bamboo production can sequester 2.03 metric tons of carbon per hectare per year, based on 12 data points from four sources. This value includes carbon sequestered in long-lived products from harvested bamboo. Because the productive lifespan of a bamboo planting is 75–100 years, we didn’t model emissions from replanting.

Bamboo is not a cure-all, though. Here is where it does have real potential.

### Where bamboo has potential

**Fast carbon capture**  
Bamboo is one of the fastest-growing plants on earth. In the early years, some species capture carbon 2–3 times faster per hectare than many tree species. For countries that need quick progress on carbon, that matters.

> **La Bambouseraie en Cévennes: 170 years of bamboo heritage**
> Founded in 1856 by botanist Eugène Mazel, who spent his personal fortune importing exotic species from Asia and the Americas, La Bambouseraie in the Cévennes region of southern France is celebrating its 170th anniversary this year. The site, open to the public, now hosts more than 230 bamboo varieties.
> 
> Director Valentine Crouzet notes that visitors see the place as a symbol of preservation, even when they are unaware of its ecological value. She points out that bamboo's environmental qualities remain widely underestimated: **few people know that bamboo absorbs 30 to 40% more CO2 than European broadleaf forests, while releasing around 30% more oxygen**. As climate pressures grow, raising awareness of bamboo as a strategic climate ally remains an ongoing challenge.


**Long-term storage through products**  
This is the strongest argument. When bamboo is harvested and turned into durable products, such as flooring, building materials and furniture, the carbon stays locked in for decades. Bamboo left to rot releases its carbon again quickly, so using it in long-lived products keeps the carbon stored for much longer.

**Degraded land, not forest**  
On badly degraded land with little wildlife left, such as abandoned farmland or eroded hillsides, bamboo can be a sensible choice. It does not push out existing ecosystems, and it improves the land.

## Growing Usage
Bamboo is becoming a credible alternative to wood, plastic, concrete and even carbon fibre. It is used across a wide range of industries: construction and insulation, furniture and interior design, paper and packaging, food and cosmetics (it is rich in silica), biofuels and biomaterials, bicycles, composite materials for automotive and aerospace, and sailboat construction. Because it is strong for its weight, it has earned the nickname "green steel", although tests show its average tensile strength is less than half that of steel. Bamboo is a grass, not a tree, and it can be harvested every year from the fifth year after planting, which makes it a fast, renewable source of raw material.

## Where does it grow?
Bamboo forests cover an estimated 37 million hectares globally. China leads the way: the city of Chishui alone has 90,000 hectares under cultivation, generating nearly €1 billion in annual production value. India, Colombia, Vietnam, Indonesia and several African and South American countries are also developing bamboo industries. In Europe, France is becoming a serious player: the start-up Horizom already manages 460 hectares across 34 departments, with plans to develop 50,000 hectares by 2050. La Bambouseraie en Cévennes, founded in 1856, hosts over 230 varieties and is a longstanding reference in Europe.

## Innovation
Several projects show what bamboo can do technically. In France, Cobratex manufactures biosourced bamboo composites for use in aviation, automotive and leisure sectors. Navigator Marc Thiercelin is working with Cobratex on a bamboo, wood, hemp and carbon sailboat for the 2028 Vendée Globe, cutting carbon footprint by 70% and cost by 50% compared to a standard carbon racing yacht. In Indonesia, entrepreneur Arief Rabik runs the Bamboo Village Trust, planting bamboo on degraded tropical land to restore soils, store CO2 and build local economies. In November 2024, French company Fiboo opened a bamboo fibre insulation panel factory in the north of France, targeting 10,000 tonnes per year from 2028, when low-carbon construction materials become mandatory.

## A note on fashion
Bamboo is turning up more and more in fashion, including luxury fashion: French designer Laure Julien worked with bamboo for Dior's summer 2025 collection. She also gives a clear warning. Most bamboo textiles on the market are viscose, made with a heavy chemical process that largely cancels out the plant's environmental benefits. The better alternative is mechanically processed bamboo linen, made without toxic solvents, but it is rarer and more expensive. Her conclusion is that bamboo should be used where it makes ecological sense, and never as a marketing tool.

## 2026 update: engineering bamboo for permanent buildings

In January 2026 the **Institution of Structural Engineers** (IStructE) published a new **manual for designing permanent buildings from bamboo**, based on the ISO 22156 standard. The lead author is **David Trujillo**, assistant professor in humanitarian engineering at the University of Warwick. The manual is a serious attempt to move bamboo out of "wellness spa and eco-lodge" territory and into mainstream construction. Construction was responsible for **around one-third of global carbon emissions** in 2022, and more than half of that came from cement.

### The buildings that already exist

Engineered bamboo already exists in real buildings. The manual highlights a growing list of projects:

- Terminal 2 of Kempegowda International Airport, Bengaluru: bamboo tubes make up the ceiling and pillars
- Ninghai bamboo tower, north-east China: over 20 metres tall, claimed as the world's first engineered-bamboo high-rise
- The Arc at Green School, Bali: an arc-shaped gymnasium engineered by UK firm Atelier One
- Composite bamboo shear walls (walls that brace a building against sideways forces), now used for earthquake-resilient housing in Colombia and typhoon-resilient housing in the Philippines, often with locally sourced materials

Neil Thomas of Atelier One puts the engineering case simply: *"Everything you can do with timber, you can do with bamboo."*

### The specific limits

The manual is clear about where bamboo stops being the right answer:

- It is not appropriate above two storeys in general use. Taller structures like Ninghai are still exceptions
- Its full potential is held back by "knowledge gaps". Trujillo links these partly to colonial-era technical education pushing out local bamboo expertise, so much of the engineering knowledge has to be rebuilt from first principles

### Why it matters for the climate

Bamboo has three properties that no fossil-based construction material has. It grows in 3 to 6 years (compared with decades for timber), it **stores carbon during the life of the building** instead of releasing it, and it needs little pesticide or fertiliser. Trujillo argues: *"The idea that we can move people away from using carbon-intensive materials and towards low-carbon materials or, better still, carbon-fixing materials seems like a very wise way of minimising the emissions from urbanisation."*

Growing bamboo can also help recover soil degraded by monoculture (growing a single crop year after year), which lines up with the wider land-use case in the [nature-based solutions](.) category.

### A European supply chain is beginning

Bamboo is now grown in Europe as well as the tropics. Portugal is growing larger bamboo varieties as a crop, partly in response to changes in the Mediterranean climate. The French firm Horizom manages 460 hectares across 34 departments, with a target of 50,000 hectares by 2050. Fiboo opened a bamboo fibre insulation panel factory in northern France in November 2024, with a target of 10,000 tonnes/year from 2028, the year low-carbon construction materials become mandatory. If this grows, it strengthens the case that the EU can supply more of its own materials, similar to the one made for [bio-based resins from the University of Oulu](../../sectors/industry/bio-based-resins-composites.md).

The Institution's manual comes with a push in education: Trujillo wants lecturers to teach bamboo alongside steel and timber, so that "the next generation of engineers and architects" is trained to be "**bamboo-ready**."
