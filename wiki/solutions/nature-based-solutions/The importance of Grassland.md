# Summary
This page looks at why grasslands matter, their role in Ireland, and how companies can help protect them.

Grasslands are often overshadowed by forests, yet they cover a huge part of the Earth's surface. These varied landscapes, which include savannas and open shrublands, do a great deal for nature and for people. Even so, they are often overlooked and get far less protection than other ecosystems.

Across the world, grasslands store **up to 34% of the world's terrestrial carbon**, the carbon held on land. Ninety percent of it is stored underground, in tough roots and soils. This underground carbon is more stable than forest carbon and less exposed to fire. Grasslands are also rich in wildlife. They make up nearly a third of Key Biodiversity Areas worldwide, and some temperate grasslands support nearly 90 plant species per square meter, which puts them among the most species-rich places on Earth.

Grasslands also support people. An estimated **1 billion people worldwide depend on grasslands** for food and income, and grasslands contribute approximately US$20.8 trillion in economic value annually. They support the pollinators that farming relies on, improve soil health, prevent erosion and regulate water, by refilling groundwater and softening the impact of floods.

In Ireland, grasslands matter enormously. They are the main land cover, at **between 56% and 67% of the total land area** depending on how it is measured, the highest share in Europe (where the average is 10%). Large areas of them, particularly on mineral soils, take a lot of carbon out of the air. Grasslands on drained peat soils are a different story: they release large amounts of CO2. Taken together, Irish grasslands are currently a net source of emissions. The Irish Climate Action Plan recognises the role of grasslands and aims to improve how they are managed and measured, but working out exactly how much carbon they take in and give off is still difficult.

Protecting grassland wildlife and the carbon grasslands store matters. Companies can play a real part in Ireland by:

- Funding and supporting nature-positive farming initiatives that create habitats like species-rich grasslands and hedgerows on farms.
- Developing and carrying out corporate biodiversity strategies that match national objectives.
- Partnering directly with farmers and conservation groups to support habitat restoration and management.
- Raising awareness of why biodiversity matters and how businesses can help.
- Adopting biodiversity-friendly practices on their own land, such as keeping hedgerows and species-rich areas.
- Supporting initiatives like the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan to help pollinator populations vital for grassland health.

If companies recognise how much grasslands matter, worldwide and in Ireland, and take concrete steps, they can help these ecosystems keep delivering for climate, nature and people.

# More details
## An Underrated Ecosystem

Grasslands are often overlooked and undervalued, even though they are vast and do a great deal for the environment and for people. The term covers a wide range of grassy ecosystems found across the globe, including savannas, open shrubland and tundra. They help limit climate change, support wildlife, provide essential services such as clean water and healthy soil, and sustain livelihoods. Yet they face serious threats and receive far less protection than other ecosystems. A growing body of research is now showing their importance and making the case for more investment and protection.

> **Ecological Importance Beyond Carbon**
> The ecological significance of grasslands extends far beyond their role in the carbon cycle.
> - Biodiversity: Semi-natural grasslands, particularly the Annex I listed habitats, are critical reservoirs of biodiversity. They support a rich variety of plant species, which in turn provide food and habitat for numerous invertebrates, including essential pollinators like bees and butterflies, as well as birds and mammals.1 The concept of "high nature value" (HNV) farming, often associated with low-intensity management of grasslands, is recognized as crucial for maintaining this biodiversity.2 The unique flora of the Burren's calcareous grasslands, for example, attracts international botanical interest.8
> - Water Regulation: Grasslands with healthy soil structures play a vital role in the hydrological cycle. They enhance water infiltration into the ground, contribute to groundwater recharge, and can help in storing water, thereby moderating runoff and potentially reducing the risk of downstream flooding.2 The vegetation and soil also act as natural filters, improving water quality by trapping sediments and nutrients. Wetland areas, which are often interspersed with or adjacent to grasslands, are particularly important for these water-related ecosystem services.2
> - Soil Health: Grasslands are fundamental to maintaining and improving soil health. The dense root systems of grasses help to bind soil particles, improving soil structure and reducing the risk of erosion. The continuous addition of organic matter from decaying plant material enhances soil fertility, water-holding capacity, and supports a diverse community of soil organisms.2 This soil biodiversity is essential for processes like nutrient cycling and organic matter decomposition.

They also support communities and livelihoods:
- Over 1 Billion People Rely on Grasslands for Their Food and Livelihoods: grasslands are a key source of income and food security, particularly for pastoralists (people who herd livestock), smallholder farmers and Indigenous communities. Livestock raised on grasslands provides important animal protein.
- Grasslands hold deep cultural, historical and spiritual importance for many communities, especially Indigenous Peoples, whose traditions are closely tied to these ecosystems. These communities often play a central role in protecting grasslands.


> **Key figures related to the benefits and status of grasslands**:
>  
> - Grasslands account for up to 34% of the world's terrestrial carbon4.
> - Ninety percent of grassland carbon is stored underground in roots and soils15.
> - Grassland, shrubland, and savanna account for nearly a third (6.8 million square kilometers) of Key Biodiversity Areas globally7.
> - Some temperate grasslands host nearly 90 species per square meter, making them among the most species-rich ecosystems7.
> - It's estimated that as much as 35% of the world's food crops depend on animal pollinators, which are supported by grassland habitats9.
> - Over the last 150 years, nearly half of the world's topsoil has been lost, partly due to the conversion and poor management of grasslands10.
> - Grasslands contribute approximately US$20.8 trillion in economic value each year11.
> - An estimated 1 billion people, many living under the poverty line, depend on grazing livestock from grasslands for subsistence and income12.
> - In underdeveloped regions like sub-Saharan Africa, grassland- and savanna-based pastoralism and extensive livestock production contribute to 5%-25% of some countries' GDP12.
> - Only 4.6% of temperate grasslands are protected, compared to 18% of forests and around 16% of wetlands2.
> - Close to half of all grasslands may have already been converted to other uses, such as agriculture or development2.
> - Indeed, half the world's grasslands have already suffered some degree of degradation16.
> - Grassland conversion could cause up to 4.25 gigatons of emissions globally by 205016.
> - Native American Nations, together with ranching and farming families, own and oversee 85% of the remaining intact Northern Great Plains grassland3.

## What does that mean in the context of Ireland? 

We talk a lot about forest management, afforestation and wetland restoration, but much less about the role grasslands play, or could play, in capturing carbon.

> Ireland's landscape is uniquely characterised by the extensive presence of grasslands. These ecosystems are the most significant land cover in the nation, encompassing between 56-67% of the total land area. This figure is notably high when contrasted with the European Union average of approximately 10% grassland cover , immediately highlighting the profound national importance of these areas. This widespread prevalence of grasslands is deeply interwoven with Ireland's agricultural heritage and contemporary land use patterns, where they are primarily utilized for livestock grazing and the production of fodder. The sheer scale of these grasslands means that their management and ecological functioning have substantial implications for the national carbon budget and overall environmental health


Most of this is permanent grassland, shaped by farming or other strong human disturbance. The mix of plants growing on it is influenced by human activity such as grazing and mowing.

> **The predominant category within this vast expanse is 'Pastures', which accounted for approximately 3.89 million hectares in 2018, or about 98.7% of the total grassland area. In contrast, 'Natural grassland' constituted a significantly smaller portion, covering around 48,567 hectares, or roughly 1.3% of total grasslands in the same year.**

'Pastures' are permanent grassland used for farming or heavily shaped by people, usually grazed or cut by machine. Because they make up almost all Irish grassland, nearly all of it is actively managed. That also means farming policy is the main tool Ireland has for changing the climate impact of its grasslands.


## How do grasslands fit into the Climate Action Plan?


> The Irish Government's Climate Action Plan (CAP), updated annually, forms the core of its strategy to meet national and EU climate obligations. The overarching goals are to achieve a 51% reduction in total economy-wide greenhouse gas emissions by 2030 (compared to a 2018 baseline) and to transition to a climate-resilient, biodiversity-rich, environmentally sustainable, and climate-neutral economy by no later than 2050.


The Climate Action Plan builds grassland management into Irelandʼs wider climate goals. It aims to help grassland soils store more carbon, cut emissions from peat soils, encourage tree planting and hedgerows, reduce fertiliser use, and support organic and biodiversity-friendly farming. Together, these measures aim to reduce net emissions from grasslands and LULUCF (land use, land-use change and forestry) by 2030, as part of Irelandʼs overall climate targets.

To reach the economy-wide targets, Ireland has set legally binding five-year carbon budgets and emissions ceilings for each sector. The agriculture sector has a specific emissions reduction target, which was stated as 25% by 2030 in some analyses, though the precise figure within the latest CAP should be verified against the most current official documentation. The Land Use, Land-Use Change, and Forestry (LULUCF) sector has a distinct objective: to reduce net emissions by 0.626 Mt $CO_2$eq by the year 2030, relative to an average baseline from the years 2016, 2017, and 2018.15


> Management practices that enhance carbon sequestration in grasslands include grazing management that recycles organic matter, improved fertiliser and liming practices, longer intervals between reseeding, maintaining permanent grasslands, and increasing plant species diversity . These practices not only increase soil organic carbon but also improve soil quality and farm productivity.



# Conclusion
- The carbon grasslands take in is under-counted in national emissions inventories at the moment, but it matters for meeting climate targets.
- Ireland’s Climate Action Plan prioritises better grassland management, peatland restoration, afforestation and better monitoring, to get the most out of carbon sinks by 2030.
- The main actions include managing 450,000 hectares of grasslands better, reducing management intensity on organic soils, increasing forest planting, and establishing long-term monitoring networks.
- Corporations can support these efforts through sustainable sourcing, investment, research collaboration, circular economy initiatives, and policy advocacy.
- Restoring Grassland and Savanna is one of the nature-based solutions put forward by Project Drawdown.
