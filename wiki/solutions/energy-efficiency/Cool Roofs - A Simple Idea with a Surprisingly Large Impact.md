Most cities are darker than they need to be. Black asphalt roads, dark rooftops and dense building materials soak up sunlight during the day and give it back slowly as heat. That keeps towns and cities several degrees warmer than the countryside around them, often well into the night. This is the **urban heat island effect**. It pushes up energy demand, makes air quality worse and speeds up local warming.

Part of the answer is very simple: make roofs lighter and more reflective.

---

### The Scale of the Opportunity

A 2010 study by Berkeley Lab using NASA data estimated what would happen if cool roof techniques were applied to every rooftop and pavement in cities across the northern hemisphere. The one-time effect would be equal to removing **more than 44 billion tonnes of CO2**, which is more than the whole world emits in a year. The figure combines the direct cooling with the knock-on effect of buildings needing less energy for air conditioning.

That is a one-off benefit rather than an ongoing cut, but it shows how much heat city surfaces hold when you add them all up.

---

### How It Works

A cool roof relies on two properties. High solar reflectance means it reflects sunlight instead of absorbing it. High thermal emittance means it releases any heat it does absorb back into the air instead of passing it down into the building. A conventional dark roof does badly on both. A well-designed cool roof does well on both.

The benefits reach well beyond the building itself:

- **Cooler outdoor air.** Less heat passes from rooftops into the surrounding air, which helps bring city temperatures down and eases the heat island effect.
- **Lower energy demand.** Buildings with cool roofs need less air conditioning, which cuts electricity use and the emissions that come with it.
- **Better air quality.** Lower city temperatures slow the formation of ground-level ozone, a main ingredient of smog that irritates the lungs.
- **Grid stability.** Less demand for cooling during heatwaves lowers peak electricity use and reduces the risk of power cuts.
- **Improved indoor comfort.** In buildings without air conditioning, a cooler roof makes the inside more bearable. This matters a lot in hot climates and in lower-income housing.

---

### The Technology Is Advancing

Painting roofs white is not a new idea. What has changed is how well these coatings work and how long they last. Researchers at Purdue University developed what Guinness World Records certified as the whitest paint ever made, reflecting **more than 98% of sunlight**. Buildings coated with it can stay cool enough to need much less air conditioning, or none at all. The drawbacks are that it gets dirty over time, which makes it less effective, and making it still involves mining raw materials.

> A French company called Cool Roof France has taken a different approach. Funded partly through the Team For The Planet impact investment programme, it has developed a white roof coating made from sea shells. When applied to large flat industrial rooftops, it can cut roof surface temperatures by around 2 degrees Celsius. In France, where the electricity grid is already relatively low-carbon, the solution delivers an average saving of around 25 kg of CO2 per square metre over 20 years. The impact is considerably higher in countries with more carbon-intensive electricity grids: around 120 kg per square metre in Europe more broadly, 150 kg in the United States, 320 kg in China, and up to 460 kg in India.

The company's own figures are larger than that 2°C. Cool Roof France says the outside roof surface temperature can be halved during heatwaves, and that indoor temperatures can drop by 5 to 15°C, depending on the building (L'Info Durable, April 2025).

That difference matters. The same cool roof does far more for the climate in a country that still relies heavily on fossil fuels for electricity, because every kilowatt-hour of cooling saved there avoids much more CO2.

---

### Worth Putting in Context

Cool roofs are most useful in hot climates and densely built cities. In cooler, cloudier countries like Ireland, the direct energy saving is smaller, although the benefits for air quality and comfort in towns still count during summers that are getting warmer.

Cool roofs also do not replace the need to clean up energy systems or insulate buildings better. They are one layer in a much bigger set of solutions for buildings, which remain one of the largest users of energy and sources of emissions in the world. Still, cool roofs are low-tech, fairly cheap, and can be added to existing buildings without major works. In a sector where many solutions take years and a lot of money, that mix of simplicity and impact deserves to be taken seriously.
