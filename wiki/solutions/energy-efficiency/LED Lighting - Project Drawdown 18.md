# LED Lighting: Project Drawdown 18

## What is it

**LEDs (light-emitting diodes)** are the most energy-efficient bulbs you can buy today. Older bulbs waste much of their energy as heat, while LEDs turn most of it into light. They use around 90% less energy than traditional incandescent bulbs and last far longer. Homes and businesses that switch to LEDs pay less for electricity and cut emissions. In low-income countries, LEDs combined with off-grid solar can replace polluting kerosene lamps altogether, which is good for both the climate and people's health.

Project Drawdown expects LEDs to be everywhere by 2050, making up 90-95% of household lighting and 80-90% of commercial lighting. As LEDs replace less efficient lighting on that scale, an estimated **14.45 to 15.69 gigatons** of carbon dioxide equivalent emissions could be avoided across homes and commercial buildings. That does not even include the extra gains for climate and health from replacing off-grid kerosene lamps with solar LEDs.

## Why it matters

Lighting is one of the easiest efficiency upgrades there is. Changing a bulb needs no building work, yet it can cut a good share of a home's or business's electricity use. Because everyone needs light, switching the world's bulbs to LEDs adds up to a meaningful amount of avoided emissions.

## The Scale of the Opportunity

Project Drawdown estimated total lighting demand separately for homes and for commercial buildings, using its Integrated Buildings model of the total addressable market along with sources such as the International Energy Agency. In 2018, that demand was around 37 petalumen-hours a year for household lighting and 62 petalumen-hours a year for commercial lighting. A petalumen-hour is a measure of the total amount of light delivered. LEDs supplied only a small share of it: an estimated 3% of the commercial lighting market and 2% of the household market in Drawdown's 2014 base year.

## The EU's Shift Away From Halogen and Incandescent Bulbs

The European Union banned incandescent light bulbs in 2009, which at first made halogen bulbs more popular as the cheaper alternative. The EU then extended the phase-out to halogen bulbs in 2018, having first announced plans to consult on it back in 2009. The decision followed studies that kept finding LEDs use around one-fifth of the energy of an equivalent halogen bulb.

The EU estimated the halogen phase-out alone would prevent **more than 15 million tonnes** of CO2 emissions by 2025. The European Commission's own 2018 figures put the yearly saving at 9.4 TWh of electricity, about 3.4 million tonnes of CO2 a year, and compared the energy saved to Portugal's electricity use. Buildings account for around 40% of Europe's energy use, and lighting makes up about 15% of that, so switching lighting technology can save a lot. Commercial and industrial LED fittings typically deliver 100-140 lumens per watt, making them around 75% more energy-efficient than halogen equivalents. LEDs cost more upfront than halogen bulbs, but the EU expects their long life, low maintenance and energy savings to pay back that cost within around 12 months.

Compact fluorescent lamps (CFLs) are a middle option. They use around 60-80% less energy than incandescent bulbs and last around 10 years, five times longer than halogen bulbs. CFLs are typically about three times cheaper than LEDs, with prices starting at around £2 a bulb, which makes them a reasonable compromise on cost. On the downside, they take longer to reach full brightness after you switch them on, and some models do not work with dimmer switches or outdoors.

## Understanding the New EU Energy Label

From 1 September 2021, EU energy labels showing a bulb's efficiency began moving to a new system, and the changeover was complete by 1 March 2023. The change came from the EU's Single Lighting Regulation (2019/2020) and Energy Label Regulation (2019/2015), which aim to protect the environment further, make products more sustainable and strengthen consumer protection. These rules were carried into UK law through the Ecodesign for Energy-Related Products and Energy Information (Lighting Products) Regulations 2021 (No. 1095).

The new labels do not mean bulbs got worse. Recent LEDs had become so efficient that most were rated in the top categories (A, A+, A++). Rather than keep adding pluses to the A class, regulators reset the scale to a simple A to G rating.

## Practical Tips for Lighting Your Home

Lighting can make up around 12% of a household's energy bill. LEDs can save 60-90% of the energy used by older bulbs, because they turn around 20% of the energy they use into light, compared with just 5% for incandescent bulbs. They typically last 4 to 8 years. According to France's environment and energy agency ADEME, improvements in LED technology over the past decade have delivered a 75% energy saving compared with earlier lighting.

A few practical points when choosing bulbs:

- **Colour temperature** affects how much blue light a bulb gives off. Warm white is around 2700-3500K, neutral white around 4000-5500K, and cold white around 6000-8000K, which suits offices better than living spaces.
- **Wattage equivalence** helps you compare brightness: a 5W LED is roughly equal to a 50W halogen bulb, and a 20W LED to a 120W halogen bulb.
- **IP ratings** show how well a fitting resists dust and moisture, and they matter for safety. IP20 is usually fine for a normal room, while bathrooms need at least IP44.

## Key facts

- LEDs use around 90% less energy than incandescent bulbs and last much longer.
- Project Drawdown projects LEDs could avoid 14.45 to 15.69 gigatons of CO2 equivalent emissions by 2050 as they reach 90-95% of household lighting and 80-90% of commercial lighting.
- In Project Drawdown's 2014 base year, LEDs supplied only around 2% of household and 3% of commercial lighting demand, so most of the opportunity was still ahead.
- The EU's 2018 halogen ban is estimated to prevent over 15 million tonnes of CO2 by 2025, around 3.4 million tonnes a year according to the European Commission.
- Commercial LED fittings reach 100-140 lumens per watt, about 75% more efficient than halogen equivalents.
- Lighting makes up around 12% of a typical household energy bill, and switching to LEDs can cut that by 60-90%.
