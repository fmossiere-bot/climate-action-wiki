# Where Does Oil Actually Go? A Breakdown of Global Demand

## What is it

Global oil demand is often talked about as a single number, but it is really the total of quite different uses. By far the largest share goes to **road transport**: cars, vans, lorries and motorbikes running on petrol and diesel. That makes up roughly half of global oil demand.

A large further share goes to petrochemicals, the raw material used to make plastics, synthetic fibres and countless other everyday materials. (Fertilisers are mostly made from natural gas, and from coal in China, rather than oil, according to the IEA.) This oil is not burned for energy at all, so switching transport to electricity does nothing to remove it. The rest is spread across aviation, shipping, heating and other industrial uses.

## Why it matters

Knowing where oil actually goes matters for the energy transition, because each use needs a different solution. Electrifying cars deals with the largest single slice of demand, road transport. It does little for the petrochemicals used in plastics, or for aviation and shipping, which are much harder to electrify with today's technology. That is one reason serious forecasts of falling oil demand still expect some "residual" demand for oil, even in a fast transition. It would be concentrated in aviation, shipping and petrochemicals rather than cars.

## Key facts

- Road transport is the single largest use of oil worldwide, accounting for roughly half of total demand.
- Petrochemicals, used to make plastics, synthetic fibres and other materials, are a large and growing share of oil demand that electrifying transport does not touch.
- Aviation and shipping depend heavily on fuels made from oil and are among the hardest sectors to decarbonise with today's technology.
- Because oil has such different uses, the energy transition needs solutions for each sector rather than a single fix.
