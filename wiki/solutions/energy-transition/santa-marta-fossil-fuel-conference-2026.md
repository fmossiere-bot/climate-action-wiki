# Santa Marta Conference: Building a Coalition to Phase Out Fossil Fuels (2026)

## What is it

In April 2026, nearly 60 countries met in Santa Marta, Colombia, for a two-day climate conference with one goal: ending the world's dependence on coal, oil and gas. Colombia and the Netherlands co-hosted it. It grew out of frustration with the annual UN climate summits, where fossil fuel-producing nations had blocked any clear commitment to phaseout year after year.

The main outcome was an agreement that each country would draw up a national **roadmap**, a plan setting out how it intends to wind down both the production and the use of fossil fuels. The roadmaps are voluntary. There are no fixed deadlines, no required format and no penalties for moving slowly. Even so, the political signal matters: this is the first time a sizeable group of countries has organised openly around ending fossil fuels, rather than around cutting emissions in general.

The conference followed a breakthrough at COP30 (Belém, November 2025). After petrostates kept fossil fuel language out of the formal agreement, the Brazilian presidency announced that the roadmap initiative would carry on outside the UN process, merging with a plan backed by Colombia and around 90 countries.

## Why it matters

For decades, international climate talks have run into a simple problem. The countries that produce and sell oil, gas and coal earn money from them, so they have every reason to slow down any deal to stop using them. Inside the UN system, decisions need consensus, which means a handful of petrostates can block progress for everyone. Santa Marta gets round this by creating a separate forum for countries that are willing to commit to phaseout.

The roughly 60 participating countries together represent more than half of global GDP and nearly a third of world energy demand. For them, the conference is a way to coordinate, share expertise and put joint pressure on the rest of the world ahead of COP31 (November 2026). For small island nations like Tuvalu, the stakes are existential. Rising seas caused by fossil fuel emissions, to which Tuvalu has barely contributed, threaten to submerge it, so a dedicated coalition is a matter of survival.

## Key facts

- **~60 countries** attended the Santa Marta conference (April 28–30, 2026)
- The coalition represents more than 50% of global GDP, ~1/3 of world energy demand and ~1/5 of fossil fuel supply ([source summary](../../sources/colombia-santa-marta-fossil-fuel-talks-2026.md))
- Almost half of the participating countries are fossil fuel producers themselves, so they are agreeing to plan the wind-down of their own industry ([source summary](../../sources/colombia-santa-marta-fossil-fuel-talks-2026.md))
- Major absentees: US, China, India, Russia, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, UAE
- France was the first developed country to publish a national fossil fuel phaseout roadmap ([source summary](../../sources/colombia-santa-marta-fossil-fuel-talks-2026.md))
- Colombia published a draft roadmap during the conference, and a new scientific panel was set up to advise all countries
- A second conference will be held in Tuvalu in early 2027, co-hosted by Ireland ([source summary](../../sources/colombia-santa-marta-fossil-fuel-talks-2026.md))
- Roadmaps are voluntary, with no fixed deadlines and no enforced format, but countries are encouraged to publish them before the Tuvalu conference
- NDCs (countries' existing Paris Agreement pledges) cannot stand in for roadmaps, because they only cover a country's domestic emissions and leave out the climate impact of the fossil fuels it exports ([source summary](../../sources/colombia-santa-marta-fossil-fuel-talks-2026.md))
- Participants agreed to look closely at fossil fuel subsidies and to work together on trade policy and climate finance

## June 2026 update: TAFF at the Bonn intersessional

By the June 2026 UN climate intersessional in Bonn, the Santa Marta initiative had a working name, the **Transition Away From Fossil Fuels (TAFF)** coalition, and was negotiating its future structures behind closed doors. DW's Tim Schauenberg reported that the petrostates which had opposed phaseout language at [COP30 in Belém](../../legislation-policy/COP30.md), Saudi Arabia, Iran, China, Russia, Tanzania and Senegal in particular, were "on edge" about a plurilateral coalition growing outside the UNFCCC process.

The Iran war and the energy price shock that came with it added momentum. UN climate chief Simon Stiell opened the Bonn talks by arguing that staying dependent on fossil fuels now meant "importing inflation and economic instability." IEA director Fatih Birol made the same point in economic terms: the crisis has shown how vulnerable energy systems are when they rely on fragile fossil supply chains. A New Climate Institute review found that the EU, Chile, Indonesia and Vietnam were using the crisis to speed up electrification, while Germany and several others fell back on tax relief for fossil fuels.

The strongest pushback on TAFF came from **African negotiators**. Lead negotiator Antwi Boasiako Amoah warned that a coalition of the willing risks bypassing the multilateral process: *"It is a multilateral process and we shouldn't engage outside the process and later come and then probably push those outcomes on others who are not part of the willing."* African positions on phaseout are complicated, because some countries export fossil fuels and others rely on them to finance energy access. Amoah also insisted that Africa cannot pay for adaptation by taking on more debt. The continent needs public grants and international finance rather than loans.

Outgoing COP30 President André Correa do Lago described the moment as a shift: *"We are moving from COPs focused on negotiations to COPs focused on implementation."* [COP31 will be held in Turkey](https://www.dw.com/en/turkey-to-host-cop31-after-australia-concedes/a-74814390) in November 2026, followed by the second TAFF conference in Tuvalu in early 2027 (co-hosted by Ireland).
