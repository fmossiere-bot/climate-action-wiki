# FACTS About the Fossil Fuel (Oil and Gas) Industry

## What it is

The fossil fuel industry means oil, gas and coal companies, together with the governments and banks that fund them. It sits at the centre of the climate crisis. It is the largest source of the emissions driving it, and one of the most organised forces resisting the move away from it. There is now a mountain of documented evidence that major fossil fuel companies **knew about the risks of climate change decades before the public did**. They chose to fund disinformation campaigns instead of acting on what their own scientists were telling them.

## Why it matters

To understand why the shift away from coal, oil and gas is still so much slower than the science demands, it helps to look at how the industry has behaved: on climate science, on its own transition pledges, and on the subsidies and financing that keep it profitable. Technology and cost are part of the story. So are lobbying, subsidies and decades of deliberate delay.

## Decades of Denial

Fossil fuel companies were not caught off guard by climate science. They were among the first to understand it. According to reporting by The Guardian, Exxon was researching the link between fossil fuels and climate change in the 1980s. It hid the evidence and spent millions on misinformation campaigns in the US to sow doubt about the very thing its own scientists were confirming internally. A 2021 investigation found that the French company TotalEnergies knew about the risks as far back as 1971.

Climate scientists are now about as sure that fossil fuels drive climate change as doctors are that tobacco causes lung cancer. The industry has handled that knowledge in much the same way the tobacco industry did, with a playbook of doubt and delay. The claim that scientists disagreed about the link is false: thousands of scientists have agreed on it for decades.

The pattern has not gone away. Climate scientist Michael Mann, in his 2021 book, describes how the industry's misinformation strategy has changed over four decades. It moved from outright denial, to deflection (shifting the blame onto individual consumers), to **doomsaying** (arguing that action is too costly or too late to matter). Recent examples include Santos Ltd, an oil company working in the Asia-Pacific region, which according to Australian climate journalist Ketan Joshi has used "net-zero" pledges to hide extensive greenwashing. Research published in the journal Global Public Policy also documents how oil majors have used economic consultants to delay climate policy ("Weaponizing Economics: Big Oil, Economic Consultants, and Climate Policy Delay").

## Broken 2022 Climate Pledges

In 2022, Oil Change International's "Big Oil Reality Check" report assessed the climate pledges of eight major US and European oil and gas companies (BP, Chevron, Eni, Equinor, ExxonMobil, Repsol, Shell and TotalEnergies) against the bare minimum needed to line up with the Paris Agreement. **None of them met it.** The report found that these eight companies alone were involved in 200 expansion projects planned for 2022 to 2025. Together these would emit as much over their lifetime as 77 new coal power plants, a potential 8.6 gigatonnes of CO2. The case for cutting oil and gas production this decade is clear, yet existing assets and planned investment show that most of the majors analysed are on track to produce more regardless. Before counting promised sales of Russian interests, Shell was the only company expected to produce less oil by 2030.

## Shareholders Are Starting to Push Back
<!-- tag: company-evaluations -->

Pressure is also coming from inside the industry. At BP's April 2026 AGM, its first under new chair Albert Manifold and new CEO Meg O'Neill, shareholders rebelled on three fronts. More than half of votes cast opposed BP's plan to scrap its existing climate reporting. More than half opposed replacing in-person AGMs with online-only meetings (in-person meetings had become "a lightning rod for climate protest"). And **18%** voted against re-electing Manifold himself, something unheard of less than a year into a chair's tenure. Legal & General Investment Management, the UK's largest asset manager, voted against both Manifold and the reporting rollback, and the proxy advisers Glass Lewis and ISS urged shareholders to do the same. See [BP board suffers triple climate rebellion from shareholders](../../climate-finance/bp-shareholder-rebellion-2026.md) for the full story.

## Subsidies: Governments Still Bankroll Fossil Fuels

Fossil fuel subsidies are still enormous. In 2018, they came to more than $400 billion worldwide. For 2021, the IMF calculated that subsidies to the fossil fuel industry worked out at $11 million a minute, adding up to **$5.9 trillion** for the year, or almost 7% of global GDP, as reported by The Guardian.

Speaking at the World Bank's annual meeting in Morocco in 2023, World Bank president Ajay Banga said the $1.25 trillion spent every year making fuel, fisheries and farming artificially cheap was too much. He noted that these three sectors together cause up to $6 trillion in environmental damage. The fossil fuel industry and governments remain close: the sector spent an estimated $135 million on lobbying in a recent year, according to a Terra.do lab talk by Dr Zach Brown. The same talk cited an estimate that around 80% of remaining fossil fuel reserves, worth some $20 trillion, must stay in the ground to meet climate goals. There is some good news: at least 53 countries reformed their fossil fuel subsidies between 2015 and 2020, according to the Global Subsidies Initiative.

## Private Finance Still Flowing In

Government subsidy is only part of the picture. In the five years after the Paris Agreement (2016 to 2020), the world's 60 biggest banks put **$3.8 trillion** into fossil fuels, and the 2026 Banking on Climate Chaos report puts the total since 2015 at $8.7 trillion from the 65 largest banks. Research group InfluenceMap reports that the 30 largest listed financial institutions in the world together provided more than $740 billion in primary financing to the fossil fuel industry in 2020 and 2021 alone. All 30 belong to industry associations that lobby against climate policy, and 29 of the 30 have also committed to net zero. The campaign group Rainforest Action Network tracks which banks have provided the most fossil fuel financing and which have promised to phase it out.

The pattern reaches beyond Europe and the US. The Guardian has reported that US funding for fossil fuel energy projects in Africa rose after the Paris Agreement, despite the continent's climate goals. As Guardian columnist George Monbiot has put it, "the more destructive the business, the more likely it is to enjoy political protection… we can protect the living world or we can protect the companies destroying it. We cannot do both."

## The Transition Risk Building Beneath the Industry

A 2020 Carbon Tracker report, "Decline and Fall: The Size and Vulnerability of the Fossil Fuel System," shows how much value is tied up in fossil fuels, and how exposed that value is. Worldwide, the three main kinds of fossil fuel asset are:

- 900 billion tonnes of coal, oil and gas in the ground, valued by the World Bank at **$39 trillion**
- supply infrastructure worth $10 trillion, and demand infrastructure (electricity, transport and heavy industry) worth $22 trillion
- financial markets holding $18 trillion of shares, $8 trillion of traded bonds, and up to four times as much again in unlisted debt

Each of these assets produces an ongoing stream of money: $1-3 trillion a year in economic rent to petrostates, $1 trillion a year spent on supply infrastructure plus $3-4 trillion on demand infrastructure, and $1-2 trillion a year in profits.

Carbon Tracker's main finding is that these streams of rent, investment and profit are damaged first, before the physical assets themselves. Stock markets tend to see those losses coming long before they appear as formal write-downs. The report identifies **$6 trillion of shares** in sectors still expanding the fossil fuel system, from oil exploration to gas turbines to diesel cars, all exposed to the effects of falling demand once it peaks.

Separate research suggests that in a net-zero transition, half the world's fossil fuel assets could be worthless by 2036, with early movers into renewables profiting while slow movers suffer. Fitch Ratings has warned that stranded assets could lower whole countries' credit ratings. Loan markets have already started penalising coal, and analysts describe gas as "the new stranded asset," with around $100 billion of investment at risk. A separate Finance Watch report found dozens of banks together exposed to some $1.35 trillion in fossil fuel assets that could lose most of their value in the move to net zero.

## Summary findings

- Exxon was researching the link between fossil fuels and climate change internally in the 1980s, and TotalEnergies knew as far back as 1971 (The Guardian; BBC).
- None of BP, Chevron, Eni, Equinor, ExxonMobil, Repsol, Shell or TotalEnergies meet the minimum bar for Paris alignment. These 8 majors alone have 200 expansion projects planned for 2022-2025, equal to 77 new coal plants or 8.6 gigatonnes of CO2 (Oil Change International, Big Oil Reality Check 2022).
- Global fossil fuel subsidies reached $5.9 trillion in 2021, about 7% of global GDP, or $11 million a minute (IMF, via The Guardian).
- The world's 60 biggest banks put $3.8 trillion into fossil fuels from 2016 to 2020, and $8.7 trillion from 2015 to 2025 (65 largest banks, Banking on Climate Chaos 2026). The 30 largest financial institutions provided over $740 billion in 2020-2021 alone, and 29 of them have also pledged net zero (InfluenceMap).
- Fossil fuel reserves are valued at $39 trillion (World Bank), with a further $32 trillion in supply and demand infrastructure and up to $18 trillion in listed shares exposed to transition risk (Carbon Tracker, 2020).
- Research suggests half the world's fossil fuel assets could be worthless by 2036 in a net-zero transition, and $1.35 trillion in bank-held fossil assets has been flagged as exposed (Finance Watch).
- At least 53 countries reformed fossil fuel subsidies between 2015 and 2020 (Global Subsidies Initiative). Campaigns such as the Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty are pushing for a full phase-out. The Paris Agreement never named fossil fuels, and the COP28 outcome in 2023 only called for "transitioning away" from them (UNFCCC).
