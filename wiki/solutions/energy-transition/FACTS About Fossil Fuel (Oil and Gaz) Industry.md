# FACTS About the Fossil Fuel (Oil and Gas) Industry

## What it is

The fossil fuel industry, oil, gas and coal companies together with the governments and banks that finance them, sits at the centre of the climate crisis, both as the largest source of the emissions driving it and as one of the most organised forces resisting the transition away from it. **There is now a mountain of documented evidence that major fossil fuel companies knew about the risks of climate change decades before the public did, and chose to fund disinformation campaigns rather than act on what their own scientists were telling them.**

## Why it matters

Understanding how the fossil fuel industry has behaved, on climate science, on its own transition pledges, and on the subsidies and financing that keep it profitable, is essential to understanding why the shift away from coal, oil and gas remains so much slower than the science demands. This is not simply a story about technology or cost. It is also a story about lobbying, subsidy, and decades of deliberate delay.

## Decades of Denial

Fossil fuel companies were not blindsided by climate science, they were among the first to understand it. Exxon was actively researching the link between fossil fuels and climate change back in the 1980s, according to reporting by The Guardian, and chose to hide the evidence while spending millions funding misinformation campaigns in the US designed to sow doubt about the very thing its own scientists were confirming internally. A 2021 investigation found that the French company TotalEnergies was aware of the risks as far back as 1971. Climate scientists are now about as confident that fossil fuels drive climate change as doctors are that tobacco causes lung cancer, and the industry's approach to managing that knowledge closely mirrors the tobacco industry's own playbook of doubt and delay, despite the claim that scientists were not aligned on the link being a straightforward falsehood: thousands of scientists have agreed on the connection for decades.

This pattern has not gone away. Climate scientist Michael Mann, in his 2021 book, describes how the industry's misinformation strategy has evolved over four decades from outright **denial**, to **deflection** (shifting responsibility onto individual consumers), to **doomsaying** (arguing that action is too costly or too late to matter). Recent examples include Santos Ltd, an oil company operating in the Asia-Pacific region, using "net-zero" pledges to obscure extensive greenwashing, according to Australian climate journalist Ketan Joshi, and research published in the journal Global Public Policy documenting how oil majors have used economic consultants to delay climate policy ("Weaponizing Economics: Big Oil, Economic Consultants, and Climate Policy Delay").

## Broken 2022 Climate Pledges

A 2022 "Big Oil Reality Check" report from Oil Change International assessed the climate pledges of seven major US and EU oil and gas companies, BP, Chevron, Eni, ExxonMobil, Repsol, Shell and TotalEnergies, against the bare minimum required for alignment with the Paris Agreement. None of them met it. The report found that eight oil and gas companies alone were involved in 200 expansion projects planned for 2022 to 2025, equivalent to the lifetime emissions of 77 new coal power plants, or a potential 8.6 gigatonnes of CO2. Although the case for reducing oil and gas production this decade is clear, existing assets and planned investment show that most of the majors analysed are on track to increase production regardless. Before accounting for pledged divestments from Russian interests, Shell was the only company projected to produce less oil by 2030.

## Shareholders Are Starting to Push Back

Pressure isn't only coming from outside the industry. At BP's April 2026 AGM, its first under new chair Albert Manifold and new CEO Meg O'Neill, shareholders delivered a triple rebellion: more than half of votes cast opposed BP's plan to scrap its existing climate reporting, more than half opposed replacing in-person AGMs with online-only meetings (in-person meetings had become "a lightning rod for climate protest"), and 18% opposed re-electing Manifold himself, unprecedented less than a year into his tenure. Legal & General Investment Management, the UK's largest asset manager, voted against both Manifold and the reporting rollback, and proxy advisers Glass Lewis and ISS urged shareholders to do the same. See [BP board suffers triple climate rebellion from shareholders](../../climate-finance/bp-shareholder-rebellion-2026.md) for the full story.

## Subsidies: Governments Still Bankroll Fossil Fuels

Fossil fuel subsidies remain enormous. In 2018, they totalled more than $400 billion globally. By 2021, the IMF calculated that subsidies to the fossil fuel industry equalled $11 million per minute, adding up to $5.9 trillion for the year, or almost 7% of global GDP, as reported by The Guardian. Speaking at the World Bank's annual meeting in Morocco in 2023, World Bank president Ajay Banga said the $1.25 trillion spent every year making fuel, fisheries and agriculture artificially cheap was too much, noting that these three sectors are together responsible for up to $6 trillion in environmental impact. Close ties between the fossil fuel industry and government persist: the sector spent an estimated $135 million on lobbying in a recent year, according to a Terra.do lab talk by Dr Zach Brown, which also cited an estimate that around 80% of remaining fossil fuel reserves, some $20 trillion worth, must stay in the ground to meet climate goals. Not all the news is negative: at least 53 countries reformed their fossil fuel subsidies between 2015 and 2020, according to the Global Subsidies Initiative.

## Private Finance Still Flowing In

Government subsidy is only part of the picture. In the years since the Paris Agreement, the world's 60 biggest banks have financed fossil fuels to the tune of $3.8 trillion. Research group InfluenceMap reports that the 30 largest publicly listed financial institutions in the world collectively enabled more than $740 billion in primary financing to the fossil fuel industry in 2020 and 2021 alone; all 30 are members of industry associations that lobby against climate policy, and 29 of the 30 have simultaneously committed to net-zero. Advocacy group Rainforest Action Network tracks which banks globally have provided the most fossil fuel financing and which have committed to phasing it out. The pattern extends internationally: The Guardian has reported that US funding for fossil fuel energy projects in Africa increased after the Paris Agreement, despite the continent's climate goals. As Guardian columnist George Monbiot has put it, "the more destructive the business, the more likely it is to enjoy political protection… we can protect the living world or we can protect the companies destroying it. We cannot do both."

## The Transition Risk Building Beneath the Industry

A 2020 Carbon Tracker report, "Decline and Fall: The Size and Vulnerability of the Fossil Fuel System," lays out just how much value is tied up in fossil fuels, and how exposed that value is. Globally, the three main fossil fuel assets are: 900 billion tonnes of coal, oil and gas, valued by the World Bank at $39 trillion; supply infrastructure worth $10 trillion and demand infrastructure (electricity, transport and heavy industry) worth $22 trillion; and financial markets holding $18 trillion of equity, $8 trillion of traded bonds, and up to four times as much again in unlisted debt. Each of these assets generates an ongoing "flow": $1-3 trillion a year in economic rent to petrostates, $1 trillion a year in capital expenditure on supply infrastructure plus $3-4 trillion on demand infrastructure, and $1-2 trillion a year in profits.

Carbon Tracker's key finding is that these flows, the rents, capital spending and profits, get damaged first, before the underlying physical assets do, and equity markets tend to anticipate those losses long before they show up as formal write-downs. The report identifies $6 trillion of equity in sectors still expanding the fossil fuel system, from oil exploration to gas turbines to diesel cars, all of which is exposed to the consequences of peaking demand. Separate research suggests that in a net-zero transition, half the world's fossil fuel assets could be worthless by 2036, with early movers to renewables profiting while slow decarbonisers suffer. Fitch Ratings has warned that stranded assets could cut entire countries' credit ratings, loan markets have already started penalising coal, and analysts describe gas as "the new stranded asset," with around $100 billion of investment at risk. A separate Finance Watch report found dozens of banks collectively exposed to some $1.35 trillion in fossil fuel assets that could lose most of their value in the transition to net zero.

## Summary findings

- Exxon was researching the fossil fuel-climate change link internally in the 1980s; TotalEnergies knew as far back as 1971 (The Guardian; BBC).
- None of BP, Chevron, Eni, ExxonMobil, Repsol, Shell or TotalEnergies meet the minimum bar for Paris alignment; 8 majors alone have 200 expansion projects planned for 2022-2025, equal to 77 new coal plants or 8.6 gigatonnes of CO2 (Oil Change International, Big Oil Reality Check 2022).
- Global fossil fuel subsidies reached $5.9 trillion in 2021, about 7% of global GDP, or $11 million a minute (IMF, via The Guardian).
- The world's 60 biggest banks have financed fossil fuels to the tune of $3.8 trillion since the Paris Agreement; the 30 largest financial institutions enabled over $740 billion in 2020-2021 alone, 29 of which have also pledged net-zero (InfluenceMap).
- Fossil fuel assets are valued at $39 trillion (World Bank), with a further $32 trillion in supply and demand infrastructure and up to $18 trillion in listed equity exposed to transition risk (Carbon Tracker, 2020).
- Research suggests half the world's fossil fuel assets could be worthless by 2036 in a net-zero transition; $1.35 trillion in bank-held fossil assets has been flagged as exposed (Finance Watch).
- At least 53 countries reformed fossil fuel subsidies between 2015 and 2020 (Global Subsidies Initiative), and campaigns such as the Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty are pushing for the phase-out the Paris Agreement and COP27 outcome text never explicitly named.
