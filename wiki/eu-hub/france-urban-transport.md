# France: how cities are changing urban transport

## What is it

French cities are moving beyond the classic model of building more metro lines and tram extensions. **Three newer approaches are now scaling across the country**: urban cable cars (10 lines operating or planned), fully free public transport (Dunkerque, Montpellier and ~40 smaller towns), and app-driven on-demand transport (over 200 urban areas and 60% of rural council groups). The reframing is deliberate — the goal is no longer "move more passengers" but "offer transport that fits local needs and uses less energy".

## Why it matters

Transport is **France's biggest single source of greenhouse gas emissions (34% of the national total)**, and more than half of that comes from private cars. France's urban-transport experiments matter because they test, in real cities, the policies that other European countries will have to grapple with: what works to get people out of cars, what doesn't, and what the honest trade-offs are between social, economic and climate objectives.

## Urban cable cars

### Why cable cars

Cable cars suit cities where terrain or hard infrastructure (motorways, high-speed rail lines, rivers) makes classic transit expensive or impossible. They are quiet, 100% electric with no local pollution, and relatively cheap to build because only the pylons and stations need civil works.

The main criticism is capacity: a cable car carries **10 to 50 times fewer passengers per day than a tram or metro**. The counter-argument is that passenger numbers so far have been respectable: Brest hit 1 million passengers in 2024; Toulouse's Téléo has moved more than 5 million since opening in 2022.

### Operating lines in France

| Year | City | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| 1934 | Grenoble | First French line; home of Poma, world leader in cable cars (founded by Jean Pomagalski) |
| 1976 | Grenoble "bubbles" | Climb to the Bastille fort; now mostly a tourist line |
| 2016 | Brest | First real urban cable car (<500 m, crosses the river Penfeld); 1 million passengers in 2024 |
| 2022 | Saint-Denis de La Réunion | — |
| 2022 | Toulouse (Téléo) | >5 million passengers since opening |
| 2025 | Ajaccio (Angelo, Corsica) | Links the coast to the higher parts of the city |
| 2025 | **Val-de-Marne, Paris region (Câble C1)** | **4.5 km, longest urban cable car in France**. Opened 13 December 2025. Needed because the area is cut off by motorways, high-speed rail lines and rail yards |

### What is next

Toulouse wants to extend Téléo. Saint-Denis de La Réunion has a second line planned. The mayor of Valenton wants the Câble C1 extended to Orly airport. A cable car linking Vitrolles train station to Marseille-Provence airport is targeted for 2030. A second Paris-region line, between Vélizy-Villacoublay and Boulogne-Billancourt, is being studied. A project in Issy-les-Moulineaux was killed by strong opposition.

## Free public transport

### The context

Fare revenue only covers part of what it costs to run a city's buses and trams. According to France's Cour des comptes:

- Paris region: tickets cover **40%** of running costs
- Other regions: **33%**
- Towns under 100,000 people: only **18%**

With local councils already paying most of the cost, some have taken it to its logical conclusion and gone fully free.

### Towns that went free

- **Compiègne (1975)** — first French town to make its buses free
- Followed by ~40 others: Morlaix, Libourne, Niort, Figeac, Gap, Bourges, Obernai...
- **Dunkerque (2018)** — first city of >100,000 to go free. Mayor Patrice Vergriete described it as "good for families' spending power, good for the environment and good for social ties". Costs less than 3% of the local budget. The real motivations were more economic (bringing life back to the centre) and social (helping poorer people get around) than environmental
- **Montpellier (from 2020)** — Mayor Michaël Delafosse rolled out full-free step by step. A family of two children saves **€1,258/year**. The scheme costs **~€40 million in 2026**

### What the audit office found

The Cour des comptes and the French Senate have both studied the Montpellier case. Their findings are blunt:

- **More passengers, overloaded network, but no positive effect on health or environment** (Cour des comptes)
- The scheme is **expensive** and risks pushing urban transport into a "financial dead end"
- **New bus and tram users are mostly former walkers and cyclists**, not former drivers — so the modal shift away from cars is small
- The French Senate: *"carefully targeted reduced fares can be just as useful as making it fully free"*; and on climate, free transport alone *"cannot start a revolution"*

**Honest summary:** free public transport is defensible as social policy — it helps lower-income households, supports local shops and strengthens social ties. But the climate case is weaker than its advocates often claim.

## On-demand transport

### The rural transport gap

**One French person in three lives in rural areas or on the edge of towns (70% of the country), but only 20% of these areas have regular bus lines**. For most residents, there is no choice but to drive. A broken-down car or a fuel-price spike becomes a serious problem.

### The first generation (1990-2005)

- **Saint-Brieuc (1990)** was the first town to offer on-demand taxis (Taxitub, now Proxitub) as part of its public transport
- 300+ on-demand transport networks were set up across France between 1990 and 2005, often with special attention to passengers with reduced mobility
- This first wave was hard to manage and stopped growing

### The app-driven revival

Booking software that plans routes in real time changed the economics. The Paris start-up **Padam Mobility** is the main example — you order a taxi, van or minibus from your phone, and the ride is mostly paid for by the local council.

- **200+ urban areas** now run flexible on-demand services (Jyvais in Meaux, Résago in Nancy, Tadi Lib' in Brittany between Rennes and Nantes, TBM Flex' in Bordeaux)
- **6 of 10 rural council groups** have adopted on-demand transport (Cerema)
- France's **Fonds vert** (green fund) helps pay for on-demand transport, car-pooling, car-sharing and electric-bike leasing in local projects

### Other related innovations

- Around ten car-sharing companies are approved by the Paris region (Clem', Communauto, ShareNow and others)
- Ecov in Nantes and Mobicoop in Nancy run car-pooling lines
- **Navya** and **EasyMile** have built self-driving shuttles with no driver, for short trips on fixed routes — potentially useful in and around French cities and in areas with poor transport, if costs stay affordable

## The emissions picture

### Transport is France's biggest GHG source

Of all greenhouse gas emissions in France:

- **34%** come from transport
- Of which **54%** comes from private cars
- And **24%** from lorries

### How French people get around

- **62.8%** by car (electric cars included)
- **23.7%** walking
- **9.1%** train
- **2.7%** cycling (electric bikes included)
- **1.7%** scooters, motorbikes or taxis

### CO2 per passenger-kilometre

French averages, lifecycle, grams of CO2 equivalent per passenger per kilometre:

| Mode | g CO2e/pkm |
|---|---|
| Short-haul plane (<1,000 km) | 225 |
| Long-haul plane (>3,000 km) | 178 |
| Petrol or diesel car | 142 |
| Bus | 122 |
| Electric car | 67 |
| Regional electric train (TER) | 27.5 |
| Electric bike | 11 |
| High-speed train (TGV) | 2.9 |
| Normal bike | 0 |
| Walking | 0 |

The numbers change with vehicle model, how full the vehicle is, and the distance. For planes, a trip with several take-offs has a bigger footprint.

<!-- tag: individual-actions -->
**A simple distance-matched rule (from the Eco360 piece):**

- **Short distances**: walk
- **In town (3-15 km)**: bike, electric bike or electric car
- **In the region (15-200 km)**: electric bike, electric car, bus or regional train (TER)
- **Long distances (>200 km)**: high-speed train (TGV) or electric car
- **Trips >1,000 km**: plane, only when the train is not a realistic option

The car does not have to be ruled out, as long as you avoid driving alone.
