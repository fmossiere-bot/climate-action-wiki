# France: how cities are changing urban transport

## What is it

French cities are looking beyond the classic answer of building more metro lines and extending trams. Three newer approaches are now spreading across the country. The first is urban cable cars, with **10 lines** operating or planned. The second is fully free public transport, in Dunkerque, Montpellier and about 40 smaller towns. The third is on-demand transport booked through an app, now in over 200 urban areas and 60% of rural council groups.

The shift in thinking is deliberate. The aim is no longer simply to move more passengers, but to offer transport that fits local needs and uses less energy.

## Why it matters

Transport is France's biggest single source of greenhouse gas emissions, at **34%** of the national total, and more than half of that comes from private cars. These experiments matter because they test, in real cities, the policies other European countries will also have to work through: what gets people out of cars, what does not, and what the real trade-offs are between social, economic and climate goals.

## Urban cable cars

### Why cable cars

Cable cars suit cities where hills or hard infrastructure (motorways, high-speed rail lines, rivers) make classic public transport expensive or impossible. They are quiet and 100% electric, with no local pollution. They are also fairly cheap to build, because only the pylons and stations need major construction work.

The main criticism is capacity. A cable car carries **10 to 50 times** fewer passengers a day than a tram or metro. Supporters point out that passenger numbers so far have been respectable: Brest reached 1 million passengers in 2024, and Toulouse's Téléo has carried more than 5 million since it opened in 2022.

### Operating lines in France

| Year | City | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| 1934 | Grenoble | First French line. Grenoble is home to Poma, the world leader in cable cars, founded by Jean Pomagalski |
| 1976 | Grenoble "bubbles" | Climbs to the Bastille fort. Now mostly a tourist line |
| 2016 | Brest | First real urban cable car (<500 m, crosses the river Penfeld). 1 million passengers in 2024 |
| 2022 | Saint-Denis de La Réunion | No further details |
| 2022 | Toulouse (Téléo) | >5 million passengers since opening |
| 2025 | Ajaccio (Angelo, Corsica) | Links the coast to the higher parts of the city |
| 2025 | **Val-de-Marne, Paris region (Câble C1)** | 4.5 km, the longest urban cable car in France. Opened 13 December 2025. Links Villeneuve-Saint-Georges, Limeil-Brévannes and Valenton to Créteil-Pointe-du-Lac station on metro line 8. Uses the standard Navigo pass. Built because the area is cut off by motorways, high-speed rail lines and rail yards |

### What is next

Toulouse wants to extend Téléo. Saint-Denis de La Réunion has a second line planned. The mayor of Valenton wants the Câble C1 extended to Orly airport. A cable car linking Vitrolles train station to Marseille-Provence airport is planned for 2030. A second Paris-region line, between Vélizy-Villacoublay and Boulogne-Billancourt, is being studied. A project in Issy-les-Moulineaux was dropped after strong local opposition.

## Free public transport

### The context

Fares cover only part of the cost of running a city's buses and trams. According to France's audit office, the Cour des comptes:

- Paris region: tickets cover **40%** of running costs
- Other regions: **33%**
- Towns under 100,000 people: only **18%**

Since local councils already pay most of the bill, some have gone one step further and made transport fully free.

### Towns that went free

- **Compiègne (1975)** was the first French town to make its buses free
- About 40 others followed, including Morlaix, Libourne, Niort, Figeac, Gap, Bourges and Obernai
- **Dunkerque (2018)** was the first city of more than 100,000 people to go free. Mayor Patrice Vergriete described it as "good for families' spending power, good for the environment and good for social ties". It costs less than 3% of the local budget. The main reasons were economic (bringing life back to the town centre) and social (helping poorer people get around) rather than environmental
- **Montpellier (from 2020)**: Mayor Michaël Delafosse brought in free transport step by step. A family with two children saves **€1,258 a year**. The scheme costs about €40 million in 2026

### What the audit office found

The Cour des comptes and the French Senate have both studied the Montpellier case. Their findings are blunt:

- More passengers and an overloaded network, but **no positive effect** on health or the environment (Cour des comptes)
- The scheme is expensive and risks leading urban transport into a "financial dead end"
- **New bus and tram users are mostly people who used to walk or cycle**, not former drivers, so the shift away from cars is small
- The French Senate found that *"carefully targeted reduced fares can be just as useful as making it fully free"*, and that on climate, free transport alone *"cannot start a revolution"*

**In short:** free public transport holds up as social policy. It helps lower-income households, supports local shops and strengthens social ties. But the climate case is weaker than its supporters often claim.

## On-demand transport

### The rural transport gap

**One French person in three** lives in rural areas or on the edge of towns, which make up 70% of the country, but only 20% of these areas have regular bus lines. Most people there have no choice but to drive. A broken-down car or a jump in fuel prices quickly becomes a serious problem.

### The first generation (1990-2005)

- **Saint-Brieuc (1990)** was the first town to offer on-demand taxis (Taxitub, now Proxitub) as part of its public transport
- More than 300 on-demand transport networks were set up across France between 1990 and 2005, often with special attention to passengers with reduced mobility
- This first wave was hard to run and stopped growing

### The app-driven revival

Booking software that plans routes in real time changed the economics. The main example is the Paris start-up **Padam Mobility**: you order a taxi, van or minibus on your phone, and the local council pays most of the fare.

- **200+ urban areas** now run flexible on-demand services (Jyvais in Meaux, Résago in Nancy, Tadi Lib' in Brittany between Rennes and Nantes, TBM Flex' in Bordeaux)
- **6 of 10** rural council groups have taken up on-demand transport (Cerema)
- France's Fonds vert (green fund) helps pay for on-demand transport, car-pooling, car-sharing and electric-bike leasing in local projects

### Other related innovations

- Around ten car-sharing companies are approved by the Paris region (Clem', Communauto, ShareNow and others)
- Ecov in Nantes and Mobicoop in Nancy run car-pooling lines
- **Navya** and EasyMile have built self-driving shuttles with no driver, for short trips on fixed routes. These could be useful in and around French cities and in areas with poor transport, if costs stay affordable

## The emissions picture

### Transport is France's biggest greenhouse gas source

Of all greenhouse gas emissions in France:

- **34%** come from transport
- Of that, **54%** comes from private cars
- And **24%** from lorries

### How French people get around

- **62.8%** by car (electric cars included)
- **23.7%** walking
- **9.1%** train
- **2.7%** cycling (electric bikes included)
- **1.7%** scooters, motorbikes or taxis

### CO2 per passenger-kilometre

These are French averages over the full life cycle, in grams of CO2 equivalent per passenger per kilometre:

| Mode | g CO2e/pkm |
|---|---|
| Short-haul plane (<1,000 km) | 225 |
| Long-haul plane (>3,000 km) | 178 |
| Petrol or diesel car | 142 |
| Bus | 122 |
| Electric car | 67 |
| Regional electric train (TER) | 27.5 |
| Electric bike | 11 |
| High-speed train (TGV) | 2.9 |
| Normal bike | 0 |
| Walking | 0 |

The figures change with the vehicle model, how full it is and the distance travelled. For planes, a trip with several take-offs has a bigger footprint.

**A simple rule matched to distance (from the Eco360 piece):**

- **Short distances**: walk
- **In town (3-15 km)**: bike, electric bike or electric car
- **In the region (15-200 km)**: electric bike, electric car, bus or regional train (TER)
- **Long distances (>200 km)**: high-speed train (TGV) or electric car
- **Trips >1,000 km**: plane, only when the train is not a realistic option

You do not have to rule out the car, as long as you avoid driving alone.
