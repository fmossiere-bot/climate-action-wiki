# France: vineyards adapting fast to climate change

**Heatwaves, water stress, a calendar thrown off: nothing is going right in the vineyard any more. To adapt, the wine sector is trying out new practices with the plants, the grape varieties and the soils... and it is not afraid to move north!**

In the vineyards, climate change gives no break. It dries out the soils, weakens the vines, and even upsets the balance and the typical character of the wines. Faced with all these threats, the answers need a global view.

"We have to try out a whole set of solutions, because every part of the vine is under threat," says Jonas Dubois, a winegrower for twenty years in the Pyrénées-Orientales. "The vine is a plant and a soil. These are the key questions: how do we give back to the soil the organic matter it lost because of monoculture? How do we keep moisture around the roots? How do we protect the aromas and control the acidity and the alcohol of the wines?"

In the vineyards, the trials are growing in number: more resistant grape varieties, from elsewhere (1) or local ones, adapted rootstocks, agroforestry to bring back biodiversity and nutrients, agrivoltaics to protect the vines from the sun, mixing different plants in the same plot... But this change runs into the rules of the appellations. "We are working on all these areas, which is not easy in an AOC (protected designation of origin) where the rulebook holds us back. But the wine world is changing and the institutions will have to follow us," says Juliette Combe, head of R&D for winegrowing and agroecology at Château Cheval Blanc, in Saint-Émilion, in the Bordeaux area.

## The benefits of agroforestry

Trees and plant cover come from the same idea, "carbon farming": capturing carbon from the air and storing it in the soils and in plants, instead of letting it escape.

"Where trees bring shade and cool air, the vine copes better. We started our agroforestry programme six years ago on 50% of our plots, planting 2,000 trees of 56 species in and around the vines, to recreate a resilient ecosystem," Juliette Combe goes on. "The main problem is the heat. Under the biggest trees, it is already 3 degrees cooler. The leaves don't burn, the roots are fed with carbon, and the plants' transpiration protects them from water stress."

The man behind this approach is Alain Canet, a pioneer of agroforestry for thirty years. "The mix of trees, groves, hedges and borders recreates the ecosystem in which the vine has always grown. This biodiversity feeds and hydrates the vine, from the leaves to the roots. More than half of the carbon the vine needs comes from the biological breathing of the soil."

But no project can simply be copied. The choice of trees depends on the local plants, the soils, the terroirs and the grape varieties. "In the Languedoc, we use the Montpellier maple, which puts water back into the soil, and the pepper-leaf pear tree, which works deep down on the roots," Alain Canet continues. It is still a minority practice, but it is one of the most requested by winegrowers.

Bringing life back to the soil also means covering it with plants, which "limits erosion during heavy rain, lowers the soil temperature in a heatwave, helps water soak in, boosts microbe activity and captures carbon," explains Konrad Schreiber, a pioneer of this approach with his project La Belle Vigne, started in 2020 in the Beaujolais. He points out that "one tonne of straw digested by the soil releases 540 litres of water".

## Under the shelter of mobile solar panels

Against cold and heat, agrivoltaics has proved itself. Take the "shutters" from Sun'Agri, the French leader in solar solutions made for vineyards. Mobile solar panels, set 4 to 5 metres above the rows and controlled by AI, turn to shade the grapes when it is very hot, or, the other way round, let the light through during flowering.

The results: the vines cope better with heatwaves, they need 12 to 34% less watering, and the soil holds more water. The winegrower Jonas Dubois fitted panels on a third of his plots three years ago. "The difference is clear! This summer, it was 5 degrees cooler outside and 3 degrees cooler in the soil. The vine is less stressed and I lose 20 to 30% fewer plants under the panels, because the plant puts down deeper roots."

Fewer than ten companies in France master these systems today, but the solution is set to grow. The same goes for all the solutions offered by agritech: drones, moisture sensors, connected weather stations and satellite images follow the state of the vines in real time, make watering more efficient, and spot diseases and water stress before they hit.

## New, more resistant grape varieties

"French wine is not going to die, it is going to open up. Around twenty grape varieties cover 80% of the vineyard area, while the country has more than 200 authorised varieties!" says Olivier Bourdet-Pees, managing director of Plaimont. Plaimont is a group of cooperative wineries that brings together 600 families of winegrowers from the Gers and the Pyrenean foothills, making Saint Mont, Madiran, Pacherenc du Vic-Bilh and Jurançon AOC wines and IGP Côtes de Gascogne wines. "The first way to fight climate change is to bring these forgotten grape varieties back."

A pioneer since 2003 with its ampelographic conservatory (a collection for the study of grapevines), Plaimont is testing 39 local grape varieties to find the best adapted ones. "Manseng noir and tardif, brought back in 2017 and 2019, ripen late, with moderate alcohol, better acidity, a fresher and more complex taste, and they handle heat well."

Chosen because they resist disease and bad weather, these "new" grape varieties are called Vifa (varieties of interest for adaptation). They are now found "in every wine region", says Christian Paly, president of the AOC national committee at Inao (the French institute for origin and quality), which approves the rulebooks. "They can be local grape varieties brought back into use, varieties from other French regions or from French breeding, or foreign ones like touriga nacional and alvarinho from Portugal, nebbiolo from Italy, and souvignier gris from Germany."

Since 2019, the Bordeaux area has been testing ten Vifa varieties in red and white, including castets, touriga nacional, alvarinho and souvignier gris. The Rhône valley is testing xinomavro and marselan, the Loire valley floréal and souvignier gris, and even in Champagne, voltis is being tried. All these grape varieties are now part of the regional appellations.

Adapting also happens underground. The rootstock, the root system the grape variety is grafted onto, decides how strong the vine is, how well it resists drought, and how well it can draw nutrients from the soil. Choosing new rootstocks means the roots can be adapted to drier soils, without changing the grape variety.

## The call of the North

Since the European Union freed up planting rights in 2016, vines are coming back to the north of France, where they were grown in the Middle Ages. It's true that the Hauts-de-France, Brittany and Normandy make up less than 0.1% of French vineyards, but these regions are gaining from the warming. They mostly make white and sparkling wine, from grape varieties like chardonnay, chenin and the German solaris, which ripen early and suit northern climates, where the ripening season is short.

Even so, these regions are not spared. "We can't keep up with the speed of climate change and its extremes. This year, for the first time, it is a real problem. More than a month without water, three heatwaves, a harvest in mid-August: that has never been seen in Brittany," says Aurélien Berthou, an agronomist and former wine adviser in Bordeaux, who planted his vines in Saint-Goustan (Morbihan) in 2022. "Even our northern vineyards are not safe any more. Climate change also means late spring frosts, or a lot of rain that encourages mildew and oidium (two fungal diseases). That is why we turn to resistant grape varieties, a safety net against a climate that is too uncertain."

## Box: Vitilience and resilience

In 2024, the French Institute of Vine and Wine (IFV) launched the Vitilience programme. What makes it different? "Testing combinations of solutions adapted to the specific features of each wine region," explains Melissa Merdy, who leads the project. The trials cover the plant material, the grape varieties and rootstocks, how the vines are managed, watering, agroforestry, grazing and winemaking... The goal is to find the combinations that work best in each area: keeping the aromas in Champagne, managing water in Occitanie, adapting practices in the Loire valley... In Touraine, for example, the RedClim plot mixes chenin, côt (malbec), syrah and viognier, planted among about sixty apple trees, to measure what agroforestry does against drought.

## Box: Antoine Bouin, winegrower in the Hauts-de-France

With his vines in Mentque-Nortbécourt, 20 kilometres from Calais, this farmer and self-taught winegrower has been growing France's most northern vineyard since 2021, in the Pas-de-Calais. "People always say it's global warming that pushes us to make wine, but if we had been allowed, we would have planted much earlier!"

In the 15th century, just in the area around Amiens, the vineyards of Picardy covered 112 hectares and brought together around a hundred families of winegrowers. "The clay and limestone soils, with flint and chalk deeper down, are just right for the vine, always grown on slopes facing full south."

On his 4 hectares of chardonnay, pinot noir and pinot meunier (the same grape varieties as in Champagne), he makes more than 5,000 bottles of sparkling white. And he aims for 35,000 by 2028, "when the vines planted three years ago give their first grapes."

The Hauts-de-France vineyard now covers nearly 150 hectares across five departments and makes white wine. "We have started the process to apply for a PGI (protected geographical indication)," says Antoine Bouin, who is also president of the Vignerons des Hauts-de-France (the region's winegrowers' association). "All our winegrowers will sell their wine from 2027."
